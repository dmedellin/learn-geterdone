@chapter Two dinner guests
@title text="Intellectual Virtues and Madness" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 8" art=thumb
What makes one mind better than another? Hobbes starts at the dinner table.

@art dinner-story
You've met both people. One tells a story that leaps from dinner to a cousin to a ship to a recipe, every link bright, none on the road to the point. Another says little, and it's exactly what the moment needed.

@quote text="So that where Wit is wanting, it is not Fancy that is wanting, but Discretion. Judgement therefore without Fancy is Wit, but Fancy without Judgement not." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 8"
Hobbes says the second has wit. Where wit is wanting, it is not fancy that is wanting, but discretion. Judgement therefore without fancy is wit, but fancy without judgement not.

@art likeness-and-difference
Fancy notices likenesses. Judgement notices differences, of times, places and persons. Fancy is a virtue only when it's steadied toward some end. Without that, a great fancy is one kind of madness.

@chapter Where each belongs
@list heading="How they mix" items="Poem: both, fancy more eminent@In a poem|History: judgement; fancy only adorns@a history|Oration of praise: fancy@praise|Demonstration and counsel: judgement does all@counsel"
In a poem you need both, and fancy leads, because poems please by extravagance. In a history, judgement must be eminent, and fancy only adorns the style. In an oration of praise or invective, fancy rules. And in demonstration and counsel, judgement does all, and metaphors are utterly excluded.

@math heading="The wit preset" items="counsellor = judgement → has wit@counsellor;;poet = judgement + fancy → has wit@poet;;digresser = fancy → no wit@digresser"
So in the lab, the counsellor, all judgement, has wit. The poet, with both, has wit. And the digresser, all fancy and no direction, doesn't. That's a false claim, not an absurd one, since fancy and judgement don't clash.

@chapter What makes the difference
@art scouts
Where do the differences come from? Not the brain or the senses, or we'd differ as much in sight and hearing as in fancy. They come from the passions, chiefly the desire of power, riches, knowledge and honour, which all come down to power. The thoughts are to the desires as scouts and spies, ranging out to find the way.

@quote text="as to have no Desire, is to be Dead: so to have weak Passions, is Dulnesse;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 8"
Then the sentence the chapter's remembered for. As to have no desire is to be dead, so to have weak passions is dullness. Passions for everything are giddiness. And passions stronger and more vehement than usual are what men call madness.

@art passion-dial
So madness isn't a separate sickness. It's the same passions that make wit, turned up, in as many kinds as there are passions. In the lab, dullness is madness comes out absurd: weak and vehement passion can't be joined. Giddiness is madness is only false.

@chapter The private spirit
@art crowd-rage
The passions of madness are pride, or great vain glory, and great dejection. One man may show nothing odd alone, but when many conspire, the rage of the multitude is visible enough. And an excessive opinion of one's own inspiration, the private spirit, was no abstract worry in sixteen fifty one.

@words text="Daemoniacs: any other thing but mad-men?"
He turns to the Gospels carefully. He sees nothing in Scripture requiring belief that the demoniacs were anything but madmen. Christ speaks to the disease as all who cure by words do. That isn't a denial that Christ cured. It's a claim about what the text obliges you to believe.

@chapter The schoolmen's speech
@quote text="The first cause does not necessarily inflow any thing into the second, by force of the Essentiall subordination of the second causes, by Which it may help it to worke" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 8"
Last, the schoolmen, whose speech he counts as another madness. His sample is a chapter title of the Jesuit Suarez: the first cause does not necessarily inflow anything into the second, by force of the essential subordination of the second causes, by which it may help it to work.

@math heading="The suarez preset" items="a first cause is a cause: true@true;;a first cause is a second cause: absurd@absurd;;a first cause is an inflow: insignificant@insignificant"
In the lab, a first cause is a cause: true. A first cause is a second cause: absurd, since first and second are opposed. A first cause is an inflow: insignificant, since inflow has no definition. That split between clashing and empty is the lab's, not Hobbes's.

@art wafer-spirit
Then transubstantiation, as a reductio. The schoolmen say whiteness, which is incorporeal, moves from the wafer into the body. But a spirit is whatever is incorporeal and movable. So by their own definitions, whiteness is a spirit. They'd deny his definition of whiteness, and the lab can't settle that.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A lively fancy is wit@The first|Madness is a separate sickness@The second|Hobbes denies Christ healed@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a lively fancy is the same as wit. The second is reading madness as a separate sickness, rather than the passions in excess. And the third is thinking Hobbes denies that Christ healed the demoniacs. He says nothing of the sort.
