@chapter Thinking as bookkeeping
@title text="Reason Is Reckoning" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 5" art=thumb
You've done this at a restaurant table. The bill comes, someone adds it up, someone else checks it, and either the totals agree or they don't. Hobbes thinks that's what all reasoning is, once it's done in words.

@quote text="When a man Reasoneth, hee does nothing else but conceive a summe totall, from Addition of parcels; or conceive a Remainder, from Substraction of one summe from another" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 5"
When a man reasoneth, he does nothing else but conceive a sum total from addition of parcels, or a remainder from subtraction of one sum from another.

@art square-from-parts
It's a deflating claim. Reason isn't a light in the mind. It's bookkeeping, kept in names. A square is a figure with four sides, right angles and equal sides. From the three names you reach the one. Subtract, and you get back to the parts.

@words text="Writers of politics add together pacts, to find men's duties"
And it isn't only numbers. The same two operations run in lines, angles, force. Writers of politics add pacts together to find men's duties. That's a plan for the rest of the book.

@chapter The definition
@list heading="Reason is reckoning of" items="consequences@consequences|of general names@general|agreed upon@agreed upon|for marking and signifying@marking"
Every phrase of the definition does a job. Reason is reckoning of consequences, what follows from what, not of facts. Of general names, so it's about kinds. Agreed upon, so it's only as good as the settled meanings it starts from. And for marking your thoughts alone, and signifying them to others, so they can be checked.

@chapter Adding affirmations
@art cancelling-middle
Two names make an affirmation, and two affirmations make a syllogism. All Greeks are men, all men are mortal. Hobbes says the affirmations are added. The lesson pictures it as man ending the first and starting the second, so it drops out, leaving all Greeks are mortal.

@art venn-regions
The lab checks it another way, by a later method that agrees with Hobbes on every case here. Each statement says some region of a Venn diagram is empty or full. The argument is valid if no filling keeps the premises and loses the conclusion. This one is Barbara, and it's valid.

@chapter Cats and dogs
@math heading="Undistributed middle" items="All cats are mammals@all cats;;All dogs are mammals@all dogs;;∴ All dogs are cats  ✗@so all dogs"
Now a failure. All cats are mammals. All dogs are mammals. So all dogs are cats. Both premises are true, and the argument's plainly bad. Mammal ends both, so nothing cancels. And the lab finds a filling where dogs sit outside the cats.

@words text="Subtraction finds the premise you'd need" sub="not one that follows"
Why does it tempt people? From a conclusion and one premise, you can subtract to find the other. But that finds the premise you would need, not one that follows. Hunt for it with cats and Greeks, and you're tempted by all Greeks are cats.

@chapter No one's reason is right reason
@art arbitrator
England in sixteen fifty one had had a civil war, a king beheaded, and many parties each sure the right was plain to anyone reasoning properly. Hobbes grants reason is always right, as arithmetic is infallible. But no one man's reason, nor any number of men's, makes the certainty.

@art trump-suit
So a disputed account is settled when the parties set up some arbitrator or judge, or they come to blows. And the man who demands right reason as judge, meaning his own, is like a card player who calls trump whatever suit he holds most of.

@words text="A reckoner may cast up false"
The natural law tradition objected that reason gives everyone access to the right, so a judge is only a convenience. Hobbes's answer: reason isn't at fault, but a reckoner may cast up false. And in eighteen forty seven, De Morgan showed that relations, like the head of a horse, escape the old syllogism. The lab shares that limit.

@chapter Error and absurdity
@list heading="Two ways to go wrong" items="Error: a wrong presumption of past or future@Error is|Absurdity: a general assertion inconceivable unless true@Absurdity"
Error is a deception about what's past or to come, when it might have been otherwise. You expected rain and it stayed dry. Absurdity is a general assertion whose possibility is unconceivable unless it's true, like a round quadrangle. That man hasn't erred. He's said nothing.

@art restaurant-bill
One last point keeps the arithmetic honest. A master of a family who adds up all the bills without checking how each was made up gains no more than if he'd accepted the account in gross. A conclusion is only as certain as every affirmation under it.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking your own conviction for right reason@The first|A valid argument has a true conclusion@The second|Absurdity is just a bigger error@And the third"
The first slip is taking your own conviction for right reason. The second is thinking a valid argument must have a true conclusion. It needs true premises too. And the third is treating absurdity as a bigger error, when it's a different thing.
