@chapter The diving board
@title text="The Passions" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 6" art=thumb
What is a feeling, exactly? Hobbes has a startlingly simple answer.

@art diving-board
Stand at the top of a high diving board. You want to be in the water, and you don't want to fall. What you feel at any moment depends on what you believe about the next second.

@quote text="For Appetite with an opinion of attaining, is called Hope. The same, without such opinion, Despaire. Aversion, with opinion of Hurt from the object, Feare." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 6"
Hobbes says that's all a feeling is: a motion toward or away from something, named by the opinion that goes with it. Appetite with an opinion of attaining is called hope. The same without such opinion, despair. Aversion with opinion of hurt from the object, fear.

@words text="Sudden courage, Anger."
Add the hope of avoiding that hurt by resisting, and fear is courage. Add suddenness, and the person just pushed in the queue isn't scared and brave by turns. He's angry. Anger is sudden courage.

@chapter Endeavour
@art two-motions
The ground is the body. Animals have two kinds of motion. Vital motion, blood, pulse and breath, needs no imagination. Voluntary motion does: imagination is its first internal beginning.

@art endeavour-arrows
Before a motion shows in walking or striking, it starts small, too small to see. Hobbes calls it endeavour. Endeavour toward the thing that causes it is appetite, or desire. Endeavour away from something is aversion. And the schools' claim that appetite is only metaphorical motion is, he says, absurd.

@chapter Good is what you want
@words text="Good: the object of a man's appetite, for his part"
Now the step that matters for politics. If appetite moves a man, then good is just his name for what he wants, and evil for what he hates. There's nothing simply and absolutely good, and no common rule taken from the objects themselves.

@art arbitrator-between
So where does a common rule come from? In a commonwealth, from the person who represents it. Without one, from each man himself, or from an arbitrator that disputing men set up. That's the shape of Part Two. Ralph Cudworth wrote against exactly this, holding that good isn't made by appetite.

@chapter Building the names
@math heading="The ladder" items="fear = aversion + opinion of hurt@fear is;;courage = fear + hope of avoiding@courage is;;anger = courage + sudden@anger is;;confidence = constant hope@confidence;;diffidence = constant despair@diffidence"
The lab builds the passions as sums. Fear is aversion and an opinion of hurt. Courage is fear and the hope of avoiding. Anger is courage and suddenness. Confidence is constant hope, and diffidence constant despair.

@math heading="What follows" items="anger is fear: true@anger is fear;;courage is anger: false, no sudden@not anger;;confidence is hope: true@confidence is hope;;diffidence is hope: absurd@absurd"
Run it. Anger is fear: true, which sounds odd until you see fear inside courage. Courage is not anger, because suddenness is missing. Confidence is hope, but hope isn't confidence. And diffidence is hope is absurd. You can't have appetite with and without the opinion of attaining at once.

@chapter One case, many names
@art the-charge
Turn it round. A soldier charges the line: aversion, an opinion of hurt, a hope of getting through, and all at once. That's anger, courage and fear together. Not indignation, which needs great hurt done to someone else.

@words text="Names overlap by construction"
So a case under two names at once isn't a muddle. Hobbes builds one name on another, so they overlap. A gambler's patient wait is confidence and hope. A lost cause, with no opinion of attaining, is diffidence, and can't be hope.

@chapter A cold view
@art laughter-and-pity
The rest of the list is a cold view of ordinary life. Much laughter at others' defects is a sign of a small spirit. Pity is grief for another's calamity, from imagining it might befall yourself. Each passion carries a comparison with someone else.

@words text="Pity = disguised self-love?" sub="Butler, 1726"
In seventeen twenty six, Joseph Butler preached against the view he ascribed to Hobbes, that pity is just self-love in disguise. Compassion, he argued, is real regard for the other. The lab sees only names, so it can't settle that.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Good is a property of the thing@The first|Courage is the absence of fear@The second|Each name is a separate feeling@And the third"
The first slip is taking good to be a property of the thing. The second is thinking courage is the absence of fear, when courage contains fear. And the third is treating each name as a separate feeling with its own cause, when one motion takes many names.
