@chapter A ledger with no meanings
@title text="Absurdity and Science" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 5" art=thumb
Imagine a bookkeeper handed a ledger whose columns are headed with words nobody ever explained. He can add all day, and the total will be a number. It'll tell him nothing.

@quote text="The first cause of Absurd conclusions I ascribe to the want of Method; in that they begin not their Ratiocination from Definitions;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 5"
That's Hobbes's first cause. The first cause of absurd conclusions I ascribe to the want of method; in that they begin not their ratiocination from definitions. It's like casting accounts without knowing the value of one, two and three.

@art bookkeeper-ledger
Philosophers, he says, never start from definitions. That method is used only in geometry, and it's why geometry's conclusions can't be disputed. As Cicero said, nothing is so absurd it can't be found in the books of philosophers.

@chapter Names of the wrong kind
@art three-kinds
The next four causes are really one mistake. Names belong to kinds: bodies, the accidents of bodies, like round or hot, and names themselves. Put a name of one kind where another belongs, and you get nonsense.

@list heading="Causes two to five" items="Body names for accidents: faith is poured@faith is|Outside accidents for our own: colour in the body@the colour|Body names for names: a creature is a genus@genus|Accident names for names: a man's command is his will@command"
Saying faith is poured or breathed in, when only a body can be poured. Saying the colour is in the body and the sound in the air. Saying a living creature is a genus, a body name given to a name. And saying a man's command is his will.

@chapter Four verdicts
@art four-verdicts
The lab draws this with a small vocabulary. Each name is a sum of simpler names, and some pairs of simple names can't be added. True means every part of the predicate is in the subject. False means a part is missing. Absurd means the sum joins a clashing pair. Insignificant means a name stands for nothing.

@words text="The lab's sorting, not Hobbes's"
One caution. Those four verdicts are the lab's sorting. Hobbes says a false general inference, though commonly called error, is indeed an absurdity. So for him, an animal is a man would be absurd as well.

@math heading="The causes preset" items="a man is an animal: true@true;;an animal is a man: false, no rational@rational;;a circle is a quadrangle: absurd@absurd;;faith is poured: absurd, accident + body@accident;;a man is transubstantiate: insignificant@insignificant"
So, by the definitions. A man is an animal: true. An animal is a man: false, rational is missing. A circle is a quadrangle: absurd. Faith is poured: absurd, an accident added to a body. And a man is transubstantiate: insignificant, because nobody defined the word.

@art round-quadrangle
Notice that the absurd claims were never tested against the facts about faith or circles. They fail before that, because the two names can't be added.

@chapter Whose definition?
@art two-definitions
That last one is his seventh cause, words learned by rote from the schools. And the verdicts depend on the definitions. A substance is immaterial is absurd if substance means body. For Descartes and Henry More, there are two kinds of substance, so the claim is just false. The lab can't say whose definition is right.

@words text="The way goes to London" sub="lawful in speech, not in reckoning"
Metaphor, the sixth cause, he treats gently. It's lawful to say the way goes to London. But in reckoning, and seeking truth, such speeches aren't admitted.

@chapter What science is
@quote text="Science is the knowledge of Consequences, and dependance of one fact upon another" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 5"
Avoid the seven, and it's hard to fall into absurdity, because all men by nature reason alike, and well, with good principles. Sense and memory are knowledge of fact. Science is the knowledge of consequences, and dependence of one fact upon another.

@art fencers
His example is fencing. One man has natural skill with his weapons. Another has that, and has also learned from a master where he can strike or be struck in every posture. The first is prudent, the second has science. Both useful, but the latter infallible.

@words text="Science can be taught · prudence's signs are uncertain"
The signs differ too. Your science is certain when you can teach it and demonstrate it. The signs of prudence are all uncertain. Still, Hobbes says the unlearned, with natural prudence, are in a nobler state than men trusting false rules.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The lab's four verdicts are Hobbes's@The first|The sixth cause bans metaphor@The second|Hobbes despises the unlearned@And the third"
The first slip is taking the lab's four verdicts for Hobbes's own. The second is reading the sixth cause as a ban on metaphor. It's only a ban in reckoning. And the third is thinking Hobbes despises people without science. His contempt is for the learned who never define their words.
