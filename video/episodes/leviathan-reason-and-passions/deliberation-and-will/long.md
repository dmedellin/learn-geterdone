@chapter An afternoon of wanting
@title text="Deliberation and the Will" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 6" art=thumb
What is the will? Hobbes's answer is so plain it's almost shocking.

@art coins-and-friend
A friend asks to borrow money. You're fond of him and sorry for his trouble. Then you remember the last time. You think what his gratitude would be worth, and then hear others have lent to him already.

@art seesaw
Over one afternoon you've wanted to lend, and wanted not to, several times. And at some point you did, or didn't. Hobbes calls the whole afternoon deliberation: the whole sum of desires, aversions, hopes and fears, continued till the thing is done, or thought impossible.

@words text="Deliberation: an end to the liberty we had"
He says it's called deliberation because it puts an end to the liberty we had of doing or omitting, as we wanted. And there's no deliberation about the past, or what you know is impossible. Beasts deliberate too.

@chapter The last appetite
@quote text="In Deliberation, the last Appetite, or Aversion, immediately adhaering to the action, or to the omission thereof, is that wee call the Will;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 6"
Then the definition. In deliberation, the last appetite or aversion, immediately adhering to the action, or to the omission thereof, is that we call the will. The act, not the faculty, of willing.

@art rational-appetite
The schools defined the will as a rational appetite. Hobbes says that's no good. If it were, there could be no voluntary act against reason. Yet a man acting against his better judgement acts voluntarily. Swap in an appetite resulting from a precedent deliberation, and the definition works.

@words text="Earlier wishes: inclinations, not the will"
So what about the moments you meant to lend? Those were inclinations. If the earlier appetites made the act voluntary, the earlier aversions would make it involuntary too, and one act would be both. That's the argument the lab checks by counting.

@chapter Running the lend
@math heading="The lend preset" items="1 pity: do@pity;;2 fear of loss: omit@fear of loss;;3 hope of gratitude: do@hope;;4 rumour of debts: omit@a rumour;;5 rumour confirmed: omit@confirmed"
Here are five steps, each leaning toward doing or omitting. Pity leans to do. Fear of loss, to omit. Hope of gratitude, to do. A rumour of other debts, to omit. And the rumour confirmed, to omit.

@math heading="Where the will falls" items="last step: omit → no loan@the last;;direction changes: 3@three times;;earlier stops giving the opposite will: 2 of 4@two of the four"
The last step is an aversion to lending, so the will is to omit, and there's no loan. The direction changed three times. And two of the four earlier stops, after pity and after hope, would have given the opposite will.

@art reversed-order
Now keep the same five motions, in a different order. The rumour and its confirmation first, pity and hope last. The will is to lend, and he lends. Nothing about the friend or the money changed. What decides is the order of the passions, not which is wiser.

@chapter Fear and the storm
@art storm-ship
A shipmaster in a gale wonders whether to throw the cargo overboard. It's his year's profit, the ship may founder, the next wave is higher. His last step is fear of what follows if he doesn't, so he throws it. And Hobbes says that act is voluntary.

@quote text="Feare, and Liberty are consistent" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 21"
Acts that begin from fear of what follows an omission are as voluntary as acts from desire. In chapter twenty one he says it outright: fear and liberty are consistent. The man throws his goods in the sea very willingly.

@chapter Objections
@list heading="Three objections" items="The last appetite wins by stipulation@by stipulation|Bramhall: the will chooses among the motions@Bramhall|Giving beasts a will empties the word@beasts"
A critic has three things to say. The definition makes the will the winner by stipulation, however idle the last appetite. Bishop Bramhall held the will is a power choosing among the motions, so without it there's no liberty. And giving beasts a will may empty the word.

@art long-chain
Hobbes adds that good and evil depend on foreseeing a long chain of consequences, which few can see to the end. Whoever sees furthest deliberates best. And there's no perpetual tranquillity, because life itself is but motion, and can never be without desire, nor without fear.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="An earlier strong wish was the will@The first|The will is a rational power over appetite@The second|An act done from fear is involuntary@And the third"
The first slip is thinking an earlier strong wish was the will. It was an inclination. The second is making the will a rational power that overrules appetite. And the third is treating an act done from fear as involuntary, when the shipmaster wills it.
