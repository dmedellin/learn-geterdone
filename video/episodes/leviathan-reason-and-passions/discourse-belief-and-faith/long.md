@chapter A web of ifs
@title text="The Ends of Discourse" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 7" art=thumb
Here's a puzzle about what reasoning can, and can't, tell you.

@art bridge-road-bus
Suppose you know that if the bridge is out, the road is closed. And if the road is closed, the bus will be late. You know a lot. You still don't know whether the bridge is out, or whether the bus will be late.

@quote text="No man can know by Discourse, that this, or that, is, has been, or will be; which is to know absolutely: but onely, that if This be, That is;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 7"
Hobbes says that's the most reasoning ever gives. No man can know by discourse that this or that is, has been, or will be, which is to know absolutely, but only that if this be, that is.

@art eye-and-memory
What turns an if into an is? Not reasoning. Knowledge of fact is originally sense, and ever after, memory. So no discourse can end in absolute knowledge of fact, past or to come.

@chapter Judgement and doubt
@art two-chains
The chapter pairs this with the last one. Alternating appetite, in deliberating about good and evil, is alternating opinion in the search for truth. The last appetite is the will. The last opinion is the judgement. And the whole alternation is doubt.

@words text="Knowledge of one name of a thing to another name of the same thing"
Science, then, is knowledge about names. If a circle is a figure whose points are all equally far from a centre, any line through the centre halves it. That follows from the word, without anyone drawing a circle. That's why it's certain, and why it brings no news about the world.

@chapter The logic of if
@math heading="The chain" items="p: it's a circle@let p;;q: all points equally far from the centre@q;;r: any line through the centre halves it@r;;p → q,  q → r  ∴  p → r   valid@valid"
The lab makes the limit precise. Let p be: the figure is a circle. Let q be: all its points are equally far from the centre. And r: any line through the centre halves it. If p then q, and if q then r, so if p then r. It's valid, a conditional from conditionals.

@math heading="Detach r" items="p → q,  q → r  ∴  r   invalid@offer r alone;;p false, q false, r false@all false;;add the fact p  ∴  r   valid@add the premise"
Now offer r alone as the conclusion. It's invalid. The row that defeats it has p, q and r all false, and both conditionals still hold. Add the premise p, that this figure really is a circle, and r follows. But p is a fact, and facts come from sense and memory.

@art experiment-lab
A critic says this sells reasoning short. In the sixteen sixties, Robert Boyle and the Royal Society founded natural knowledge on matters of fact made public by experiment. Hobbes thought that wasn't philosophy, and they quarrelled in print.

@chapter Conscience
@art witnesses
When reasoning doesn't start from definitions, it ends in opinion. And here Hobbes turns to conscience. It first meant two or more people knowing one fact together, fit witnesses. Then men used it for knowing their own secret thoughts.

@words text="They know at most, but that they think so"
Last, men in love with their own new opinions gave them that reverenced name, and so pretend to know they're true, when they know at most that they think so. In sixteen fifty one, after a war fought largely over conscience, that was not a neutral remark.

@chapter Belief in a person
@quote text="So that in Beleefe are two opinions; one of the saying of the man; the other of his vertue." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 7"
Now faith. When your reasoning starts from someone else's word, whose ability and honesty you don't doubt, it's about the person, not the thing. So that in belief are two opinions: one of the saying of the man, the other of his virtue.

@math heading="Testimony" items="s: he says p@he says;;r: he is reliable@reliable;;s, r, (s and r) → p  ∴  p   valid@valid;;drop r  ∴  p   invalid@drop"
The lab shows it. He says p. He's reliable. And if he says it and is reliable, it's so. Then p follows: valid. Drop the premise that he's reliable, and it's invalid. There's a row where he says it and isn't reliable, and p is false.

@art livy-cow
The same goes for history. If you disbelieve Livy's story of a cow that spoke, you don't distrust God, but Livy. And without immediate revelation, believing that Scripture is God's word is trust in the Church, whose word you take.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A valid chain of ifs proves its last then@The first|Belief is one opinion about what's said@The second|Strong conviction is knowledge@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a valid chain of ifs proves its last then. The second is treating belief as one opinion, when it holds two, and the second is about the man. And the third is taking a strong conviction for knowledge.
