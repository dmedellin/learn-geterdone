@chapter Why a model
@title text="Modeling and Measuring Culture" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Culture is a word with a hundred definitions. To study it in software delivery, the authors needed a model that's well defined in the scientific literature, can be measured, and has predictive power in their field.

@quote text="Westrum's further insight was that the organizational culture predicts the way information flows through an organization." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 3"
Here's the sentence that explains their choice. Westrum's further insight was that the organizational culture predicts the way information flows through an organization.

@chapter Three levels
@art schein-levels
Within organizational culture, the authors follow Edgar Schein, who describes three levels. Basic assumptions are the least visible: the things a team simply knows and struggles to say. Values and norms can be debated openly and shape how people act. And artifacts are the most visible: mission statements, procedures, technology, heroes and rituals.

@words text="The level of values"
The authors work at the second level, values. That's what most people mean when they say a team has a culture.

@chapter Westrum's three types
@art three-types
The model they chose is Ron Westrum's, a sociologist who studied safety in fields where accidents are costly. His typology from nineteen eighty eight has three types.

@list heading="Westrum's typology" items="Pathological: power-oriented@pathological|Bureaucratic: rule-oriented@bureaucratic|Generative: performance-oriented@generative"
A pathological organization is power oriented, marked by fear and threat, where people hoard information or bend it. A bureaucratic organization is rule oriented and protects its departments and turf. A generative organization is performance oriented, and everything serves the mission.

@chapter Good information
@art information-flow
Westrum's point is about information. Good information answers the receiver's questions, it's timely, and it's presented so the receiver can use it. Good flow matters most where work is fast and mistakes are expensive, and technology organizations are among those.

@art aspect-dials
Chapter three then sets the three types side by side, and they differ on six dials.

@list heading="Six aspects" items="Cooperation@cooperation|Messengers@messengers|Responsibilities@responsibilities|Bridging@bridging|Failure@failure|Novelty@novelty"
The three types differ on six aspects: cooperation, how messengers are treated, responsibilities, bridging between groups, how failure is handled, and how novelty is received. On failure, the contrast runs from blaming someone, through judging them by the rules, to asking what happened.

@words text="Bureaucracy aims at fairness" sub="rules counting for more than the mission"
The authors don't make bureaucracy the villain. They quote Mark Schwartz, who argues its purpose is fairness: the same rules for every case. They've seen generative federal government teams and pathological startups, so you can't read the type off the kind of organization.

@chapter Scoring a team
@art scale-continuum
Because the types sit on a continuum, they can be scored. The lab asks each respondent to rate the six aspects from one to seven. The lesson sets its own cut points, three and five, since the chapter gives none: below three is nearest pathological, three up to five bureaucratic, and five and above generative.

@words text="2016: 31% · 48% · 21%" sub="pathological · bureaucratic · generative"
A footnote in the chapter reports that in twenty sixteen, thirty one percent of respondents were classified as pathological, forty eight percent as bureaucratic, and twenty one percent as generative. It doesn't say there where the lines were drawn.

@math heading="Respondent r1" items="6 + 5 + 6 + 6 + 5 + 6 = 34@thirty four;;34 / 6 = 5.667  → generative band@five point six six seven"
Respondent r one answered six, five, six, six, five, six: thirty four, and thirty four over six is five point six six seven. That's five or above, the generative band.

@math heading="The whole team: mixed" items="totals: 34, 28, 17, 34, 22, 14, 28@all seven;;177 / 42 = 4.214  → bureaucratic band@four point two one four;;bands: 2 pathological · 3 bureaucratic · 2 generative@two in the pathological"
All seven totals add to one hundred and seventy seven, over forty two answers, for a team mean of four point two one four, the bureaucratic band. But look at the people: two in the pathological band, three bureaucratic, and two generative. Nobody is at four point two one four.

@art same-mean
Now a second team, even, with the same team mean. Everyone answers four or five on everything, so all seven sit in the bureaucratic band. Same figure, very different teams. Look at answers person by person before acting on a team mean.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Three boxes, not a scale@The first|Bureaucratic as an insult@The second|The team mean describes the people@And the third"
The first slip is treating the types as three boxes. The authors use them as points on a scale, which is what makes them measurable. The second is reading bureaucratic as an insult. And the third is taking the team mean as a description of the people in the team.
