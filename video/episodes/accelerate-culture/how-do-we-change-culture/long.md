@chapter The question
@title text="How Do We Change Culture?" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 3" art=thumb
If culture predicts how well a team delivers, the obvious next question is: how do you change it? The last section of chapter three gives its answer, and it starts in a car plant.

@art car-plant
John Shook helped transform the culture of the Fremont, California, car plant, the starting point of the Lean manufacturing movement in the United States. What he learned there is the line that opens the section.

@quote text="the way to change culture is not to first change how people think, but instead to start by changing how people behave—what they do" cite="John Shook, as quoted in Accelerate, Chapter 3"
The way to change culture is not to first change how people think, but instead to start by changing how people behave, what they do.

@chapter The hypothesis
@art practices-to-culture
The authors take that as a hypothesis for software. Following the theory of the Lean and Agile movements, implementing their practices can have an effect on culture. So they measured the impact of both technical practices and management practices on the culture score.

@list heading="Practices that affect culture" items="Lean management@Lean management|Continuous delivery@continuous delivery"
Their research shows, they say, that Lean management, together with the set of technical practices called continuous delivery, does affect culture. The practices themselves come in later chapters.

@chapter What it does and doesn't say
@list heading="The chapter does not say" items="Culture changes by decree@by decree|A values statement will do it@values statement|How long a change takes@how long"
The conclusion is practical: you can act your way to a better culture, by implementing these practices. It doesn't say culture changes by decree, by a values statement, or by a training day. And it doesn't say how long a change takes.

@words text="A pattern across many teams" sub="not one team followed through a change"
Its evidence is the relationship between practices and culture scores in the survey data. It doesn't follow one team through a change. And that difference is what the lesson's lab explores.

@chapter One team, two surveys
@art two-surveys
The lab takes two surveys, six months apart, of the same six invented people, on the six Westrum aspects with cut points three and five. Between them, the team adopted some new practices. The lesson doesn't say which, because the numbers can't.

@math heading="Respondent s1" items="before: 17 / 6 = 2.833  pathological@seventeen over six;;after: 26 / 6 = 4.333  bureaucratic@twenty six over six;;change: +1.500@one point five"
Take respondent s one. Before, seventeen over six, two point eight three three, the pathological band. After, twenty six over six, four point three three three, bureaucratic. A change of plus one point five.

@math heading="The team" items="before: 126 / 36 = 3.500@one hundred and twenty six;;after: 149 / 36 = 4.139@one hundred and forty nine;;change: +0.639 = 23/36@zero point six three nine"
For the whole team, one hundred and twenty six over thirty six is three point five. After, one hundred and forty nine over thirty six is four point one three nine. The change is plus zero point six three nine.

@art person-changes
Now look person by person. The changes run from plus one point five down to minus zero point one six seven. Five people rose or stayed level, and one fell a little. 
@art band-thresholds
Only s one changed band, moving from pathological to bureaucratic. Nobody crossed into generative, so the team stays bureaucratic. A score can rise by two thirds of a point and leave the team in the same band, which is a good reason to look at both the change and the bands.

@chapter Is that a lot?
@words text="Changed nothing: 3.500 → 3.639" sub="+0.139"
Is plus zero point six three nine a lot? A second team began from the same answers and adopted nothing. Its people simply answered again, and their mean moved from three point five to three point six three nine, plus zero point one three nine. People answer a little differently on a second day.

@art other-causes
And between the surveys, the team may also have lost a person, gained a manager, shipped a hard release, or been reorganized. Any of those can move a score. The change tells you the numbers moved. It doesn't tell you why.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Change minds first, expect behaviour to follow@The first|A rise proves the practices did it@The second|The team mean is each person's change@the third"
The first slip is changing how people think first, and expecting behaviour to follow. The second is reading a rise in the score as proof the practices did it. And the third is reading the team mean as the change in each person. It can hide a fall.

@chapter Try it
@words text="x1 · x2 · x3, before and after"
Your turn. Three people take two surveys. Find each person's mean before and after, the change for each, and the team's mean and change. With cut points three and five, who changes band? And what would you need to know to explain the change?
