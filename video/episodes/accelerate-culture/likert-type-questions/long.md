@chapter From types to a scale
@title text="Measuring Culture" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 3" art=thumb
How do you turn culture into a number? Chapter three's step is to notice that Westrum's three types are points on a scale, a continuum. And anything on a scale is a good candidate for Likert type questions.

@quote text="For this approach to work, the statement must be worded strongly, so that people can strongly agree or disagree" cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 3"
The authors begin the practical part like this. For this approach to work, the statement must be worded strongly, so that people can strongly agree or disagree.

@chapter Likert-type answers
@art likert-scale
A Likert scale comes from psychometrics, the measurement of people's perceptions. A respondent rates a statement by how far they agree. The authors give the answers values from one, the strongest disagreement, to seven, the strongest agreement, with neutral at four.

@words text="1 strongly disagree · 4 neutral · 7 strongly agree"
The book's figure three point one re creates the statements they wrote from Westrum's model. The lesson doesn't reproduce them, and its lab uses four items of its own.

@chapter Building a construct
@art construct-bundle
Asking is the easy half. Next, the authors test whether people read the statements the same way, and whether the statements belong together. Only a set that passes is called a construct, here Westrum organizational culture, and only then does it go into further research.

@list heading="Tested before any correlation" items="Discriminant validity@discriminant|Convergent validity@convergent|Reliability@reliability"
Three kinds of test are named. Discriminant and convergent validity ask whether unrelated items stay unrelated and related ones hang together. And reliability, also called internal consistency. The authors report their Westrum construct has consistently been found valid and reliable.

@art averaging
The authors say that, because the construct passed, you can use the questions in your own surveys.

@words text="score = mean of the 1–7 answers"
A response is scored by averaging its one to seven values over every question. Why several statements? That's the lesson's gloss: one answer carries a person's mood that day, and a mean of several lets one stray answer weigh less.

@chapter Reversing an item
@art survey-sheet
Now a count. The lesson's lab takes a scale of seven, four items in one construct it calls flow, and six respondents, each answering every item.

@art reverse-item
The chapter doesn't discuss reversing. The lesson adds it, because any survey that mixes directions has to deal with it. One of the four items, blame, is worded so that agreeing is bad news.

@math heading="Reverse on a scale of 7" items="reversed = 8 − answer@eight minus;;1 ↔ 7 · 2 ↔ 6 · 3 ↔ 5 · 4 stays 4@one and seven"
For a reversed item on a scale of seven, replace the answer with eight minus the answer. One and seven swap, two and six, three and five. And four stays put.

@chapter One respondent, both ways
@math heading="r3 answered 3, 4, 3, 6" items="blame reversed: 8 − 6 = 2@eight minus six;;3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 12,  12 / 4 = 3.0@twelve over four;;forgotten: 3 + 4 + 3 + 6 = 16,  16 / 4 = 4.0@sixteen over four"
Take respondent r three, who answered three, four, three and six, the six to blame. Reverse it: eight minus six is two. The answers sum to twelve, and twelve over four is three point oh. Forget the reversal and the sum is sixteen, and sixteen over four is four point oh.

@art pulled-to-middle
Three is below the midpoint of four. The forgotten score sits right on it. Across all six respondents, the construct mean drops from four point six two five to four point three seven five, and everyone's pulled toward the middle.

@math heading="Six respondents, forgotten" items="r6: 2.0 → 3.5@r six;;r4: 6.5 → 5.0@r four;;construct: 4.625 → 4.375@four point three seven five"
R six, who strongly agreed that blame is common, moves from two point oh to three point five. R four, who strongly disagreed, drops from six point five to five point oh. So the construct moves to four point three seven five. And someone who answers four is unchanged, so a neutral person never shows the mistake.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Averaging without reversing@The first|One statement as the measure@The second|A score as a fact about the organization@the third"
The first slip is averaging a negative item without reversing it. The second is treating one statement as the measure. R three answered four to ask, which alone would be neutral, but four items give three point oh. And the third is reading a score as a fact about the organization. It's a summary of what people said they perceive.

@chapter Try it
@words text="q1: 7, 6, 7, 1 · q2: 4, 4, 4, 4 · q3: 2, 3, 2, 6"
Your turn. Three respondents answer the same four items, blame last. Score each with blame reversed, and then without. Whose score changes most when the reversal is forgotten, and by how much? And why does one respondent's score not change at all?
