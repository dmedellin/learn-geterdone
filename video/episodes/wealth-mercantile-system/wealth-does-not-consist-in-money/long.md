@chapter The question
@title text="Wealth Does Not Consist in Money" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 1" art=thumb
When you say a neighbour is worth a great deal, you mean money. For one person, wealth and money do run together. Book Four asks whether the same holds for a whole country.

@quote text="consists in money, or in gold and silver, is a popular notion which naturally arises from the double function of money, as the instrument of commerce, and as the measure of value" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 1"
Smith says the belief that wealth consists in money, or in gold and silver, is a popular notion which naturally arises from the double function of money, as the instrument of commerce, and as the measure of value.

@chapter Gold or cattle?
@art gold-and-cattle
The Spaniards' first question on any unknown coast was whether there was gold or silver nearby. The Tartars asked a visiting monk whether France had plenty of sheep and oxen. Same question, different money. Of the two, Smith says, the Tartar notion was perhaps the nearest to the truth.

@chapter The merchants' case
@words text="Money is a steady friend"
The belief had serious defenders. Locke held that other goods are consumed and wasted, while money is a steady friend. Others said a country fighting wars abroad must send money to pay for them. So Spain and Portugal, and once England, France and Scotland, banned or taxed its export.

@art seed-time
Then the merchants complained. Thomas Mun compared foreign trade to a farmer at seed time, throwing good corn into the ground. Sending gold out could bring more back. And since gold is easily smuggled, they said, government should watch the balance of trade instead.

@words text="Partly solid and partly sophistical"
Smith's verdict is balanced. Those arguments were partly solid and partly sophistical. Solid, because no law can stop gold going where trade wants it. Sophistical, because gold needs the government's attention no more than wine does. The result swapped one fruitless care for another, just as fruitless.

@chapter What gold is
@art packet-boat
His answer starts with what gold is. A country with wherewithal to buy wine always gets the wine it needs, and the same goes for gold. One packet boat from Lisbon could bring fifty tons of gold, more than five million guineas. The same value of grain would need a thousand ships of a thousand tons each.

@art kitchen-pots
Nobody thinks a family dines better for keeping more pots and pans than its cooking needs. Gold and silver, Smith says, are utensils, as much as the furniture of the kitchen. Money necessarily runs after goods, but goods don't always run after money.

@math heading="Paying for the war" items="90,000,000 · 2/3 = 60,000,000 abroad@two thirds;;12 · 6/19 = 72/19 months@twelve times"
And war? The last French war cost Britain upwards of ninety million pounds, and more than two thirds was spent abroad, about sixty million. The mines of Spain and Portugal brought in about six million a year. Against nineteen million spent in seventeen sixty one, twelve times six nineteenths is a little under four months. Fleets and armies are maintained with goods.

@chapter The balance, partner by partner
@math heading="Three partners" items="Holland: 400,000 − 300,000 = +100,000@Holland;;France: 100,000 − 250,000 = −150,000@France;;Portugal: 300,000 − 120,000 = +180,000@Portugal;;total: +130,000@in total"
Here's the figure the system watched, with partners written for the lesson. Exports minus imports. With Holland, plus a hundred thousand. With France, minus a hundred and fifty thousand. With Portugal, plus a hundred and eighty thousand. So the country is ahead by a hundred and thirty thousand in total, but behind with France.

@art balance-scale
On the system's view, restrain the French trade. Do it, with France doing the same, and the balance rises to three hundred thousand while the total trade falls from one million four hundred and seventy thousand to one million one hundred and forty thousand. A third preset, with the same trade and no balance at all, would score lowest. Does that score tell you anything?

@chapter What trade is for
@art wider-market
Smith says foreign trade carries out the surplus for which there's no demand at home, and brings back something there is a demand for. By widening the market, it lets the division of labour go further. Bringing in gold is not the principal, much less the sole, benefit. What enriched Europe in America was a new market, not cheaper plate.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking money doesn't matter@The first|Reading a favourable balance as a gain@The second|Hearing the merchants as simply wrong@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Smith says money doesn't matter. Wealth just isn't money. The second is reading a favourable balance as a gain. And the third is hearing him say the merchants were simply wrong. Keynes later argued they had a real point about interest rates, which this chapter doesn't take up.
