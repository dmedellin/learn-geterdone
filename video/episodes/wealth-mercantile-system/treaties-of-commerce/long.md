@chapter The question
@title text="Treaties of Commerce" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 6" art=thumb
In seventeen oh three, England signed a treaty with Portugal that was celebrated as a masterpiece of commercial policy. Smith thinks it was nothing of the kind, and his reason is simple.

@art one-wholesaler
Think of a shop that promises to buy its wine from only one wholesaler. That wholesaler no longer needs to be the cheapest, and the shop's customers pay for the arrangement. A treaty that admits one country's goods on better terms than others does the same.

@quote text="Such treaties, however, though they may be advantageous to the merchants and manufacturers of the favoured, are necessarily disadvantageous to those of the favouring country." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 6"
Such treaties, however, though they may be advantageous to the merchants and manufacturers of the favoured, are necessarily disadvantageous to those of the favouring country.

@words text="A smaller gain, not a loss outright"
But he's careful. The favouring country won't sell below cost, so it loses part of its gain, not all of it. Even the favouring country may still gain by the trade, though less than with free competition.

@chapter The Methuen treaty
@list heading="The Methuen treaty, 1703" items="Portugal admits English woollens as before@Portugal|Britain admits Portuguese wine at two thirds of the French duty@two thirds"
The treaty had two main promises. Portugal would admit English woollens as before, which Smith says was no favour at all. In return, Britain would admit Portuguese wine paying only two thirds of the duty on French wine. So far, he says, it's evidently advantageous to Portugal, and disadvantageous to Great Britain.

@chapter Pricing the preference
@math heading="A tun of wine" items="French: 20 + 30 = 50@French;;Portuguese: 22 + (2/3) · 30 = 42@Portuguese"
Let's price it. Only the two thirds is Smith's. The rest is written for the lesson. French wine costs twenty pounds a tun delivered, plus thirty duty, fifty. Portuguese wine costs twenty two, but pays only two thirds of the duty, twenty. That's forty two. Portuguese wine costs more to bring, yet it's cheaper to the buyer. The preference decides what's bought.

@art two-barrels
But Smith's point is what happens next. The favoured merchants will often sell their goods for a better price than if exposed to free competition. They don't need to be cheapest, only cheaper than France.

@math heading="1,000 tuns · Portuguese price rises to £29" items="buyer pays: 29 + 20 = 49@forty nine;;treasury: 30,000 → 20,000@treasury;;sent abroad: 20,000 → 29,000@sent abroad;;loss: 10,000 − 1,000 = 9,000@the country"
Let the Portuguese price rise to twenty nine. The buyer pays forty nine, still just under France's fifty. On a thousand tuns, buyers save a thousand pounds. But the treasury takes twenty thousand instead of thirty thousand, and what's sent abroad rises from twenty thousand to twenty nine thousand. So the country loses nine thousand, the extra price paid to Portugal.

@art price-creeps-up
And no tile that watches only the duty can see it. The extra seven pounds a tun is hidden inside the foreign price. The cheapness of one thing is the dearness of another.

@chapter The case for gold
@art packet-boat-gold
The treaty's defenders pointed to gold. The weekly packet from Lisbon was said to bring more than fifty thousand pounds in gold. Fifty two weeks makes two million six hundred thousand a year, which Smith thinks more than Brazil is supposed to afford. And even if true, most of that gold must be sent out again for goods. Buying the goods directly would be better.

@chapter Seignorage
@art penelope-web
The chapter ends on coinage. In England, coinage was free. When the gold coin wore to two per cent below standard, new coin was melted and sold as bullion at a profit. Smith compares it to the web of Penelope. The work done in the day was undone in the night.

@math heading="Melting profit = shortfall − seignorage" items="2 − 5 = −3@five per cent;;2 − 2 = 0@two per cent;;2 − 1 = 1@one per cent"
A seignorage, a charge on coinage, stops it. With the coin two per cent below standard, a seignorage of five per cent makes melting a loss of three. At two per cent, neither profit nor loss. At one per cent, a profit of one.

@art melting-coin
Where money passes by tale, not by weight, a seignorage is the surest way to stop good coin being melted or exported. And a moderate one, Smith says, is advanced by everybody and finally paid by nobody, since everyone gets it back in the coin's value.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a treaty is always a loss@The first|Counting the gold as a gain in itself@The second|Thinking seignorage must be borne by someone@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Smith calls every treaty a loss. The second is counting the gold from Portugal as a gain in itself. And the third is thinking a moderate seignorage is a tax someone must bear. Ricardo later used this very case, cloth and wine, to show both countries can gain.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Coin 2% below standard · seignorage 5%" sub="Profit or loss from melting?"
Your turn. A gold coin is two per cent below its standard weight, and the seignorage is five per cent. Is melting it down a profit or a loss, and of how much? Then try the wine presets on a thousand tuns.
