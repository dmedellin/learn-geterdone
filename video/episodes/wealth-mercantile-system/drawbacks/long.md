@chapter The question
@title text="Drawbacks" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 4" art=thumb
Merchants weren't content with a monopoly at home. They wanted the most extensive foreign sale too. Abroad, their government couldn't give them a monopoly, so they asked for encouragements instead: drawbacks, bounties, treaties and colonies.

@words text="Of these encouragements, what are called drawbacks seem to be the most reasonable."
This short chapter takes the first. Of these encouragements, Smith says, what are called drawbacks seem to be the most reasonable. The question is why.

@chapter What a drawback is
@art duty-at-the-port
A man who imports sugar pays a duty at the port. A man who makes cloth may pay an excise at home. If either sells abroad, that duty is added to his price, and he's at a disadvantage against foreign sellers who paid none.

@art refund-on-export
A drawback gives it back. It's a refund, when goods are exported, of the whole or part of a duty paid on them. Refund more than the duty paid, and it isn't a drawback any more. It's a bounty.

@chapter Why it's reasonable
@art three-ledgers
Think of three ledgers. With no duty, so much is exported. With a duty and no drawback, less. With a drawback, the same as with no duty. So a drawback can never cause more exports than there'd have been with no duty at all. It doesn't overturn the natural balance of employments. It stops the duty overturning it.

@words text="The revenue profits by the part retained"
And the revenue isn't robbed. It profits from drawbacks by the part of the duty that's retained. If the whole duty had been kept, Smith says, the foreign goods could seldom have been exported, nor so imported, and the duty would never have been paid at all.

@chapter Smith's tobacco
@art hogsheads-split
Smith's example is tobacco. Before the colonies revolted, Britain imported about ninety six thousand hogsheads from Maryland and Virginia, and home consumption wasn't supposed to exceed fourteen thousand. To let the other eighty two thousand go abroad, the whole duties were drawn back if they left within three years.

@math heading="Duty of £5 a hogshead" items="paid: 96,000 · 5 = 480,000@paid on;;drawn back: 82,000 · 5 = 410,000@drawn back;;kept: 480,000 − 410,000 = 70,000@keeps"
Smith gives no duty per hogshead, so the lesson writes in five pounds. Duty paid on all ninety six thousand is four hundred and eighty thousand. Drawn back on the eighty two thousand, four hundred and ten thousand. The revenue keeps seventy thousand.

@math heading="What the revenue keeps" items="70,000 / 14,000 = 5 a hogshead@fourteen thousand;;half drawn back: 410,000 · 1/2 = 205,000 · keeps 275,000@half;;none drawn back: keeps 480,000@none"
Divide seventy thousand by the fourteen thousand that stayed, and it's exactly five pounds each. A full drawback leaves just the duty on what's consumed at home. Draw back half, as under the old subsidy, and the refund is two hundred and five thousand, keeping two hundred and seventy five thousand. Draw back none, and it keeps the whole four hundred and eighty thousand.

@chapter A foolish motive, a reasonable rule
@words text="The motive abundantly foolish · the institution reasonable enough"
The original drawbacks were perhaps granted to encourage the carrying trade, which was thought to bring in gold. Smith separates the motive from the rule. Though the motive was, perhaps, abundantly foolish, the institution itself seems reasonable enough. The carrying trade deserves no preference, but it should be left free.

@chapter Where the case stops
@art colony-monopoly
There are two limits. The first is the colonies. Where British merchants already hold a monopoly, the same goods would go even if the whole duty were kept. Then the refund is pure loss to the revenue.

@art smuggled-back
The second is fraud. A drawback is only useful if the goods really leave, and aren't quietly landed again at home. Tobacco, Smith says, was frequently abused that way, to the harm of the revenue and the fair trader. A paper trail can be made without the voyage.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Calling a drawback a bounty@The first|Thinking it costs the whole refund@The second|Thinking Smith defends every drawback@And the third"
The first slip is calling a drawback a bounty. The second is thinking it costs the revenue the whole refund. Without it, the goods might not have come at all. And the third is thinking Smith defends every drawback. The chapter is mostly a list of exceptions.
