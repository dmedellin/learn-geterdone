@chapter The question
@title text="The Balance of Trade and Banks of Deposit" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 3" art=thumb
England sends tobacco to France and gets back wine worth more. The books call it a loss. Smith calls it a gain. This chapter, the longest of the six, is about which count is right.

@art walled-ports
The system's second tool was to restrain trade with countries where the balance seemed against you. Britain's example was France. Duties of twenty five per cent in sixteen ninety two, another twenty five in sixteen ninety six, and subsidies on top. Seventy five per cent was the lowest duty on most French goods. The French did the same, and now smugglers are the principal importers.

@chapter On the system's own terms
@list heading="Even on its own terms" items="The general balance might not suffer@First|French goods could be sold on at a profit@Second|There's no certain criterion of the balance@third"
Smith argues on the system's own ground first. First, even if the balance with France were against us, the general balance might not be, since cheaper French goods mean smaller total imports. Second, French goods could be sold on to others at a profit. And third, the big one: there's no certain criterion for which side the balance lies on.

@words text="Custom-house books · the course of exchange"
Two tests were used. The custom house books, he says, are now generally acknowledged a very uncertain criterion, because goods are valued inaccurately. The other test was the course of exchange.

@chapter Paid through Holland
@math heading="Two partners" items="Holland: 900,000 − 600,000 = +300,000@Holland;;Baltic: 200,000 − 700,000 = −500,000@Baltic;;sent to Holland: 500,000 − 300,000 = 200,000@That uses up"
Here's why exchange misleads, with figures written for the lesson. England sells Holland nine hundred thousand and buys six hundred thousand, a balance of plus three hundred thousand. With the Baltic ports, minus five hundred thousand. English merchants paid the Baltic with bills on Holland. That uses up Holland's three hundred thousand and leaves two hundred thousand to send.

@art bills-through-holland
So England may have to send money to Holland even though its balance of trade with Holland is very much in its favour. The debts between two places are set by their dealings with everyone else too.

@chapter Worn coin and bank money
@art worn-coins
The computed exchange has more errors. Before King William's recoinage, English silver was rather more than twenty five per cent below its standard, so a quoted exchange against England might really have favoured it. And coinage was free in England, while France deducted eight per cent.

@art bank-of-amsterdam
Then the digression on the bank of Amsterdam. A great port receives clipped and worn coin of every country. So in sixteen oh nine a bank was set up, under the city's guarantee, that took coin at its real value and gave credit in its books. Every bill of six hundred guilders or more had to be paid in bank money.

@math heading="The agio" items="sold: 100 · 105/100 = 105@five per cent;;bought: 100 · 104/100 = 104@four;;3,000,000 · 11 = 33,000,000 guilders@eleven guilders"
Bank money is always worth more than the same sum in common currency, and that premium is the agio. The bank sold bank money at five per cent agio, a hundred and five guilders for a hundred, and bought at four. Its treasure is guesswork. Allow two thousand accounts at fifteen hundred pounds, and at eleven guilders a pound, that's thirty three million guilders.

@chapter What gain really is
@math heading="Tobacco for wine" items="balance: 100,000 − 110,000 = −10,000@the system;;gain: 110,000 − 100,000 = 10,000@Smith counts"
Now Smith's own ground. Gain means a rise in the exchangeable value of the annual produce, not in the quantity of gold. His figures: tobacco worth a hundred thousand in England buys wine worth a hundred and ten thousand. Counted as the system counts, minus ten thousand. Counted as Smith counts, the capital of England rises by ten thousand.

@art tobacco-for-wine
Same trade, a loss on one measure and a gain on the other. And Smith says nations have been taught that their interest consisted in beggaring all their neighbours. In fact, the wealth of neighbouring nations, though dangerous in war, is certainly advantageous in trade.

@chapter The balance that matters
@art produce-and-consumption
The balance that matters is between produce and consumption. If produce exceeds consumption, the society lives within its revenue, and its savings add to its capital. If not, the capital decays. A nation, Smith says, may import more than it exports for half a century, perhaps, while its capital still grows.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading exchange as the balance@The first|Thinking each pair must balance@The second|Taking bank money for a lending machine@And the third"
The first slip is reading the course of exchange as the balance of trade. The second is thinking each pair of countries has to balance. And the third is taking bank money for a lending machine. The bank professed to lend none of what was deposited.
