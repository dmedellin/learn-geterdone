@chapter The question
@title text="Bounties and the Corn Trade" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 5" art=thumb
You can't force foreigners to buy your goods, the way you force your own countrymen. So what's the next best thing? Pay them to buy. Smith finds that idea telling.

@quote text="The next best expedient, it has been thought, therefore, is to pay them for buying." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 5"
We cannot give our workmen a monopoly in the foreign, as we have done in the home market. The next best expedient, it has been thought, therefore, is to pay them for buying.

@chapter A losing trade
@art paid-to-buy
His first point is about which trades need bounties at all. Only those where the merchant sells for less than it cost to bring his goods to market. A trade that paid wouldn't need one. So a bounty always props up a losing trade, forcing capital into a channel less advantageous than it would find on its own.

@words text="The bounty is the smallest part of the expense"
Defenders pointed out that corn exports exceeded imports by far more than the bounties paid. Smith answers that the bounty is the smallest part of what exporting really costs. The farmer's capital has to be replaced too, and the whole reason for the bounty is that the price abroad didn't replace it.

@chapter Two taxes
@art two-taxes
Then the heart of it. The corn bounty, like every export bounty, imposes two taxes on the people. First, the tax they pay to fund the bounty. Second, the tax that comes from the higher price of corn at home.

@math heading="Smith's supposition" items="6d a bushel · 8 = 48d = 4s a quarter@a bushel;;31 · 4 = 124s = £6 4s@thirty one"
Smith supposes a bounty of five shillings a quarter raises the home price by six pence a bushel. A quarter is eight bushels, so that's four shillings a quarter. He takes from the Tracts that one quarter is exported for every thirty one eaten at home. Thirty one times four is a hundred and twenty four shillings, six pounds four.

@art one-in-thirty-one
So for every five shillings the public pays toward the bounty, it pays six pounds four in the price of its bread. And remember, the four shilling rise is a supposition. Smith says let us suppose. It isn't a measurement.

@math heading="Scaled: 1,000 exported, 31,000 at home" items="first tax: 5 · 1,000 = 5,000@first tax;;second tax: 4 · 31,000 = 124,000@second;;together: 129,000 · 129 a quarter exported@together"
The lab scales it up. A thousand quarters exported, thirty one thousand eaten at home, in shillings. The first tax is five thousand. The second, a hundred and twenty four thousand. Together, a hundred and twenty nine thousand, or a hundred and twenty nine shillings for every quarter exported.

@chapter Money price, real value
@quote text="The nature of things has stamped upon corn a real value, which cannot be altered by merely altering its money price." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 5"
But surely a higher price encourages growing corn? Not if it's only a money price. The money price of corn sets the money price of labour, and through it nearly every home good. The nature of things has stamped upon corn a real value, which cannot be altered by merely altering its money price.

@art dam-head
The real effect is to lower the value of silver at home. Smith's picture comes from Spain and Portugal. Dam up a stream, and once the dam is full, as much runs over as if there were no dam. The metal is just deeper, and cheaper, behind it.

@chapter The four trades in corn
@art ship-and-crew
The digression splits the corn trade into four: the inland dealer, the importer, the exporter and the carrier. The inland dealer's interest is exactly the same as the people's, even in scarcity. By raising his price as far as the scarcity requires, he makes them thrifty, like a prudent ship's master rationing his crew.

@art many-dealers
And dealers can't combine. Corn is divided among more owners than any other commodity, scattered everywhere. A dearth, Smith says, never arose from a combination of dealers, only from real scarcity. And a famine only from government trying to cure a dearth by improper means. But the exporter's interest may be opposed to the people's.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking the 4s rise as a measurement@The first|Treating the money price as the real price@The second|Hearing that dealers are good men@And the third"
The first slip is taking the four shilling rise as a measurement. The second is treating the money price of corn as its real price. And the third is hearing that dealers are good men. The argument is about interest and numbers. Thompson and Sen later challenged his account of dearths.
