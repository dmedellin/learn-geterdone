@chapter Not all production is work
@title text="The Time of Production" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 13" art=thumb
Grain ripens, wine ferments, pots dry, while the work stands still. And the capital waits through all of it.

@quote text="The working time is always the time of production, that is to say, the time during which capital is held in the sphere of production. But vice versa, not all time during which capital is engaged in the process of production is necessarily a working time." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 13"
Read that carefully. Every working hour is production time, but not the other way round.

@art drying-pots
Marx isn't talking about nights and Sundays, the pauses labour-power itself needs. He means pauses that belong to the product. Grape juice that must ferment, pottery that must dry, linen bleached on the grass. The object is left to natural processes, and the capital simply waits.

@art grain-season
Winter grain needs about nine months to mature, and between sowing and harvest, work is almost entirely suspended. So production time splits in two: a working period, and a period when the product is left to nature. Both lengthen the turnover.

@chapter Shortening the pause
@words text="6 to 18 months → 1½ to 2 months"
Where the pause isn't fixed by nature, it can be shortened. Chemical bleaching replaced the bleaching green, and drying machines replaced the open shed. Marx's sharpest example is leather. By the old method, tannic acid took six to eighteen months to soak into the skins. With the air pump, one and a half to two months.

@art tanning-pits
Nothing in that change saves the tanners an hour. The acid soaks in faster, the capital comes back sooner, and the work is what it was.

@art bessemer
The same story runs through steel, from puddling to Bessemer's process, with a sting in the tail. The time of production has been enormously shortened, Marx says, but the investment of fixed capital has increased accordingly.

@chapter The tannery in figures
@math heading="4 weeks of work, 4 of selling" items="old pits:  52 + 4 = 56 weeks;  52/56 = 13/14@old method;;air-pump:  8 + 4 = 12 weeks;  52/12 = 13/3@air pump"
The lab takes a tannery with four weeks of work and four of selling. By the old method, a year in the pits, the turnover is fifty six weeks: thirteen fourteenths of a turnover a year. With the air pump, eight weeks, the turnover is twelve weeks: thirteen thirds a year. The working time is four weeks in both.

@chapter Farming and the forest
@math heading="Winter grain" items="turnover:  39 + 13 = 52 weeks;  once a year@once a year;;working share:  13/39 = 1/3@a third"
Take winter grain at thirty nine weeks, thirteen of them worked, and thirteen weeks to sell. It turns over once a year, and only a third of the production time is work. However hard the farm works, it can't turn over more than once a year.

@art winter-workshop
Agriculture is where the gap is widest. In northern Russia, field work is possible on a hundred and thirty to a hundred and fifty days a year. The peasants filled winter with weaving, tanning and smithing at home. For the capital, Marx says, all the differences even out in the turnover. Not so for the labourer.

@art forest-century
At the far end is the forest. Timber at a hundred years, with six months of work in it, turns over once a century, and only one two hundredth of its production time is work. Marx judges forestry little suited to private enterprise.

@chapter The wine in the cellar
@words text="Does waiting add value?" sub="James Mill · McCulloch"
Marx calls it senseless to identify production time with working time, naming MacCulloch and James Mill. Their own standard case is wine that's worth more after years in a cellar, with no more work done.

@words text="Waiting adds no value" sub="the going rate: Volume III"
James Mill counted the year as more labour. McCulloch let nature's operations count as labour. On Marx's definitions, the waiting adds no value, and why waiting capital still earns the going rate is left to volume three.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Shorter production means less labour@The first|Unworked months cost nothing@The second|Natural processes are labour@And the third"
The first slip is thinking shorter production time always means less labour. The tannery saved none. The second is treating the unworked months as free. The capital is advanced for the whole period. And the third is counting natural processes as labour.
