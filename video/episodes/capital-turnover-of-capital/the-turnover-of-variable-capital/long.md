@chapter Two capitals
@title text="The Turnover of Variable Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 16" art=thumb
Here are two capitals that employ the same workers, for the same day, at the same rate of exploitation. One shows an annual rate of surplus value of a thousand per cent. The other, a hundred.

@art capital-a
Set up capital A as Marx does. Five hundred pounds of variable capital, on a five week turnover, four weeks of work and one of circulation. In a fifty week year, that's ten turnovers. Each week a hundred pounds of wages yields, at a hundred per cent, a hundred of surplus value.

@quote text="If 500 p. st. of variable capital produce a surplus-value of 500 p. st., then 5000 p. st. produce ten times 500, or 5000 p. st. of surplus-value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 16"
Over the year, five thousand of wages is paid from the same five hundred coming back ten times. Five thousand of surplus value, on five hundred advanced, is a thousand per cent.

@chapter The formula
@words text="S′ = s′ · n" sub="annual rate = real rate × turnovers"
Marx writes the general formula. The annual rate, S prime, is the real rate, small s prime, times the turnovers, n. A hundred per cent times ten. Only when n is one is the annual rate the real one.

@chapter Capital B
@art capital-b
Now capital B. It advances five thousand pounds for a whole fifty week year, and turns it over once. It too pays a hundred pounds a week, employs the same workers for the same day, and produces five thousand of surplus value.

@math heading="£100 a week of wages · 100% · 50 weeks" items="A:  n = 50/5 = 10;  S′ = 5,000 / 500 = 1,000%@capital A;;B:  n = 50/50 = 1;  S′ = 5,000 / 5,000 = 100%@capital B"
For capital A, ten turnovers: five thousand over five hundred, a thousand per cent. For capital B, one turnover: five thousand over five thousand, a hundred per cent. A difference of nine hundred per cent, Marx says, in the annual rate.

@art same-workers
Same workers, same day, same real rate, same surplus value. The annual rates differ because B must advance ten times the money to keep the same labour at work.

@chapter A puzzle with a history
@words text="It routed the Ricardian school" sub="from the 1820s"
Marx says this puzzle had a history. The appearance that surplus value depends on circulation as well as exploitation completely routed the Ricardian school from the eighteen twenties, disguised as the annual rate of profit. His answer is to separate the variable capital advanced from the variable capital employed.

@art ten-times-labour
Put B on A's footing. Let B spend its five thousand every five weeks, a thousand a week. Now it also turns over ten times, its annual rate is a thousand per cent, and it produces fifty thousand, ten times A's, because it employs ten times the labour. The real rate never moved.

@chapter Below one turnover
@art under-a-year
The rule runs below one turnover too. Marx's capital C needs fifty five weeks to turn over five thousand five hundred, at a hundred a week.

@math heading="Capital C: 55-week turnover" items="n = 50/55 = 10/11@ten elevenths;;S′ = 100% · 10/11 = 10/11@annual rate"
In a fifty week year it employs five thousand and produces five thousand of surplus value, on five thousand five hundred advanced. That's ten elevenths of a turnover, and an annual rate of ten elevenths, below the real rate.

@chapter Who pays whom
@art railway-drain
A's workers, from the second turnover on, are paid with the money form of what they made in the first. B's are paid from money their product won't replace until next year. Scale that up to a railway, which takes labour and materials out of the market for years and puts nothing back, and you get pressure on prices and money.

@words text="Social intelligence after the fact"
A communist society, Marx says, would reckon in advance how much it could spare. In capitalist society, where social intelligence acts only after the fact, great disturbances must occur.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Faster turnover, harder exploitation@The first|Dividing by capital employed@The second|The annual rate never falls below@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a faster capital exploits its workers harder. The annual rate measures how little money was advanced, not how hard anyone worked. The second is dividing by the variable capital employed. That gives back the real rate. And the third is expecting the annual rate never to fall below the real rate.
