@chapter A unit for capital
@title text="Turnover Time and the Number of Turnovers" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 7" art=thumb
You know the working day from volume one. Labour-power is bought by the day, and the day splits into necessary and surplus labour. Marx now wants the matching unit for capital, and he chooses the year.

@art harvest-year
His reason is agricultural. The main crops of the temperate zone, where capitalist production grew up, come once a year. Nothing in the arithmetic depends on that reason. The year is just a yardstick, and the rest of this part of the book measures against it.

@chapter Both times count
@art two-times
The chapter opens by recalling the last course. A capital's whole time of rotation is the sum of its production time and its circulation time. Production is the stretch in the workshop. Circulation is the stretch as goods waiting to sell, or money waiting to be laid out.

@words text="Away from its owner"
Both count. A capital whose goods sit in a warehouse is away from its owner just as surely as one whose goods are still on the loom.

@chapter Which circuit to measure
@words text="M…M′ · P…P · not C′…C′"
Which of the three circuits should be measured? The money circuit shows best what turnover does to surplus value, and the productive circuit what it does to the product. But the commodity circuit won't do, because it starts from the whole product, surplus value included, not from the value advanced.

@words text="Newman · Chalmers" sub="each took one circuit for the whole"
The economists, Marx says, never separated these. Newman, an American textbook writer, measured from the time outlays are made till returns are received. Chalmers, the Scottish preacher and economist, began the cycle when the receipts come in. Each took one circuit for the whole thing.

@art repeating-loops
Marx's own definition is one sentence. The rotation of capital, taken as a periodical process, not as an individual event, is its turnover. A single circuit is only a fragment in the life of capital. What matters is that it happens again, so it can be counted.

@chapter Counting turnovers
@words text="n = T / t" sub="T: the year · t: turnover time · n: turnovers"
Here's the notation. T is the year, t is the turnover time of one capital, and n is the number of turnovers it makes in the year. So n equals T over t.

@art year-ruler
Picture the year as a ruler, and the turnover as one hop along it. Count how many hops fit, and you have the number of turnovers.

@math heading="Marx's two cases" items="t = 3 months:  n = 12/3 = 4@three months;;t = 18 months:  n = 12/18 = 2/3@eighteen months"
A turnover time of three months gives twelve over three, four turnovers. Eighteen months gives twelve over eighteen, two thirds. That capital completes only two thirds of its turnover in one year.

@art long-turnover
A number below one isn't a mistake. It's a capital that's away for longer than a year.

@chapter Value turned over
@quote text="From the point of view of the capitalist, the time of turn-over is the time for which he must advance his capital in order to create value with it and have it returned in its original form." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 7"
That's the definition the rest of the course builds on.

@math heading="£400 advanced, 52-week year" items="t = 9 + 4 = 13 weeks@thirteen weeks;;n = 52 / 13 = 4@four times;;value turned over = 4 · £400 = £1,600@sixteen hundred;;capital advanced = £400@still four hundred"
The lab measures in weeks. Take Marx's three months as thirteen weeks, split for the example into nine of production and four of circulation. Four hundred pounds turns over four times, so sixteen hundred pounds of value passes through it. But the capital advanced is still four hundred.

@art same-coins
Because it's the same four hundred pounds going round four times. At no moment does the capitalist hold more than that in the business.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Twice the turnovers, twice the capital@The first|Timing by production alone@The second|Fewer than one is an error@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a capital that turns over twice is twice the capital. Turnovers multiply the value passing through, not the capital. The second is timing the turnover by production alone. Drop the four weeks of circulation and you'd get fifty two ninths instead of four. And the third is treating fewer than one turnover a year as an error.

@chapter Try it
@words text="10 weeks production · 3 circulation"
Your turn. Production takes ten weeks and circulation three, in a fifty two week year. How many turnovers does the capital make? And if it's three hundred pounds turning over six times, what's the capital advanced?
