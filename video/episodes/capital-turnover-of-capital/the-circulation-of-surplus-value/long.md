@chapter The puzzle
@title text="The Circulation of Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 17" art=thumb
Capital A sells every five weeks, and its owner can pay his household out of surplus value as it comes in. Capital B sells once a year, so its owner must live a year on funds from somewhere else. Either way, before surplus value can be spent, it has to become money.

@quote text="How can the entire capitalist class manage to draw continually 600 p. st. out of the circulation, when they continually throw only 500 p. st. into it?" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 17"
Let five hundred pounds stand for the whole capital of society, and a hundred for its surplus value. That's the question.

@art five-in-six-out
Notice what isn't in question. Surplus value is produced in the workshop. Marx says the question isn't where surplus value comes from. It's where the money comes from to exchange it. An opponent once put this to Thomas Tooke, and Marx says neither Tooke nor anyone else had answered it.

@chapter Four evasions
@art same-coins-twice
He disposes of four evasions first. That the same coins serve many purchases, which assumes the money it's meant to explain. That one capitalist's spending sells another's product, which only asks where the spender got it. That wages flow back. And that money for fixed capital stays out for years. None supplies the money it presumes.

@quote text="From nothing comes nothing. The capitalist class as a whole cannot draw out of circulation what was not previously in it." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 17"
In capitalist society the capitalist is the starting point of the money in circulation, wages included. So the question is real.

@chapter The answer
@quote text="But, mark well, it is not thrown into circulation as advanced money, not as capital. The capitalist class spends it for their individual consumption." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 17"
Paradoxical as it seems, the capitalists themselves throw in the money that realises their surplus value. But not as capital.

@chapter Marx's farmer
@art farmer-household
Marx's farmer makes it concrete. In his first year he advances five thousand pounds, four thousand for means of production and a thousand for labour-power. At a hundred per cent, the harvest is worth six thousand. He gets nothing back until the year's end, so meanwhile he spends a thousand from his own pocket on living.

@math heading="The farmer's first year" items="thrown in as capital:  £5,000@five thousand;;thrown in as spending:  £1,000@a thousand;;returned:  £6,000@six thousand;;s realised:  £6,000 − £5,000 = £1,000@surplus value realised"
At the year's end he sells for six thousand. Five thousand replaces his capital, and a thousand is his surplus value realised in money: money he threw in himself, not as a capitalist, but as a consumer.

@art harvest-sale
From the second year on, what he spends is last year's surplus value, and it flows back again at the end of each year.

@chapter Where the money came from
@art gold-mine
And where did the money come from in the first place? Marx's simplest case is a country that mines its own gold. The gold producers throw their wages, costs and surplus value straight into circulation as new money, replacing the coin worn out each year.

@chapter Spending less
@art short-of-money
Now let the class spend only fifty, not a hundred.

@math heading="£500 capital · product worth £600" items="spend £100:  £600 returns;  £100 realised@a hundred;;spend £50:  £550 returns;  £50 realised@only fifty"
Spending a hundred, all six hundred returns, and the whole hundred of surplus value is realised. Spending only fifty, there's only five hundred and fifty of money to buy a product worth six hundred, so only fifty of the surplus value comes back as money.

@chapter What about accumulation?
@words text="Who buys the growing product?" sub="Rosa Luxemburg, 1913"
For accumulation, Marx says, there's no new problem: the realised surplus value is thrown in as capital instead of spent. Rosa Luxemburg thought that was exactly where the problem was. If capitalists invest instead of consume, who buys the growing product? Her answer was buyers outside capitalist production. Her critics, Bukharin among them, said capitalists buy the extra means of production from one another.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Someone outside must supply the money@The first|Source of s = source of its money@The second|Consumption money is an advance@And the third"
The first slip is thinking someone outside the capitalist class must supply the money. The second is confusing where surplus value comes from with where its money comes from. And the third is calling the consumption money an advance of capital. It's spent, and the goods are consumed.

@chapter Try it
@words text="£800 capital · product £1,000 · spends £200"
Your turn. A capitalist class advances eight hundred as capital, produces commodities worth a thousand, and spends two hundred on itself. How much surplus value is realised? Then let it spend only a hundred and twenty.
