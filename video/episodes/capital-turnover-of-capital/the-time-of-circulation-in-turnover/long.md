@chapter Selling and buying time
@title text="The Time of Circulation in the Turnover" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 14" art=thumb
Circulation time is the time of selling and the time of buying. And its largest part is often just distance.

@words text="Selling time: relatively the most decisive"
Marx calls the time of selling, when capital has the form of commodities, relatively the most decisive part of circulation time. It varies between capitalists in one branch, and between good years and bad. But distance is the steady cause.

@chapter Shipped to India
@art india-voyage
Here's his example. Some commodities are shipped to India, which takes, say, four months. Assume the selling time is zero: they're made to order and paid for on delivery to the producer's agent. The money takes another four months to come back.

@quote text="Thus it takes eight months, before the same capital can again serve as productive capital and renew the same operations." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 14"
Notice how much Marx gives away. No waiting in a warehouse at all. And still the capital is gone for eight months, four as cloth on a ship and four as money coming home. During the voyage, as Marx puts it, capital is fettered in the form of commodity capital.

@chapter The same factory, three markets
@art three-markets
The lab keeps a production period of four months, fifty two thirds of a week, and changes only the market.

@math heading="Production: 4 months = 52/3 weeks" items="near:  52/3 + 2 months = 26 weeks → 2 a year@a month away;;India:  52/3 + 104/3 = 52 weeks → 1 a year@eight months;;faster passage:  104/3 weeks → 3/2 a year@halve each leg"
A market a month away, two months there and back, gives a twenty six week turnover, twice a year. Marx's India, eight months there and back, gives fifty two weeks, once a year. Halve each leg of the voyage, and the turnover falls to a hundred and four thirds weeks, three halves a year.

@words text="Circulation share in India: 2/3"
In the India case, two thirds of the turnover is spent in circulation. And nothing in the factory changed in any of the three. Distance alone halved its turnovers.

@chapter Distance in time
@art rail-bypass
Better ships and railways shorten journeys absolutely, but they change relative distances unevenly. A railway from a factory to an inland city can make a nearer town, off the line, relatively further away. Towns on the new lines grew. Towns left on a quiet branch declined.

@art frequent-sailings
Frequency matters as well as speed. When ships leave Liverpool for New York on successive days, goods needn't pile up waiting for one sailing, and money flows back in a steady stream. But cheaper transport also means more production for the world market, so more of society's capital is always on its way somewhere.

@words text="Six- and ten-month bills" sub="a material basis of terms of credit"
Marx's India example wasn't idle. Trade with the East ran on bills of exchange of six or ten months, and he calls these differences in turnover one of the material bases of the various terms of credit.

@words text="1866: four-month bills tried, and dropped"
The banks tried in eighteen sixty six to cut the Indian bills to four months, and had to give up. A bracketed note adds that the Suez canal has since revolutionised all of it.

@chapter Buying time
@art wool-auction
Buying time is the other half. A wool spinner in London had to buy at the great auctions held every three months, so had to keep money for three months' wool. A cotton spinner could buy more evenly.

@art money-waits
Either way, part of the capital always waits as money. Marx closes on the point he'll build on next: it's the portion always available as money capital that economists forget.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Only the merchant's time is wasted@The first|Measuring distance in miles@The second|Forgetting the journey back@And the third"
The first slip is thinking circulation time is wasted only for the merchant. It's the producer's own capital that's tied up. The second is measuring distance in miles, when what lengthens a turnover is time. And the third is forgetting the journey back. The money must come home before it can buy labour-power again.
