@chapter The problem
@title text="Turnover Time and the Capital Advanced" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 15" art=thumb
Nine weeks of work at a hundred pounds a week make nine hundred pounds of product. Then it takes three weeks to sell. What happens in those three weeks?

@art idle-weeks
The turnover is twelve weeks. At the end of nine, the capital is all product, waiting to be sold. The new production can't start until the thirteenth week. So the factory would stand still three weeks in every twelve, a quarter of the year.

@words text="Shrink: £900 over 12 weeks = £75 a week"
Marx sees two ways out. The first is to shrink. Spread the nine hundred over twelve weeks, at seventy five a week, so work never stops. But that's a smaller business in every respect, and competition usually sets a minimum scale.

@chapter A second capital
@art two-capitals
The second way is more capital. While the first nine hundred is out being sold, a further three hundred keeps the work going.

@quote text="During the period of turn-over of twelve weeks, 1,200 pounds sterling are successively invested in twelve weeks, and 300 is one quarter of this sum as three weeks is of twelve." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 15"
Capital two stands to the whole as circulation time stands to the turnover.

@math heading="£100 a week · 9 weeks work · 3 to sell" items="capital I = 9 · £100 = £900@capital one;;capital II = 3 · £100 = £300@capital two;;advanced = £900 + £300 = £1,200@twelve hundred;;II share = 300 / 1,200 = 1/4@a quarter"
Capital one, for the working period, is nine times a hundred, nine hundred. Capital two, for circulation, is three times a hundred, three hundred. Together, twelve hundred advanced, and capital two is a quarter of it.

@chapter Capital set free
@art crossing-capitals
Then the two capitals start to cross. In the thirteenth week, the first nine hundred comes back. But the second working period, opened by the three hundred in week ten, already has three of its nine weeks done. It needs only six hundred more.

@art set-free
So three hundred of the original nine hundred is set free. From then on, at the close of each working period, three hundred pounds lies free.

@chapter Marx's rule
@list heading="When is capital set free?" items="Equal periods: nothing@equal|Circulation a whole multiple of work: nothing@whole multiple|Work longer: all of capital II@longer|Otherwise: the leftover excess@Otherwise"
Marx works case after case, and draws a rule. When the working and circulation periods are equal, nothing is set free. The same when circulation is a whole multiple of the working period. If the working period is longer, the whole of capital two is set free. Otherwise, it's the capital covering the excess of circulation over the largest multiple of working periods.

@art equal-relay
So five weeks against five gives five hundred and five hundred, and nothing set free: the capitals relieve each other exactly. Four weeks of work against five of circulation gives four hundred and five hundred, with a hundred set free each time.

@words text="Another part is always in circulation"
Behind the tables is the point Marx thinks economists miss. While one part is busy in production, another must always be in circulation. Say a thousand pounds turned over five times, and the time of circulation vanishes from memory.

@chapter When conditions change
@art money-market
The last section changes one thing at a time. If circulation shrinks from three weeks to two, a business with six weeks of work needs eight hundred, not nine. A hundred is thrown on the money market, though nobody saved it.

@words text="Prices halve: £900 → £450"
And if the prices of materials and labour-power halve, the nine week turnover needs four hundred and fifty instead of nine hundred. Marx uses these to show how loanable money can pile up or run short with no change in the quantity of money.

@words text="Engels: Marx entangled himself" sub="in his computation of turnovers"
Engels, who edited this chapter, says Marx entangled himself in these computations, and kept only the simplest, correct figures. He also doubts the release matters as much as Marx thought. Both agree that a considerable part of industrial capital must always be held as money.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="One working period's capital is enough@The first|Capital II is needed only once@The second|Capital is always set free@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a capital needs only enough for one working period. Then it stops while its product sells. The second is thinking capital two is needed only once. It's part of the capital for as long as the business runs. And the third is expecting capital to be set free in every case.
