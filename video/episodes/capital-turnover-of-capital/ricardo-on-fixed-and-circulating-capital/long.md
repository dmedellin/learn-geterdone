@chapter Ricardo's exception
@title text="Ricardo on Fixed and Circulating Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 11" art=thumb
David Ricardo was a stockbroker who made a fortune in government loans, retired to the country, and wrote the most rigorous economics of his day, in eighteen seventeen.

@art prices-tilt
His first chapter argues that commodities exchange in proportion to the labour they contain, then lists exceptions. Two goods made with the same labour can have different prices if one uses long lasting machines and the other mostly wages. When wages rise, the second costs more, and the first hardly changes.

@quote text="Ricardo mentions the distinction between fixed and circulating capital merely for the purpose of illustrating the exceptions to the law of value, namely, in cases where the rate of wages affects the prices." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 11"
That's why fixed and circulating capital enter his book at all.

@chapter Two differences, run together
@words text="Supports labour · tools, machinery, buildings"
Ricardo's two sorts are the capital that supports labour, and the capital in tools, machinery and buildings. Marx's first objection: that puts two different differences side by side as if they were one.

@list heading="Two different questions" items="Durability: how fast value returns@Durability decides|Constant to variable: how much surplus value@The proportion"
Durability decides how fast a machine returns its value. The proportion of constant to variable capital decides something else entirely: how much surplus value is produced.

@art missing-cotton
His second objection: where did the cotton go? Raw material isn't a durable instrument, so it can't be fixed. And it doesn't support labour, so it can't be circulating either. Ricardo has too much logical instinct not to feel this, Marx says, so that part of capital disappears entirely for him.

@chapter Durability as the test
@art durability-scale
Ricardo's own definition makes the split a matter of how quickly capital perishes. He himself calls it a division not essential, where the line can't be drawn accurately. Marx agrees, for Ricardo's version. A steam engine, a ship, the worker's clothes and food all lie on one scale, and buying a machine and buying labour-power differ only in degree.

@quote text="The wheat bought by a farmer to sow is comparatively a fixed capital to the wheat purchased by a baker to make into loaves." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 11"
Ricardo's own example makes the point for Marx.

@art farmer-and-baker
The farmer waits a year for his return, the baker a week. But the seed passes its whole value into the harvest, just as the baker's wheat passes its whole value into the bread. Both are circulating capital.

@art year-and-week
Speed of return is turnover time, which the next lessons measure directly. It isn't fixity.

@chapter Two capitals, side by side
@art twin-capitals
The lab sets two capitals side by side. Both are a thousand pounds, both a quarter fixed. A spends five hundred on materials and two hundred and fifty on wages. B spends two hundred and fifty on materials and five hundred on wages.

@math heading="Rate of surplus value 100%" items="A and B:  fixed £250, circulating £750@identical;;A:  s = 100% · £250 = £250@A produces;;B:  s = 100% · £500 = £500@B five hundred"
Ricardo's split is identical: two hundred and fifty fixed, seven hundred and fifty circulating, for each. But at a hundred per cent, A produces two hundred and fifty of surplus value, and B five hundred. The fixed share can't tell them apart.

@words text="Different fixed shares, same £250 of wages → same s"
Reverse the test. A capital that's mostly machine and one that's mostly material, with the same two hundred and fifty in wages, split very differently and produce the same two hundred and fifty.

@chapter Where Ricardo is right
@words text="Prices: durability and composition both matter" sub="Sraffa, 1960"
Ricardo's position has a strong modern form. In a theory of prices, how long capital is tied up really does decide how prices respond to wages, and Piero Sraffa rebuilt exactly that argument. Marx doesn't deny it. He leaves it for volume three. His claim is narrower: for producing surplus value, only the variable capital counts.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The fixed split explains profit@The first|Slow return means fixed@The second|Durability never matters@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the fixed and circulating split explains where profit comes from. The second is taking speed of return for fixity, like the farmer's seed. And the third is concluding that durability doesn't matter at all. For prices, Marx says, it does.
