@chapter Two speeds
@title text="The Aggregate Turnover of Advanced Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 9" art=thumb
The parts of a capital turn over at different speeds. Cotton and labour-power come back with every sale. A machine goes on working for years. So how fast does the whole capital turn over?

@art two-speeds
Here's the problem. The circulating capital is renewed in its natural form every turnover: new cotton, new labour-power. The machine isn't. Seen as production to production, the two parts just don't move alike.

@art into-money
But see them as money, and they do. Each year some of the machine's value comes back as money, just as the cotton's does. So Marx fixes every element in the mould of money. Once everything is money, an average is a sum and a division.

@chapter Marx's figures
@quote text="Take it that the fixed capital is 80,000 pounds sterling, its period of reproduction 10 years, so that 8,000 pounds of this capital annually return to their money-form, or complete one-tenth of its turn-over." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 9"
The circulating capital is twenty thousand pounds, turned over five times a year. So the whole capital is a hundred thousand.

@math heading="£80,000 fixed · £20,000 circulating" items="wear = £80,000 / 10 = £8,000@eight thousand;;circulating = 5 · £20,000 = £100,000@five times;;turned over = £8,000 + £100,000 = £108,000@a hundred and eight;;whole = 108,000 / 100,000 = 27/25@twenty seven"
The fixed capital returns eight thousand a year by wear. The circulating twenty thousand turns over five times, which is a hundred thousand. Together that's a hundred and eight thousand turned over, on a hundred thousand advanced. So the whole capital turns over twenty seven twenty fifths times a year, once in about eleven months.

@art big-and-small
Notice the speed. The circulating part goes round five times, but the whole capital not much more than once. Four fifths of it is in a machine that returns a tenth of its value a year.

@chapter Capital advanced stays put
@words text="£4,000 × 5 = £20,000 of value" sub="what's advanced is still £4,000"
The old rule still holds. Four thousand pounds turned over five times is twenty thousand of value. But what returns and is advanced again is the original four thousand. Its size isn't changed by how often it goes round.

@art machine-after-year
At the end of Marx's first year, the capitalist has twenty thousand back for the circulating capital, and eight thousand set free from the machine. The machine's still at work, now worth seventy two thousand.

@chapter The cycle of turnovers
@art ten-year-cycle
Nine more years, and the machine is used up and replaced. That ten year span is a cycle of turnovers, set by the fixed capital, however fast the circulating part goes round.

@quote text="So much at least is evident that this cycle comprising a number of years, through which capital is compelled to pass by its fixed part, furnishes a material basis for the periodical commercial crises in which business goes through successive periods of lassitude, average activity, overspeeding, and crisis." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 9"
That's a large claim, and he hedges it in the same paragraph.

@art crisis-wave
It's not a question of any one definite number, he says. A crisis is the moment a lot of new fixed capital is bought at once, so the next cycle starts together. Whether replacement really paces the trade cycle is a question for the data, and the lesson computes none of it.

@chapter Scrope did it first
@math heading="Scrope's $50,000" items="¼ renewed every 10 years:  $1,250@every ten years;;¼ every year:  $12,500@every year;;½ twice a year:  $50,000@twice a year;;turned over:  $63,750@sixty three thousand"
Marx ends with a writer who did this sum before him, Scrope. His capital is fifty thousand dollars. A quarter is renewed every ten years, a quarter every year, and half twice a year. That's twelve hundred and fifty, plus twelve thousand five hundred, plus fifty thousand: sixty three thousand seven hundred and fifty turned over. It's Marx's method exactly.

@words text="Terms of payment ≠ turnover"
Marx's quarrel with Scrope is elsewhere. Scrope says wages turn over weekly because sales come in weekly. Marx answers that terms of payment and credit are one thing, and the turnover that follows from the nature of capital is another.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Circulating turnover = the capital's@The first|Adding the whole machine@The second|Value turned over as capital@And the third"
The first slip is taking the circulating part's turnover for the whole capital's. Five isn't twenty seven twenty fifths. The second is adding the whole fixed capital to the year's turnover. Only the wear comes back, and adding the machine would give a hundred and eighty thousand. And the third is reading value turned over as capital.

@chapter Try it
@words text="£30,000 fixed, 10 years · £10,000 circulating, 4 a year"
Your turn. Thirty thousand pounds of fixed capital on a ten year life, and ten thousand circulating, turned over four times a year. What value turns over in the year, and how many times does the whole capital turn over?
