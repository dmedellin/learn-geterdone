@chapter Value in two places
@title text="Fixed Capital and Circulating Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 8" art=thumb
Think of a spinning machine. Every week yarn leaves the mill and is sold, and a little of the machine's value goes with it, as wear. The machine itself never leaves.

@art machine-drains
So its value is in two places. One part is still in the machine. The other has already gone back into money with the yarn.

@quote text="During the entire period of its service, a portion of its value always remains fixed in it, independent of the commodities which it helps to produce." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 8"
That's where the name comes from. Marx's machine lasts ten years. Its value comes back piece by piece, building up as a reserve fund of money, and only then is that money turned back into a new machine. A turnover of ten years.

@chapter What makes it fixed
@art ox-two-ways
What makes something fixed capital is only the way its value circulates, and that follows from what it does in production. The same object can be either.

@quote text="An ox is fixed capital, so long as it is a beast of toil. If it is eaten, it does not perform the functions of an instrument of production, and is, therefore, not fixed capital." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 8"
And immobility doesn't decide it.

@art building-and-loco
A factory building and a locomotive are both fixed capital. And a machine sitting in its maker's warehouse isn't fixed capital at all. It's a commodity for sale.

@chapter Circulating capital
@art coal-and-cotton
Everything else in productive capital is circulating capital: the cotton, the coal and the wages. As constant capital, cotton only transfers its value, while labour-power creates new value. But in how their value comes back, wholly with each sale, the two are alike.

@list heading="Two different cuts" items="Cotton: circulating, constant@Cotton is|Wages: circulating, variable@wages are|Machine: fixed, constant@a machine is|Nothing is fixed and variable@Nothing"
So the same capital can be cut two ways, and Marx warns against confusing them. Fixed against circulating is about how value returns. Constant against variable is about whether new value is created. Cotton is circulating and constant. Wages are circulating and variable. A machine is fixed and constant. Nothing is fixed and variable.

@chapter Wear, and virtual wear
@art worn-rails
The railways were the great fixed capital of Marx's day. Engineers expected rails to last a century. They lasted about twenty years, ties twelve to fifteen, a locomotive ten to twelve.

@words text="£40,000 → £30,000 ten years later" sub="Lardner on cars and locomotives"
There's a second kind of wear with nothing to do with use. Lardner reported cars and locomotives costing forty thousand pounds could be bought ten years later for thirty thousand. Marx calls it virtual wear. Competition, he adds, forces in new machinery before the old is worn out.

@chapter The arithmetic
@math heading="£10,000 machine, 10-year life" items="wear a year = £10,000 / 10 = £1,000@a thousand;;after 4 years:  £10,000 · 6/10 = £6,000 left@six thousand"
Marx's ten thousand pound machine on a ten year life transfers a thousand pounds a year. After four years, six tenths of its value, six thousand pounds, is still locked in it.

@math heading="Repairs folded into wear" items="15-year life:  £2,000/3 a year@two thousand over three;;booked at 10 years:  £1,000 a year@booked;;repairs:  £1,000/3 a year@one thousand over three"
Repairs are a separate, extra outlay. Many businesses fold them into the wear. Marx's machine lasting fifteen years wears two thousand over three pounds a year, but is booked as if it lasted ten, at a thousand. The extra, one thousand over three, pays for the repairs that let it last fifteen.

@art cheaper-twin
Now suppose a machine of the same kind appears at half the price, in the fourth year. The old one is now worth what the new one would be at the same age. It passes on five hundred a year, not a thousand. Three thousand is left in it, and another three thousand has vanished without any wear at all.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Fixed means constant@The first|Durability or immobility is the test@The second|Repairs are part of the original advance@And the third"
The first slip is the one Marx calls fundamental: treating fixed and circulating as the same as constant and variable. The second is making durability the test. Seed sits in the ground a year and is circulating. A locomotive moves all day and is fixed. And the third is counting repairs as part of the original advance.
