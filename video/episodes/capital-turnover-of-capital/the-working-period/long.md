@chapter One long act
@title text="The Working Period" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 12" art=thumb
Some products are finished every day. A locomotive needs three months of connected work before it can be sold. That difference turns out to need more capital, not just more patience.

@art yarn-vs-loco
Take Marx's two branches, a cotton spinnery and a locomotive factory, with equal ten hour days. The spinner's yarn is finished every day, and can be sold every week. The locomotive isn't finished until three months of days are joined together.

@quote text="I call such a working day, which is formed by the succession of more or less numerous connected working days, a working period." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 12"
For the workers, each day is a separate day. For the product, they're one long act. And this has nothing to do with fixed and circulating capital. It'd be there if both factories had exactly the same proportions of each.

@art long-products
The variety is enormous. An ordinary carpet in a week, a locomotive in three months, an ironclad in a year or more. Grain in nearly a year, cattle in several, timber in twelve to a hundred years.

@chapter The arithmetic
@art weekly-stacks
Let both factories lay out the same sum every week, and be paid on delivery. At the end of the week the spinner sells and starts again with the same money. The locomotive builder must find new wages and materials every week, for twelve weeks, before anything comes back.

@quote text="All other circumstances being assumed as equal, the one must have twelve times more circulating capital at his disposal than the other." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 12"
The investment of one is for one week. The other's is one week's investment, multiplied by twelve.

@math heading="£100 a week of wages and materials" items="spinnery:  £100 · 1 = £100@the spinner;;locomotive:  £100 · 12 = £1,200@the locomotive;;ironclad:  £100 · 52 = £5,200@fifty two weeks"
The lab puts it in figures at a hundred pounds a week. For the spinner, one week ties up a hundred. For the locomotive, twelve weeks tie up twelve hundred. And an ironclad's year of fifty two weeks ties up five thousand two hundred. Only the length of the connected act differs.

@chapter Fixed capital is different
@art engine-stays
Fixed capital behaves differently. The steam engine passes its wear to the yarn weekly or to the locomotive quarterly, but it's the same engine either way, and needn't be replaced for twenty years.

@art half-built-ship
Circulating capital is the problem. The labour-power bought this week is used up this week, must be paid this week, and sits in an unfinished product that can't circulate. A crisis that halts a ship or a house half built wastes all of it.

@chapter Long periods came late
@words text="20 to 50 times his resources" sub="a London builder, 1857"
That's why long working periods came late to capitalist hands. Roads and canals were built by states and towns. Houses were paid for in instalments. But in eighteen fifty seven, a London contractor told Parliament he now built a hundred to two hundred houses on speculation, an enterprise twenty to fifty times his resources, the rest borrowed on mortgage.

@chapter Shortening it
@art many-gangs
What shortens a working period? Machinery, co-operation and the concentration of capital, almost always by advancing more capital for a shorter time. A railway is finished sooner with huge armies of labourers working in many places at once.

@words text="Sheep in 2 years, not 4 or 5" sub="Bakewell, Dishley Grange"
Where nature sets the period, it's harder. Robert Bakewell bred his sheep to be ready in two years instead of four or five. Marx reports that he reduced the skeleton of sheep to the minimum required for their existence.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="It's only a question of patience@The first|Adding the fixed capital weekly@The second|Working period = working day@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a long working period is only a question of patience. It's a question of capital. The second is adding the fixed capital to the weekly outlay. Only wages and materials have to be found again each week. And the third is confusing the working period with the working day, which is the same ten hours in both factories.
