@chapter Quesnay's line
@title text="The Physiocrats and Adam Smith on Fixed Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Fixed and circulating capital didn't start with Marx. This chapter traces the terms back, and follows what went wrong when they were borrowed.

@art farm-advances
Begin with Quesnay, physician to the court of Louis the fifteenth and leader of the Physiocrats. In his Tableau of seventeen fifty eight, he split the farmer's capital in two. Annual advances, like seed and wages, which the harvest must replace in full. And primitive advances, implements and cattle, which wear out over ten years or so.

@words text="A line inside productive capital"
Marx thinks that was right. The line runs through productive capital, and it's drawn by how each part enters the value of the product.

@chapter Smith generalises
@quote text="Thus Adam Smith transforms the annual advances into circulating capital, and the primitive advances into fixed capital. But his progress is confined to this generalization of the categories. His analyses are far inferior to those of Quesnay." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 10"
Adam Smith took the distinction over in seventeen seventy six and made it general, for every capital, not just the farmer's.

@art changes-masters
Read Smith's definition closely and the trouble shows. Fixed capital yields a profit without changing masters. So circulating capital is capital that does change masters, and his example is the merchant, whose capital keeps going from him in one shape and coming back in another.

@words text="Capital of circulation ≠ circulating capital"
That, Marx says, isn't circulating capital. It's capital of circulation: goods being sold and money being spent, forms every capital passes through, machine value included.

@chapter Two tests
@art warehouse-machine
Two tests show the confusion. A machine in its maker's warehouse changes masters when it's sold, so by Smith's words it's circulating capital, though it's the very type of a fixed one.

@art copper-mine
And a copper mine uses no raw material at all, only labour, coal and machinery, none of which changes masters. According to Smith's own definition, Marx says, the whole capital of a mine would be fixed.

@quote text="Hence capital is not fixed capital, because it is fixed in the means of production, but because a part of the value invested in means of production remains fixed in them, while another part circulates as a part of the value of the product." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 10"
Marx's own test is the one from the last lessons, stated against Smith's.

@chapter Wages beside cotton
@art bread-not-labour
The damage Marx cares most about comes next. Smith lists wages with materials as circulating capital, and treats them as the workers' maintenance, their food and clothing, as if wages were fodder.

@words text="Labour-power creates more value than it costs"
Sorted that way, the capital laid out on labour-power sits beside the cotton, and its one peculiarity disappears from view: it buys something that creates more value than it costs. That mistake led on to the wage fund doctrine, which John Stuart Mill taught and gave up in eighteen sixty nine.

@chapter One capital, sorted twice
@art two-sorts
Smith's case has real strength. For a manager reading the books, wages and cotton really do come back together. Marx grants that. His complaint is that a classification good for timing returns was then used to explain profit.

@math heading="Mill: £500 machine, £100 cotton, £100 wages" items="Smith: fixed £500;  circulating £200@Smith's split;;Marx: constant £600;  variable £100@Marx's;;s = 100% · £100 = £100@at a hundred per cent"
Take a mill with a five hundred pound machine, a hundred of cotton and a hundred of wages. Smith's split is five hundred fixed and two hundred circulating. Marx's is six hundred constant and a hundred variable. And at a hundred per cent, only the hundred of wages yields surplus value.

@words text="Farm and mine: £600 fixed, £400 circulating"
Now a farm and a mine, both sorted by how their value returns, Marx's test. Both split six hundred fixed and four hundred circulating.

@math heading="Same split, different surplus" items="farm:  £200 seed + £200 wages@fodder and seed;;mine:  £150 coal + £250 wages@of coal;;s:  £200 against £250@two hundred against"
But the farm has two hundred of fodder and seed and two hundred of wages. The mine has a hundred and fifty of coal and two hundred and fifty of wages. Same fixed and circulating, different surplus value: two hundred against two hundred and fifty.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Wages and materials are one kind@The first|Circulating means changes hands@The second|Provisions are the capital@And the third"
The first slip is thinking wages and raw materials are the same kind of capital because both circulate. The second is defining circulating capital as capital that changes hands. And the third is counting the workers' provisions as the capital, when the capitalist buys labour-power, not the bread.
