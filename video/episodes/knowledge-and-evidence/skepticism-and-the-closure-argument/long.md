@chapter The skeptic's argument
@title text="Skepticism and the Closure Argument" sub="Knowledge and Evidence" art=thumb
Do you know you have hands? It seems obvious. But there's a short, valid argument that says you don't. And the interesting thing is how little logic can do to stop it.

@math heading="Two letters" items="h: I know I have hands@h stand for;;b: I know I am not a brain in a vat@b stand for"
Let h stand for, I know I have hands. And let b stand for, I know I'm not a brain in a vat being fed experiences.

@math heading="The skeptic" items="h → b@if I know I have hands;;¬b@I don't know;;∴ ¬h@So I don't know I have hands"
The skeptic argues in three lines. First, if I know I have hands, I know I'm not envatted. Second, I don't know I'm not envatted. So I don't know I have hands. That's modus tollens, and it's valid.

@art vat-experience
The second premise is the skeptic's claim that nothing in your experience could rule the vat out, since the vat is built to make your experience exactly what it is. And the first premise is the closure principle, applied to a case.

@art closure-bridge
Closure says that if you know something, and you know it brings another claim with it, you know the other claim too. Here: if you know you have hands, you know you're not a handless brain in a vat. It looks like plain common sense. That's why both sides use it.

@chapter Moore reads it backwards
@math heading="Moore" items="h@I know I have hands;;h → b@if I know that;;∴ b@so I know"
Moore answered by reading the argument backwards. He was more certain that he had hands than he could be of any philosophical premise. So: I know I have hands. If I know that, I know I'm not envatted. So I know I'm not envatted. That's modus ponens, also valid.

@art moore-hands
Notice what Moore didn't do. He didn't find a flaw in the skeptic's logic. There isn't one. He took the same conditional, and started from the claim he trusted most, that here is a hand. The logic ran just as smoothly the other way.

@art shared-conditional
Put the two side by side. They share a conditional, and a disagreement. The skeptic is more sure that b is false than that h is true. Moore is more sure of h. And the three sentences, the conditional, not b, and h, can't all be true together. One has to go.

@chapter The third option
@art zebra-mule
Dretske and others keep both not b and h, and give up the conditional. You can know there's a zebra in the pen without knowing it isn't a cleverly painted mule. So the third option is to deny closure.

@math heading="¬b ∴ ¬h" items="counterexample: h = T, b = F@h true and b false"
Without the conditional, the argument from not b to not h is invalid, and its counterexample row is h true and b false. That row is the denier's position written as an assignment: knows hands, doesn't know about the vat.

@chapter What each choice costs
@art three-tiles-ring
So there are three sentences that can't all stand, and three people, each keeping two and dropping one. The skeptic drops h. Moore drops not b. The denier drops the conditional. Each choice works, logically, and each has a price.

@list heading="The price of each" items="Skeptic: nobody knows much about the external world@The skeptic|Moore: claims to know without settling evidence@Moore|Denier: I know I have hands but not that I'm no handless brain@the denier"
Say plainly what each choice costs. The skeptic accepts that nobody knows much of anything about the external world. Moore accepts that a philosopher can claim to know the vat isn't there without any evidence that settles it. And the denier accepts a sentence that sounds absurd aloud: I know I have hands, but I don't know I'm not a handless brain in a vat.

@words text="Validity can't choose."
The logic says none of these is inconsistent, and nothing more. Both arguments are valid. Choosing between them takes a judgement about which premise is more certain, and no truth table supplies that.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a valid argument compels its conclusion@The first|Hearing Moore's reply as a refutation@The second|Treating closure as obviously true@the third"
The first slip is thinking a valid argument compels you to accept its conclusion. If you're more sure of h than of not b, rejecting not b isn't a mistake of logic. The second is hearing Moore's reply as a refutation. The skeptic can accuse it of begging the question. And the third is treating closure as obviously true. It's a principle about knowledge, not a theorem of logic.

@words text="Which premise do you hold most firmly?"
Here's one to try. Write out the three arguments, the skeptic's, Moore's, and the denier's. Then name the premise you hold most firmly, and say in words what giving up each of the others would cost you.
