@chapter Right isn't enough
@title text="Belief, Truth and Justification" sub="Knowledge and Evidence" art=thumb
Believing something isn't yet knowing it. And being right isn't knowing it either. So what's missing?

@art coin-flip
Here's a case. Someone believes it'll rain tomorrow, and they're right. But they only believe it because they flipped a coin to decide what to believe. They don't know it'll rain. The oldest serious answer to what's missing is that the belief must also be justified.

@chapter Three conditions
@math heading="The tripartite analysis" items="knows  ⟺  J ∧ T ∧ B@knows that p exactly when;;T: p is true@p is true;;B: the person believes p@believes p;;J: the belief is justified@is justified"
This is the traditional analysis. A person knows that p exactly when three conditions hold. P is true. The person believes p. And the belief is justified.

@art necessary-sufficient
That definition packs in two claims, and they fail differently. Each condition is necessary: nobody who lacks one of the three knows. And the three together are sufficient: anybody who has all three knows.

@list heading="Two ways to fail" items="Too narrow: shuts out a case of knowledge@Too narrow|Too broad: lets in a case that isn't knowledge@too broad"
So a definition can be too narrow, when some case lacks a condition and is knowledge all the same. Or it can be too broad, when some case has every condition and still isn't knowledge. This lesson tests the first claim. The next one breaks the second.

@chapter Four cases
@art cup-on-table
To test a definition, you need cases whose verdict you're confident of. Start from the plainest one there is. You look at the table, you see a cup on it, you believe the cup is there, and it is. If anything is knowledge, that is. Every other case is a variation on it, with one thing taken away.

@math heading="J  T  B → knows?" items="ordinary perception:  1 1 1 → yes@Ordinary perception;;lucky guess:  0 1 1 → no@The lucky guess;;confident error:  1 0 1 → no@the confident error;;unbelieved truth:  1 1 0 → no@the unbelieved truth"
Here are four cases. Ordinary perception: you see the cup, you believe it's there, and it's there. Knowledge. The lucky guess lacks justification. The confident error lacks truth. And the unbelieved truth lacks belief.

@art unbelieved-pass
That last one deserves a picture. A student has good evidence that she passed, and she did pass. But she can't bring herself to accept it. Justified, true, and not believed. Each of the three failing rows is a reason one condition is on the list.

@chapter Drop a condition
@math heading="Drop one, test again" items="T ∧ B:  lucky guess says yes, verdict no@truth and belief;;J ∧ B:  confident error says yes, verdict no@justification and belief;;J ∧ T:  unbelieved truth says yes, verdict no@justification and truth"
The full definition agrees with all four verdicts. Now remove a condition. Truth and belief alone call the lucky guess knowledge. Justification and belief call the confident error knowledge. Justification and truth call the unbelieved truth knowledge. Each shortened definition is too broad, on exactly the row that the dropped condition was there to exclude.

@art four-rows-only
But notice the limit. Agreement on four rows is agreement on four rows. The full definition survives these four cases, and no more than these four.

@chapter The method of cases
@list heading="The method" items="List the conditions@List the conditions|Record the verdict first@Record your verdict|Evaluate every row@evaluate|Find the first disagreement@find the first|Report the direction@report the direction"
So here's the method. List the conditions. Record your verdict on each case first, before looking at the definition, or the definition decides your verdict and the test tests nothing. Then evaluate the definition on every row, find the first disagreement, and report the direction. It's too broad on the lucky guess is a finding. It fails is not.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating a definition as a list of typical features@The first|Expecting the table to say which verdict is right@The second|Thinking the knower can check the truth condition@the third"
The first slip is treating a definition as a list of typical features. The confident error has two conditions of three, and it isn't two thirds knowledge. It isn't knowledge at all. The second is expecting the table to tell you which verdict is right. The verdict column is yours. And the third is thinking truth is something the knower can check.

@art inside-view
From the inside, the confident error feels exactly like ordinary perception. Justification and belief are conditions on the person. Truth is a condition on the world. That's why it's the easiest condition to forget, and the hardest to use.
