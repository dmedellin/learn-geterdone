@chapter Asking why, again and again
@title text="The Regress of Justification" sub="Knowledge and Evidence" art=thumb
Here's an old puzzle about justification, one that skeptics have pressed for a very long time, and a way to set it out that shows exactly what each answer gives up.

@art dripping-tap
Suppose you believe the tap is dripping, because you heard it. That seems justified. But what justifies hearing as a source? That it's been reliable. And what justifies that? A track record of checks, each of which was itself a belief.

@art chain-of-support
Keep asking for the support of each supporter, and you can answer in only a few ways. The chain of support ends in a foundation, or it loops back on itself, or it runs on forever. And the old skeptical argument, Agrippa's trilemma, says none of those is satisfactory.

@chapter Six claims
@math heading="Five letters" items="j: some belief is justified@j says;;n: every justified belief needs a justified supporter@n says;;c, f, i: a circle, a foundation, an infinite chain is acceptable@c, f and i"
Here's the argument as sentences. J says some belief is justified. N says every justified belief needs a justified supporter. And c, f and i say that a circle, a foundation that needs no further support, and an infinite chain are acceptable ways for the chain to end.

@list heading="The Agrippa set" items="1. j@some belief is justified|2. j → n@so it needs|3. n → (c ∨ f ∨ i)@the chain is a circle|4. ¬c@circles are not acceptable|5. ¬f@foundations are not|6. ¬i@nor are infinite chains"
Now the six claims. One: some belief is justified. Two: if so, it needs a justified supporter. Three: then the chain is a circle, a foundation, or infinite. Four: circles are not acceptable. Five: unsupported foundations are not. Six: nor are infinite chains.

@chapter No part can be spared
@art no-model
Search every assignment of true and false to the five letters. There's no row that makes all six true. The set is inconsistent. So something has to give, and the question becomes which.

@art whole-set-load
And the smallest inconsistent subset is the whole set. That matters. Remove any one sentence and a model appears, so every one of the six is load bearing for the trouble. None of them is innocent.

@chapter Four positions
@list heading="Four deletions" items="Delete ¬f: foundationalism@Foundationalism|Delete ¬c: coherentism@Coherentism|Delete ¬i: infinitism@Infinitism|Delete j: skepticism@skepticism"
So each single deletion gives a named position. Foundationalism deletes not f. Some beliefs justify others without needing a justifier of their own. Coherentism deletes not c. Beliefs support one another in a web. Infinitism deletes not i. Justification is never completed, only deepened as far as questions are asked. And skepticism deletes j itself. If nothing is justified, there's nothing for the regress to apply to.

@art foundation-stop
Take the foundationalist set, with not f removed. A model appears: j, n and f true, c and i false. A justified belief that needs a supporter, and the supporter is a foundation. The chain stops there.

@words text="Consistent ≠ correct"
But that only shows the position is coherent as a set of sentences. Whether any belief really is a foundation is the substantive question, and no table answers it.

@art web-and-line
And circles and infinite chains really are different complaints. A circle complains that a claim ends up supporting itself. An infinite chain complains that no one can ever finish giving the support. They get different letters, and deleting each gives a different model.

@chapter The limit
@words text="Five unanalysed claims"
One limit should be stated. The letters are unanalysed claims. The table doesn't look inside every justified belief needs a supporter and see a chain. The structure of the regress is carried by the sentences, not computed from them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating circular and infinite as one complaint@The first|Thinking the inconsistency refutes epistemology@The second|Expecting the table to pick the right position@the third"
The first slip is treating circular and infinite as the same complaint. The second is thinking the inconsistency refutes epistemology. It says something has to go, not which, and each denial can be argued against. And the third is expecting the table to pick the right position. Every deletion has a model.
