@chapter Smith's coins
@title text="Gettier Cases and the Fourth Condition" sub="Knowledge and Evidence" art=thumb
Knowledge is justified true belief. That's the traditional analysis, and it says that anyone with all three conditions knows. Here's a case built to break that claim.

@art ten-coins
It's Gettier's own case. Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get the job, and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. So Smith believes that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. In fact, Smith himself gets the job, and Smith himself has ten coins.

@words text="Justified · true · believed" sub="Did Smith know?"
Smith's belief is justified, true and held. And almost nobody thinks Smith knew it. He wasn't careless. His evidence was strong. It's the luck between the evidence and the truth that fails him.

@chapter The stopped clock
@art stopped-clock
Russell's stopped clock has the same shape, with a simpler mechanism. You look at a clock that stopped twelve hours ago. You read the time off it, and believe it's a quarter past two. By chance, it is. You had good reason to trust the clock, the belief is true, and you hold it.

@math heading="J  T  B → knows?" items="ordinary perception:  1 1 1 → yes@Ordinary perception;;Smith's coins:  1 1 1 → no@Smith's coins;;stopped clock:  1 1 1 → no@the stopped clock;;lucky guess:  0 1 1 → no@the lucky guess"
Put the cases in a table. Ordinary perception has all three, and it's knowledge. Smith's coins and the stopped clock also have all three, and they aren't. And the lucky guess lacks justification.

@art identical-rows
Look at those rows. Perception and Smith's coins are identical in all three columns, and different in the verdict. So whatever a definition says about one, it must say about the other. No formula of these three conditions, of any length, can fit. The three conditions are not jointly sufficient. The definition is too broad.

@chapter A fourth condition
@math heading="Add a column" items="L: relies on a false lemma@relies on a false lemma;;knows  ⟺  J ∧ T ∧ B ∧ ¬L@and not L"
The usual repair adds a condition. In both cases, the belief depends on something false along the way: that Jones will get the job, that the clock is running. Call that L, for relies on a false lemma, and require it to be absent. Justified, true, believed, and not L.

@art false-lemma
Now the table has a new column, one on the coins and the clock, and zero on perception. So the repair separates them. It agrees on four of five rows. And the fifth row is why the story doesn't end there.

@chapter Fake barns
@art fake-barns
Henry drives through country dotted with barn facades. He looks at the one real barn. He sees a barn, believes it's a barn, and he's right. He relies on no false lemma at all. And most people think he doesn't know, because he could easily have been looking at a facade.

@math heading="J  T  B  L → knows?" items="ordinary perception:  1 1 1 0 → yes@Perception;;fake barns:  1 1 1 0 → no@fake barns"
Perception and fake barns now agree on every column, including L, and differ in the verdict. Same trouble, new place.

@art moving-failure
The repair did what it was built to do and removed one row. It didn't stop the failure. It moved it. That's the usual history of a proposed fourth condition, and fixing this row takes a fifth. The literature has tried several.

@chapter What it shows
@list heading="What Gettier cases show" items="The three are not jointly sufficient@not jointly sufficient|Not that knowledge is impossible@impossible|Not that justification is irrelevant@irrelevant"
So be precise about what these cases show. The three conditions are not jointly sufficient. They don't show knowledge is impossible, because perception still has every condition and the verdict yes. And they don't show justification is irrelevant. Drop J from the repaired definition, and it calls the lucky guess knowledge.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Concluding knowledge is impossible@The first|Believing a fourth condition ends it@The second|Reading justified as certain@the third"
The first slip is concluding that knowledge is impossible. The second is believing a fourth condition ends the matter. A repair is a new definition, and meets the same test. And the third is reading justified as certain. Raising the standard of justification doesn't help, because any standard short of the truth itself can be met by a case with this structure.
