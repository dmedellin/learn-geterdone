@chapter A hundred tickets
@title text="The Lottery Paradox" sub="Knowledge and Evidence" art=thumb
You hold one ticket in a fair lottery. Do you believe it'll lose? Most people would say yes. But follow that thought, and you end up believing something you know is false.

@art hundred-tickets
A fair lottery sells a hundred tickets and draws one winner. For each ticket, there's a claim: this ticket loses. And every one of those claims has credence ninety nine hundredths, because ninety nine of the hundred equally likely outcomes make it true.

@math heading="Threshold rule" items="accept a claim when P ≥ t,  t < 1@at least t;;t = 99/100:  each claim accepted@ninety nine hundredths"
Now take a simple account of belief, the threshold rule. To accept a claim at threshold t is for its credence to be at least t, where t is below one. Set t to ninety nine hundredths. Each claim has exactly that credence, so each is accepted. All hundred of them.

@art threshold-line
And it doesn't feel like a stretch. All hundred claims are equally probable, so they all clear the line together, or none do. Each acceptance is reasonable on its own terms. The trouble only shows up when you put them together.

@chapter The contradiction
@list heading="Put the claims together" items="Each ticket i loses: accepted@Each claim|The lottery has a winner@has a winner|So the accepted claims can't all be true@cannot all be true"
Each claim, ticket i loses, is accepted. But the lottery has a winner, so not every ticket loses. So the hundred accepted claims cannot all be true, and their conjunction, which says every ticket loses, has probability zero.

@art no-survivor
No ticket survives, because it would have to be the winner, and then the claim about it would be false. That's the lottery paradox.

@chapter Two principles and a fact
@list heading="What's in play" items="Accept what is probable enough@probable enough|Accept the conjunction of what you accept@the conjunction|The lottery has a winner@The fact"
There are two principles and one fact. The first principle: accept a claim when it's probable enough. The second: accept the conjunction of what you accept. That's closure under conjunction. The fact: the lottery has a winner. The three can't stand together, and the fact isn't negotiable.

@chapter Three exits
@list heading="Three exits" items="Give up the threshold rule@Give up the threshold rule|Keep the threshold, give up closure@give up closure|Restrict it: statistics alone never accept@Restrict"
So the exits give up or restrict a principle. Give up the threshold rule: belief is more than high credence. Keep the threshold and give up closure: accept each claim, and not their conjunction. Or restrict the rule: statistical evidence alone, however strong, isn't the kind that supports belief.

@art three-doors
Each exit has defenders, and each has a price. The first needs a theory of what belief is. The second gives up an inference used whenever premises are combined, and leaves you holding beliefs you know aren't all true. And the third owes an account of why the same worry doesn't arise for a claim supported by an ordinary statistic.

@chapter A higher threshold?
@math heading="Raise t to 999/1000" items="100 tickets:  each 99/100, nothing accepted@a hundred tickets;;1,000 tickets:  each 999/1000, all accepted@a thousand tickets"
Why not just raise the threshold? Set it to nine hundred and ninety nine thousandths. With a hundred tickets, each claim is below it, so nothing is accepted and the paradox is gone. But with a thousand tickets, each claim has credence nine hundred and ninety nine thousandths. All are accepted, and the conjunction is impossible again.

@art bigger-lottery
For any threshold below one, there's a lottery large enough to reach it. So no threshold avoids the problem. Setting it at one avoids it by accepting only what's certain, which is a different and much narrower account of belief.

@art conjunction-chain
And notice where the trouble isn't. The credences add up correctly, and no Dutch book exists. The incoherence appears only in the acceptances, in the move from credence to belief.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Assuming belief is closed under conjunction@The first|Thinking a bigger threshold fixes it@The second|Concluding the credences are incoherent@the third"
The first slip is assuming rational belief is just obviously closed under conjunction. In the lottery, that principle with the threshold makes you believe no ticket wins while knowing one does. The second is thinking a bigger threshold fixes it. It only moves the example. And the third is concluding the credences are incoherent.
