@chapter A new justification condition
@title text="Reliabilism and the Clairvoyant" sub="Knowledge and Evidence" art=thumb
What makes a belief justified? One answer looks at what you have to go on: your evidence. Another looks at how the belief was made. This lesson tests both against two strange cases.

@math heading="Two theories" items="reliabilism:  R ∧ T ∧ B@The reliabilist;;evidentialism:  E ∧ T ∧ B@The evidentialist"
The reliabilist keeps the shape of the old definition and swaps the justification condition. A belief is justified when it was produced by a reliable process. So knowing is reliable process, truth and belief. The evidentialist keeps accessible evidence in that place instead.

@art barometer
Reliability is easy to misread. It doesn't mean the process got it right this time. A coin toss can deliver a true belief once, and guessing by coin still isn't reliable. A good barometer can give a false reading on a rare day, and stay reliable. The measure is the proportion of true beliefs a process delivers across its uses.

@chapter Norman
@art clairvoyant
Norman has a faculty of clairvoyance that, as it happens, is reliable. One day he finds himself believing that the President is in New York. He has no evidence for it, and no evidence that he has the faculty. And the President is in New York.

@math heading="R  E  T  B → knows?" items="perception:  1 1 1 1 → yes@perception;;Norman:  1 0 1 1 → no@Norman;;envatted twin:  0 1 0 1 → no@twin;;stranger's guess:  0 0 1 1 → no@guess"
Here's the table. Ordinary perception has everything. Norman has a reliable process, no evidence, truth and belief. The envatted twin, coming up, has evidence and no reliability. And the stranger's guess has neither.

@art too-broad-norman
Reliabilism says Norman knows, because reliability, truth and belief all hold. The recorded verdict is that he doesn't. So reliabilism is too broad on that row. Evidentialism says he doesn't know, because he has no evidence, and agrees with the verdict.

@chapter The brain in a vat
@art vat-twin
Now the envatted twin. Their experiences are exactly like yours, but they're a brain in a vat, fed those experiences by a computer. Nearly all their beliefs about the outside world are false, so as a case of knowing, the twin is simply a no. Both theories agree.

@math heading="R  E → justified?" items="perception:  1 1 → yes@perception;;Norman:  1 0 → no@Norman;;envatted twin:  0 1 → yes@the twin;;stranger's guess:  0 0 → no@the guess"
But ask a different question: is the twin justified? Change the target to justification. Perception: yes. Norman: no. The twin, if their beliefs are as well justified as yours: yes. And the guess: no.

@words text="Justified iff reliable" sub="too broad on Norman · too narrow on the twin"
Now justified if and only if reliable fails twice. It's too broad on Norman, who's reliable and not justified by this verdict. And too narrow on the twin, who's justified and not reliable. One definition, failing in both directions at once, on different rows.

@chapter The chicken sexer
@art chicken-sexer
Justified if and only if you have evidence fits all four rows. That doesn't make evidentialism correct. The reliabilist replies with the chicken sexer, who sorts chicks reliably, can't say how, and seems to know. His row has the same values as Norman's, with the opposite verdict, and no formula can fit two rows like that.

@art two-rows-clash
That's a fact about tables, not about knowledge. If two rows have identical inputs and different verdicts, no formula over those inputs can fit both. Add the chicken sexer beside Norman, and you'd need a new condition, a new column, to tell them apart.

@words text="The choice of rows decides what fits."
So a theory that fits the table isn't thereby right. The rows are a few chosen cases. And reliabilists have answered Norman by adding that the believer must have no reason to doubt the faculty, which is a new condition, tested the same way.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading reliable as right this time@The first|Concluding that a fitting theory is true@The second|Treating a counterexample as a refutation@the third"
The first slip is reading reliable as right this time. The second is concluding that a theory which fits the table is true. And the third is confusing a counterexample with a refutation. A definition that's too broad on one row has a reason for revision, not necessarily for dropping the idea.
