@chapter The question
@title text="Rent as a Monopoly Price" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11" art=thumb
What is rent? The price of using land, you'd say. Smith opens the longest chapter of the book by saying exactly how that price is fixed, and the answer puts rent on a different side of the sum from wages and profit.

@words text="The highest which the tenant can afford to pay"
Rent, he says, is naturally the highest which the tenant can afford to pay in the actual circumstances of the land. The landlord leaves him only enough to keep up his stock, seed, labour, cattle and tools, plus the ordinary profit of farming in the neighbourhood.

@art lease-table
Picture them at the renewal of a lease. The farmer knows what a year costs him, and what his stock would earn on any other farm nearby. Below that he walks away. The landlord knows he knows. So the rent is whatever the harvest is worth beyond those two things.

@chapter Not a return on improvement
@art kelp-rocks
Isn't rent just a fair return on the landlord's draining and building? Partly, sometimes, Smith says, but scarce more, because landlords ask rent for unimproved land too. Kelp, a seaweed burnt for glass and soap, grows on rocks the tide covers twice a day. It was never increased by human industry. Yet a kelp shore pays rent like a cornfield.

@art shetland-fishing
And round Shetland, the sea is rich in fish, but the fishers need a house on land. So the rent is in proportion to what the farmer can make from land and water together.

@quote text="The rent of land, therefore, considered as the price paid for the use of the land, is naturally a monopoly price." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11"
So: the rent of land, therefore, considered as the price paid for the use of the land, is naturally a monopoly price. It's fixed not by what the landlord laid out, but by what the farmer can afford to give.

@chapter Effect, not cause
@words text="Wages and profit: causes of price · Rent: its effect"
Then the sentence that separates rent from everything before it. High or low wages and profit are the causes of high or low price. High or low rent is the effect of it. A produce that only just repays its wages and profit can come to market, but it affords no rent at all.

@chapter Three farms
@art three-farms
The lab works it with three farms the lesson wrote. Each pays sixty pounds in wages and lays out forty in other stock, an outlay of a hundred. Ordinary profit is ten per cent, ten pounds. The north farm yields a hundred quarters, the middle farm eighty, and the poor farm fifty five.

@math heading="Corn at £2 a quarter" items="north:  100 · 2 − 100 − 10 = £90@the north;;middle:  80 · 2 − 110 = £50@middle farm;;poor:  55 · 2 − 110 = £0@poor farm;;total rent:  £140@a hundred and forty"
At two pounds a quarter, the north farm's produce is worth two hundred. Take off the hundred outlay and ten profit: ninety pounds rent. The middle farm, a hundred and sixty less a hundred and ten, fifty. The poor farm, a hundred and ten less a hundred and ten, nothing. Total rent, a hundred and forty.

@art remainder-bars
The poor farm is still brought to market. It pays its workers and its ordinary profit, and leaves the landlord nothing at all.

@chapter The price falls
@math heading="Corn at £3/2 a quarter" items="north:  150 − 110 = £40@north;;middle:  120 − 110 = £10@middle;;poor:  82.50 − 110 → no rent@poor farm's;;total rent:  £140 → £50@fifty"
Now let corn fall to one pound ten shillings. The north farm's rent drops from ninety to forty, the middle farm's from fifty to ten. The poor farm's produce is worth only eighty two pounds fifty, against a hundred and ten to cover. No rent, and it wouldn't be farmed if the tenant had a choice. Total rent falls from a hundred and forty to fifty.

@art price-falls
A quarter off the price took away nearly two thirds of the rent. That's what it means for rent to be the remainder. The outlay doesn't move, so the whole change in price lands on rent.

@words text="Wages £60 → £66" sub="north farm: £90 → £83.40"
Last, hold corn at two pounds and raise every farm's wages to sixty six. Outlay becomes a hundred and six, profit ten sixty. The north farm's rent falls to eighty three forty, and the poor farm now runs at a loss of six sixty. With the price held, dearer labour is paid out of rent.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking rent raises the price of corn@The first|Reading rent as interest on improvements@The second|Thinking monopoly squeezes the tenant below profit@The third"
The first slip is thinking rent is a cost that raises the price of corn. The second is reading rent as interest on the landlord's improvements. The third is thinking monopoly price means the tenant is squeezed below ordinary profit. He keeps it. And David Ricardo, in 1817, gave another account: rent is the surplus better land yields over the poorest land in use.
