@chapter The question
@title text="Silver from America" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11, Digression" art=thumb
For two centuries, a quarter of wheat in England cost about two ounces of silver. Then American silver arrived. What happened to the price, and how sure can we be?

@art silver-ships
Smith's second period runs from about 1570 to 1640. Silver sank in value, and corn rose from about two ounces a quarter, about ten shillings, to six and eight ounces, about thirty and forty shillings. Nobody disputes the fact or its cause, he says: the mines of America.

@chapter The Windsor prices
@math heading="Windsor, 1621 to 1636" items="best wheat, 9 bushels:  £2 10s = 600d@two pounds ten;;8 bushels:  600 · 8/9 = 1600/3 d@take off a ninth;;middle wheat:  600 · (8/9)² ≈ 474.07d = £1 19s 6d@another ninth;;silver:  474.07 / 62 ≈ 7.65 oz@sixty two"
His evidence is Eton College's accounts at Windsor, best wheat in a quarter of nine bushels. From sixteen twenty one to sixteen thirty six, it averaged two pounds ten, six hundred pence. To get a quarter of eight bushels, take off a ninth. For middle wheat, take off another ninth. That's one pound nineteen and sixpence. At sixty two pence an ounce, about seven and two thirds ounces of silver.

@art ninth-off
For the years before, fifteen ninety five to sixteen twenty, the same two steps give about one pound twelve and eightpence, about six and a third ounces. So a pound of his money, which bought two quarters before 1570, buys about nought point six one, then nought point five one.

@art silver-sinks
Silver now buys about a third as much corn as in the first period, and then about a quarter. That's the sinking of silver the digression is about.

@chapter The third period
@words text="About 1636" sub="the fall in silver's value complete"
Then Smith says, about sixteen thirty six, the effect of America's mines in reducing silver's value seems to have been completed. Silver never sank lower against corn. But the evidence needs care. The average from sixteen thirty seven to seventeen hundred is a shilling higher than before.

@list heading="What else raised the price" items="The civil war@civil war|The bounty of 1688@bounty|Clipped and worn coin@clipped"
Smith names three distortions. The civil war discouraged farming and sent best wheat at Windsor to four pounds five in sixteen forty eight and four pounds in sixteen forty nine. The bounty on exports of sixteen eighty eight raised the home price. And by sixteen ninety five, clipped and worn coin was near twenty five per cent below standard.

@art war-spike
The two war years alone settle most of it. Their excess over two pounds ten is seven hundred and eighty pence. Spread over sixty four years, about twelve pence a year, close to the twelve and a third by which the average rose.

@words text="It scarce, perhaps, deserves the name of belief"
From seventeen oh one to seventeen sixty four, the Windsor average fell to two pounds and sixpence three farthings. Silver, he says, seems to have risen somewhat against corn this century. But he grades his own opinion carefully. It scarce, perhaps, deserves the name of belief.

@chapter Why silver held
@art east-trade
Why should silver get dearer while more came in? Because the market grew faster. Europe, richer and settled, needed more coin and plate. America was a new market. And the East Indies took silver by the shipload, because there an ounce of gold fetched only ten or twelve of silver, against fourteen or fifteen in Europe.

@chapter Quantity is not value
@art ox-and-lamb
Spain and Portugal imported silver at about twenty two times the weight of gold, yet gold was worth only fourteen or fifteen times silver. Smith's answer is an ox and a lamb. An ox at ten guineas, two hundred and ten shillings, against a lamb at three and six, is sixty to one. Nobody infers sixty lambs in the market for every ox.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the rise after 1636 as silver still sinking@The first|Reading a nominal price as silver paid@The second|Inferring values from quantities@The third"
The first slip is reading the rising price after sixteen thirty six as silver still sinking. The second is reading a nominal price as the silver actually paid, when coin was clipped. The third is inferring the ratio of values from the ratio of quantities.
