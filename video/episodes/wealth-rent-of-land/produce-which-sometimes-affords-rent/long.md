@chapter The question
@title text="Produce Which Sometimes Affords Rent" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11" art=thumb
Food always affords rent. What about everything else, skins and wool, timber and stone, coal, metals and gems? Smith's answer is: sometimes.

@quote text="Human food seems to be the only produce of land, which always and necessarily affords some rent to the landlord." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11, Part 2"
Human food seems to be the only produce of land, which always and necessarily affords some rent to the landlord. Other sorts of produce sometimes may, and sometimes may not.

@chapter Food comes first
@art skins-thrown
In a rude society, land gives materials for clothing and lodging to far more people than it can feed. The hunter who feeds himself has more skins than he can wear, and much is thrown away. They're worth, at most, the labour of fitting them for use, and pay no rent. It takes foreign commerce, like Flemish buyers for English wool, to give them a price.

@art stomach-vs-palace
Then Smith's favourite argument. Among savage nations, he says, a hundredth of the year's labour provides clothing and lodging, and ninety nine parts go to food. Once one family can feed two, half of society is free for other things. The desire of food is limited by the narrow capacity of the human stomach. The desire for buildings, dress and furniture seems to have no limit.

@chapter Four coal mines
@art four-mines
But that demand doesn't always come. Take coal. Some mines are too barren to work. Some pay the labour and the ordinary profit, but no rent. Some are fertile but badly placed, with no roads or water to carry the coal away.

@math heading="At £1 a ton · outlay and profit £110" items="barren, 100 tons:  100 − 132 = −£32@barren mine;;second, 110 tons:  110 − 110 = £0@second mine;;middling, 150 tons:  150 − 110 = £40@middling;;fertile, 200 tons:  200 − 110 = £90 · total £130@fertile"
Here are the lesson's four mines. The barren mine costs more, a hundred and thirty two with profit, and makes a loss of thirty two. The second mine pays its way exactly, no rent. The middling mine pays forty. And the fertile mine, two hundred tons, pays ninety. Total rent, a hundred and thirty.

@chapter The best mine sets the price
@words text="The most fertile coal mine, too, regulates the price of coals at all the other mines in its neighbourhood."
Now the key sentence. The most fertile coal mine, too, regulates the price of coals at all the other mines in its neighbourhood. By slightly underselling its neighbours, it gets more rent and more profit, and they're soon obliged to follow.

@art best-undersells
Let the fertile mine bring the price to three quarters of a pound a ton. Its rent falls to forty, the middling mine's to two fifty. The second mine now loses twenty seven fifty, the barren one fifty seven. Total rent drops from a hundred and thirty to forty two fifty. Some works are abandoned altogether. Ricardo later turned this round, letting the poorest mine in use set the price.

@chapter Shares of the produce
@art share-circles
Smith gives rules of thumb. An estate's rent is commonly about a third of its gross produce. For coal, a fifth is a very great rent, and a tenth the common one. So where thirty years' purchase is a moderate price for an estate, ten is a good price for a coal mine.

@math heading="Cornwall against Peru" items="tin:  1/6 + 1/20 = 10/60 + 3/60 = 13/60@a sixth;;silver:  1/5 = 12/60@a fifth;;13 : 12@thirteen to twelve"
Metals compete across the world. Smith is told a sixth of the gross produce is the rent of Cornwall's tin mines, and the Duke of Cornwall's tax is about a twentieth more. Peru's silver paid the Spanish crown a fifth. That's thirteen sixtieths against twelve: as thirteen to twelve.

@art world-silver
The precious metals bear carriage so well that Peru's price reaches the mines of Europe, and even China. So rent depends on a mine's superiority over others, not its absolute fertility. Find mines far richer than Potosi, and even Potosi might not be worth working.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Expecting every mine to pay rent@The first|Taking rent to measure absolute fertility@The second|Reading thirteen to twelve as money sums@The third"
The first slip is expecting every mine, like every farm, to pay rent. The second is taking a mine's rent to measure its absolute fertility. And the third is reading thirteen to twelve as a comparison of money. It compares shares of each mine's produce, not amounts.
