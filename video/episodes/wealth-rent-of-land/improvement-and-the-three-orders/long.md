@chapter The question
@title text="Improvement and the Three Great Orders" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11" art=thumb
As a country improves, what happens to the price of cattle, wool and fish? And who gains? The last part of Smith's chapter on rent sorts produce into three kinds, and ends with his sharpest page on the three orders of society.

@list heading="Three sorts of rude produce" items="Scarce in the power of industry to multiply@scarce in the power|Multiplied in proportion to the demand@in proportion|Industry limited or uncertain@limited or uncertain"
The first sort is what it's scarce in the power of human industry to multiply at all. The second, what it can multiply in proportion to the demand. The third, where industry's power is limited or uncertain.

@chapter Rarities
@art woodcock
The first sort is rare birds and fishes, game and wild fowl. If woodcocks became so fashionable they sold for twenty guineas each, no effort could bring many more to market. Their price has no ceiling. That's how Smith explains what Romans paid for a white nightingale: the high price of a rarity, not cheap silver.

@chapter Cattle
@art cattle-ceiling
The second sort is cattle, poultry, hogs and the dairy. Nature makes them abundant, and cultivation crowds them out, so their price rises. But only up to a ceiling: the price at which feeding them on cultivated land pays as well as corn. Past that, more land would be put to them.

@words text="The greatest of Scotland's gains from the Union"
Until cattle reach that price, the land can't be fully improved, because far from towns, farms are manured only by their own cattle. So Smith rates the rise in the price of cattle after the Union with England as perhaps the greatest of Scotland's commercial gains from it.

@math heading="A Highland pasture, 60 beasts" items="outlay and profit:  24 + 16 + 4 = £44@twenty four;;at £2:  120 − 44 = £76@two pounds;;at £6:  360 − 44 = £316@six pounds;;316 / 76 ≈ 4.16@four point one six"
Smith says Highland cattle tripled in price after the Union, and many rents tripled and quadrupled. The lesson's pasture shows why. Wages of twenty four pounds and stock of sixteen, plus four profit, make forty four. At two pounds a beast, the rent is seventy six. Triple the price to six pounds, and the rent is three hundred and sixteen. That's about four point one six times.

@art highland-pasture
The produce takes no more labour to collect than before, so all of the rise goes to the landlord. The lab holds the outlay fixed, and a real estate's would move. But that's the shape of Smith's argument.

@chapter Wool and manufactures
@words text="A tod of wool: 12 bushels → 6"
The third sort includes wool and hides. Their market is the world, so their price needn't rise as a country improves. In England, Smith reckons, a tod of twenty eight pounds of wool once bought twelve bushels of wheat. In his day, six. Half the real price. He calls it the effect of violence and artifice: bans on exporting English wool.

@art wool-bales
In the lesson's sheep country, a hundred sheep at five pounds, against three hundred and thirty of outlay and profit, pay a rent of a hundred and seventy. Close the wool market so a sheep falls a fifth, to four pounds, and the rent is seventy. A fifth off the price takes more than half the rent.

@art cheaper-watch
Manufactures go the other way. Improvement lowers their real price. A better watch than twenty pounds bought in the middle of the last century may now cost perhaps twenty shillings. So whatever cheapens manufactures lets the landlord's rent buy more.

@chapter The three orders
@art three-orders
So every improvement tends to raise the real rent of land. And the whole produce divides into rent, wages and profit, the revenues of three great orders. Landlords' interest is bound up with the public's, but they're often ignorant, their revenue costing neither labour nor care. Labourers suffer most in a decline, and are little heard.

@quote text="To widen the market, and to narrow the competition, is always the interest of the dealers." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11, Conclusion"
And the dealers. Their interest is always in some respects different from, and even opposite to, the public's. To widen the market, and to narrow the competition, is always the interest of the dealers. So any proposal from them should be examined with the most suspicious attention.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading dearer cattle as cheaper silver@The first|Calling the rise in cattle a public calamity@The second|Hearing the chapter as praise of landlords@The third"
The first slip is reading dearer cattle as proof that silver got cheaper. Cheaper silver would raise everything together. The second is calling the rise in cattle a public calamity. It's the forerunner of improvement. The third is hearing the chapter as praise of landlords. All three orders are described, and none is flattered.
