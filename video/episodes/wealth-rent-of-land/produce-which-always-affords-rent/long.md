@chapter The question
@title text="Produce Which Always Affords Rent" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11" art=thumb
Some land pays rent and some doesn't. Is there any produce that always does? Smith says yes: food. And his reason is about people.

@quote text="As men, like all other animals, naturally multiply in proportion to the means of their subsistence, food is always more or less in demand." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11, Part 1"
As men, like all other animals, naturally multiply in proportion to the means of their subsistence, food is always more or less in demand. It can always command some labour.

@art food-commands-labour
And land, in almost any situation, grows more food than it takes to feed the labour that brings it to market. So something always remains for the landlord. Even the most desert moors of Norway and Scotland, with pasture for cattle, pay some small rent.

@chapter Fertility and situation
@art near-and-far
Two things make that rent larger or smaller. Fertility: rent rises with the goodness of the land. And situation, whatever the fertility. Land near a town rents higher than equally fertile land far away, because distant produce costs more to carry, so more labour must be kept out of it.

@math heading="100 quarters at £2 · profit 10%" items="near:  200 − 1.1 · (50 + 30) = £112@The near;;distant:  200 − 1.1 · (70 + 30) = £90@distant one;;after the road:  200 − 1.1 · (55 + 30) = £106.50@after a road"
The lesson's farms both yield a hundred quarters. The near farm's rent comes to a hundred and twelve pounds. The distant one pays twenty more to carriers, so its rent is ninety. Now suppose that after a road, carriage falls from twenty pounds to five. The distant farm's rent rises to a hundred and six fifty.

@art new-road
The road saved fifteen pounds and the rent rose sixteen fifty, because profit is charged on a smaller outlay. Smith calls good roads, canals and rivers the greatest of all improvements. Counties near London once petitioned against new turnpikes, fearing cheaper rivals. Their rents, he says, have risen since.

@chapter Corn, meat and gardens
@art corn-and-pasture
A cornfield grows much more food than the best pasture of equal size. But meat and bread change value as farming spreads. In most of Britain, a pound of the best meat is worth more than two pounds of the best white bread. And the two rents stay level, because land moves between corn and pasture until they do.

@art sugar-and-vineyard
Hop gardens, kitchen gardens and vineyards pay more, but seldom more than fair interest on their greater expense. The exception is a crop with too little land for the demand: a few rare vineyards, and the sugar islands. There, the rent may exceed the ordinary in almost any degree.

@chapter Potatoes
@math heading="One acre" items="wheat:  2000 lb@two thousand;;potatoes:  12000 lb, half water → 6000 lb@twelve thousand;;6000 / 2000 = 3 times the food@three times"
Then Smith asks what if a country's common food yielded far more per acre? An acre of wheat gives two thousand weight. An acre of potatoes, twelve thousand. Allow half the weight of the root for water, and it's still six thousand weight of solid food, three times the acre of wheat.

@math heading="A penny a pound · profit 10%" items="wheat:  20 − 1.1 · (7 + 5) = £6.80@wheat acre;;potatoes:  60 − 1.1 · (6 + 4) = £49@potato acre"
The lesson prices solid food at a penny a pound. The wheat acre is worth twenty pounds, with twelve in outlay. Its rent is six eighty. The potato acre is worth sixty, with ten in outlay. Its rent is forty nine. That's the sense of Smith's remark that under potatoes, rents would rise much beyond what they are.

@art potato-and-wheat
But he's candid about the catch. Potatoes are hard to keep through the year, and impossible to store like corn for two or three years. A reader who knows Ireland's famine of the eighteen forties knows what that risk means. Smith didn't foresee it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking all produce affords rent@The first|Taking a distant farm's lower rent as poorer soil@The second|Setting a rare crop's rent by its costs@The third"
The first slip is thinking all produce affords rent because food does. Other produce only sometimes. The second is taking a distant farm's lower rent as poorer soil. Both farms yield a hundred quarters. The third is thinking a rare crop's rent is set by its costs. It's set by how eagerly buyers compete.

@chapter Try it
@words text="100 quarters at £2 · wages £70 · stock £30" sub="rent at 10%? and if carriage falls by £15?"
Your turn. Take the distant farm. Add the outlay, add ten per cent, and subtract from two hundred. Then cut fifteen from the wages and do it again. You should find ninety, then a hundred and six fifty.
