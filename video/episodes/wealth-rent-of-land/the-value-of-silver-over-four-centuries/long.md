@chapter The question
@title text="The Value of Silver over Four Centuries" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 11, Part 3" art=thumb
If the price of wheat in silver falls, what happened? Did wheat get cheaper, or did silver get dearer? Smith's digression on silver says it's usually the second, and he reads four centuries of prices to show it.

@art three-paths
Silver's market is the whole commercial world, and three things could happen to it. If demand grows faster than supply, silver gets dearer, and corn's money price falls. If supply grows faster, corn gets dearer in silver. If they keep pace, the price stays about the same. Smith thinks all three happened in Europe, nearly in that order.

@chapter The first period
@words text="1350: a quarter at 4 oz of silver ≈ 20s" sub="by 1500: 2 oz ≈ 10s"
The first period. In 1350, a quarter of wheat seems to have cost no less than four ounces of silver, Tower weight, about twenty shillings of Smith's money. By about 1500 it had fallen to two ounces, about ten shillings, and stayed there until about 1570.

@art statute-scroll
How does he know? First, a statute. In 1350, Parliament, complaining of servants trying to raise their wages, ordered that wheat paid as wages be valued at no more than tenpence a bushel. A law fixing a moderate price, he says, is better evidence than the prices of particular years.

@math heading="The Statute of Labourers" items="a quarter:  8 · 10d = 80d = 6s 8d@eight bushels;;its silver:  8 · 1/2 oz = 4 oz@half an ounce;;the feast of 1309:  £19 / 53 = 380s / 53 ≈ 7s 2d@fifty three"
Tenpence was then about half an ounce of silver. So the quarter of eight bushels was eighty pence, six and eightpence, holding four ounces. And in 1309, a prior's installation feast at Canterbury bought fifty three quarters of wheat for nineteen pounds, about seven shillings and twopence a quarter.

@art feast-table
And the assize of bread and ale of 1262 ran from one shilling to twenty a quarter, with a middle price he argues was not less than six and eightpence. Three different sources, all pointing to about four ounces.

@chapter Same name, less silver
@math heading="Wheat at 6s 8d, in 1776 money" items="1350:  £1@In 1350;;1436 to 1463:  £2/3@fourteen thirty six;;1512 to 1562:  £1/2@fifteen twelve;;quarters a pound buys:  1 → 1.5 → 2@a pound"
For two hundred years, statutes still treated six and eightpence as the moderate price. But the coin kept losing silver. In 1350 that sum was a pound of Smith's money. By fourteen thirty six, thirteen and fourpence, two thirds. By fifteen twelve, ten shillings, a half. So a pound of his money buys one quarter, then one and a half, then two.

@art coin-and-sacks
Silver buys twice as much corn at the end as at the start. The legislators saw no reason to change the figure, because silver itself had grown dearer.

@chapter Why corn?
@art corn-vs-cattle
Why measure by corn, not cattle? Because labour is the real measure, and corn, the labourer's food, comes nearest to it. Cattle and game were cheap in old times, but that was their low value, not silver's high value. An ox at Buenos Ayres cost twenty one pence halfpenny. And corn is a necessary, silver only a superfluity.

@chapter Fleetwood's table
@math heading="Fleetwood's divisions, average a quarter" items="£2.96 · £1.93 · £1.29@two ninety six;;£1.06 · £0.70 · £0.50@one oh six;;last division:  £2.37@two thirty seven"
Then the table. Smith puts the wheat prices Bishop Fleetwood collected, from twelve oh two to fifteen ninety seven, into seven divisions in his own money. The averages are about two ninety six pounds, one ninety three, one twenty nine, one oh six, nought point seven, and nought point five. Then the last division jumps to two thirty seven.

@art fleetwood-dots
A steady fall to the sixth, under a fifth of the first, and then the rise he expects. But Smith admits these were mostly prices remarked for extraordinary dearness or cheapness, and says no very certain conclusion can be drawn from them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading a falling corn price as cheaper corn@The first|Taking cheap old cattle as proof silver was dear@The second|Supposing silver must cheapen as a country grows rich@The third"
The first slip is reading a falling money price of corn as corn losing value. The second is taking the cheapness of old cattle and game as proof silver was dear. The third is supposing silver must get cheaper as a country grows rich. Smith calls that notion groundless. And Ricardo objected that corn itself isn't a fixed measure.
