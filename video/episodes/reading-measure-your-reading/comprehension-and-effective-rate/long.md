@chapter The question
@title text="Comprehension and the Effective Rate" sub="Reading Faster · Measure Your Reading" art=thumb
Does reading faster actually get more read? To find out, you need two timed readings and a way to count what you understood, not just how quickly the words went past.

@art two-readings
So the drill has two readings. The first is at your natural pace. The second is as fast as you can make yourself go. Each is followed by six questions, with the text hidden.

@chapter The usual result
@art speed-up-score-down
For most people told to go as fast as they can, the second reading is faster, often by a lot. But the usual result is a rate that went up and a score that went down. The question that matters is what happened to their product.

@chapter The effective rate
@math heading="Effective reading rate" items="comprehension = right / asked@questions right;;effective rate = rate × comprehension@the rate times"
Comprehension is questions right divided by questions asked. The effective reading rate is the rate times comprehension, in words a minute. Think of it as understood words per minute.

@art product-rectangle
Jackson and McClelland, in nineteen seventy nine, used this product. They called it effective reading speed, and used it to compare readers who traded speed against understanding in different amounts.

@words text="Rate alone can't tell a fast reader from a fast page-turner"
That's why every drill in this Subject reports it beside your rate. A rate on its own can't tell a fast reader from a fast page turner.

@chapter Pushed too far
@math heading="400 words, two readings" items="A: 100 s, 6 of 6 → 240 × 6/6 = 240@all six right;;B: 50 s, 2 of 6 → 480 × 2/6 = 160@two right;;B − A = −80@fell by eighty"
Here's the pushed too far preset. Reading A is four hundred words in a hundred seconds, with all six right. That's two forty words a minute, and an effective rate of two forty. Reading B is the same length in fifty seconds, with two right. Four eighty words a minute, but an effective rate of one sixty. The rate doubled, and the effective rate fell by eighty.

@chapter When faster wins
@math heading="Worth it: B in 75 s, 5 of 6" items="400 × 60 / 75 = 320@three twenty;;320 × 5/6 ≈ 266.7@five sixths;;≈ 26.7 more than A's 240@twenty six point seven"
Now change only B. Four hundred words in seventy five seconds, with five right. That's three twenty words a minute, and three twenty times five sixths is about two sixty six point seven. About twenty six point seven more than A's two forty.

@art balance-scale
So the lesson isn't that slow is good. It's that the product decides. A faster reading that keeps most of its score can win.

@chapter How big is one question?
@art six-questions
Read the scores with their size in mind. With six questions a passage, one question is about sixteen point seven points of comprehension.

@words text="1 question ≈ 16.7 points" sub="a one-question difference is not a result"
So five right against six is a single question, and a single question can turn on a lapse of attention or on a guess. A one question difference is not a result. The lab prints that sentence above the tiles.

@art two-passages
And two readings differ in more than pace. They're different passages, an explanation and a story, and one came first. The natural pace reading always comes first, so the fast one gets any warm up and any fatigue. One pair of readings can't separate pace from passage or order.

@chapter The protocol
@list heading="Comparing two readings" items="Two fresh passages of one kind@two fresh|Answer without looking back@without looking back|Multiply before you compare@Multiply|Discount one question@discount one"
Use two fresh passages of one kind. Answer the questions without looking back, because a question answered by rereading measures the rereading. Multiply before you compare. And discount one question: if the scores differ by one, call it a tie and repeat it another day, in the other order.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Faster must be better@The first|Judging by how it felt@The second|Treating one question as a change@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the faster reading is the better one. The second is judging comprehension by how the reading felt. A fast reading can feel fluent and leave little behind. And the third is treating a one question difference as a change.

@chapter Try it
@words text="A: 300 wpm, 4 of 6 · B: 250 wpm, 6 of 6" sub="which has the higher effective rate?"
Your turn. Reading A is three hundred words a minute with four of six right. Reading B is two fifty with six of six. Which has the higher effective rate? Multiply first, then compare the two products, not the two rates. You should find B wins, two fifty against two hundred.
