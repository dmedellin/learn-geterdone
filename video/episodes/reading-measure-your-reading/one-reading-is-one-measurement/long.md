@chapter The question
@title text="One Reading Is One Measurement" sub="Reading Faster · Measure Your Reading" art=thumb
Say you time a reading, try nothing new, time another, and the second one's faster. Did you improve? This lesson is about why one reading can't tell you, and what can.

@art three-readings
You start with two more readings at your natural pace. The Seed Tin, then Lights on the Coast, each with its six questions. The first joins your earlier two as a baseline. The second is a test, and it's the experiment, because nothing changed between it and the others.

@chapter The baseline range
@words text="Baseline range" sub="lowest to highest of your first three natural-pace rates"
Your baseline range runs from the lowest to the highest of your first three natural pace rates, in words a minute. Any later reading is inside it, above it, or below it.

@art tight-wide
Readers differ in how much they vary. And so do passages, because a rate depends on the passage as well as on the reader.

@math heading="Two readers" items="Tight: 238, 251, 245 → range 238 to 251, 13 wide@two thirty eight;;Test 247 → inside@two forty seven;;Wide: 212, 268, 240 → range 56 wide@two twelve;;Test 262 → inside@two sixty two"
The tight reader's baselines were two thirty eight, two fifty one and two forty five. That's a range from two thirty eight to two fifty one, thirteen wide, and a later two forty seven is inside. The wide reader read two twelve, two sixty eight and two forty, a range fifty six wide. There, a later two sixty two is inside too.

@chapter The arithmetic of chance
@art four-runners
Now the arithmetic of chance. Take four readings of one kind of passage, with nothing changed between them. Each one is as likely as any other to be the fastest of the four.

@list heading="Four readings, nothing changed" items="Fourth is fastest: 1 time in 4@fastest one time|Fourth is slowest: 1 time in 4@slowest one time|Outside the range: half the time@half the time"
So the fourth is the fastest one time in four, and then it's above the range of the first three. It's the slowest one time in four as well. A reading with no reason to differ lands outside the range half the time.

@words text="262: above the range once" sub="a fourth reading does this about one time in four"
Give the tight reader a test at two sixty two. That's eleven above the top of the range. It's the result you were hoping for, and it's also what chance alone produces one time in four. That's all one reading can say.

@chapter The rule: a median of three
@math heading="Tight reader, range 238 to 251" items="Tests: 262, 270, 258@two seventy;;Median = 262@median is;;262 > 251 → above on the median@above two fifty one"
So the Subject asks for more. A change counts as evidence when the median of your last three tests is above the baseline range. Say the tests are two sixty two, two seventy and two fifty eight. The median is two sixty two, above two fifty one. That's above the range on the median.

@art median-three
Why a median? Because one lucky reading can't move it. A median moves only when at least two of the three move.

@art lucky-dice
And even this is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee. With nothing changed, the median of three tests still clears the range about one time in five. That's why you read the verdict again each week, never once.

@chapter Each time a test comes in
@list heading="The routine" items="Keep the baseline fixed@Keep your baseline|Place the latest test@place the latest|Wait for three tests@wait for three|Test fresh, at natural pace@fresh passages"
Keep your baseline fixed. Don't recompute it from later readings, because a range that moves with the tests can't measure them. Then place the latest test, and if it's above once, write it down and don't act on it. Then wait for three tests. And always test on fresh passages at natural pace.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Calling one test your speed@The first|Calling one high reading progress@The second|Comparing a test with the mean@And the third"
The first slip is calling one test your reading speed. The wide reader read at two twelve and at two sixty eight with nothing changed. The second is thinking one reading above your range means you improved. And the third is comparing a test with the mean. Half of all readings are above the middle, which is why the verdict uses the range.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Baselines: 230, 252, 241" sub="first test: 255"
Your turn. A reader's baselines are two thirty, two fifty two and two forty one. What's the range? The first test is two fifty five. What's the verdict? Then later tests come in at two forty nine, two fifty eight and two sixty two. What now?

@art range-band
You should find a range from two thirty to two fifty two, twenty two wide. Two fifty five is above it once, which chance does about one time in four. And the median of the last three is two fifty eight, above the range on the median.
