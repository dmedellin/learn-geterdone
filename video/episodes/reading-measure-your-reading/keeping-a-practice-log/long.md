@chapter The question
@title text="Keeping a Practice Log" sub="Reading Faster · Measure Your Reading" art=thumb
Practice feels like progress. But how would you know if it is? Every drill in this Subject writes an entry to a log, and the log is how you find out.

@art five-kinds
The log is kept in your browser. Each entry carries one of five kinds, set by the drill that wrote it. And only two of those kinds ever count toward a verdict.

@chapter Five kinds of entry
@list heading="Five kinds" items="baseline@a baseline|test@a test|practice@practice|condition@a condition|other@other"
A baseline is a natural pace reading of a fresh passage, with questions, before any technique. A test is a reading made like a baseline, later, to see whether anything has changed. Practice is a reading done to train. A condition is one side of a comparison, like your as fast as you can reading. And other is notes and plans.

@art counted-greyed
Only baselines and tests count. Practice, condition and other entries are kept and listed, greyed out, and never counted. A pacer reading measures that condition. Mixed into the tests, it would make a technique look like a change in you.

@chapter Scored by effective rate
@words text="effective rate = rate × right / asked"
Each counted session is scored by its effective rate, the rate times comprehension. So a reading that got faster by understanding less doesn't look like progress.

@words text="Same reading, two verdicts?"
That means two pages can give one reading different verdicts. A fast reading with a question wrong can be above the range in words, and inside, or even below it, in understood words.

@chapter Three example logs
@art three-dots
The starting preset has three baselines, at effective rates of two thirty eight, two fifty one and two forty five. The median is two forty five, and the verdict is: needs a reading after the three baselines. Three readings make a range. They can't test it.

@math heading="Six weeks" items="range = 238 to 251@two thirty eight to;;tests: 255, 262, 270, 266@two fifty five;;last three: 262, 270, 266 → median 266@median of;;266 > 251: above on the median@above the top"
The range runs from two thirty eight to two fifty one. The six weeks preset adds four tests, at two fifty five, two sixty two, two seventy and two sixty six. The last three have a median of two sixty six, above the top of the range at two fifty one. The first test drops out once three later ones arrive.

@math heading="Speed only" items="288 × 5/6 = 240@two eighty eight;;354 × 4/6 = 236@three fifty four;;488 × 3/6 = 244@four eighty eight;;median 240: inside the range@inside the baseline range"
The speed only preset is the warning. Its tests ran at two eighty eight, three fifty four and four eighty eight words a minute, with five, four and three right. Their effective rates are two forty, two thirty six and two forty four. Median two forty: inside the baseline range.

@art fast-flat
Those readings felt faster, and they were faster in words. In understood words, they didn't move. Felt speed and effective rate come apart, and only the tests in the log can say which you've got.

@chapter Four habits
@art log-book
The log can only count what you let it. So there are four habits to keep for the rest of the Subject.

@list heading="Habits" items="Let every drill log its kind@log its kind|Make tests like the baselines@like the baselines|Read the verdict, not the latest rate@Read the verdict|Export it now and then@Export"
Let every drill log its kind, and don't repeat a drill to replace a bad reading. Make tests like the baselines: natural pace, a fresh passage, no pacer, six questions. Read the verdict, not the latest rate. And export it now and then.

@art export-file
The log lives in this browser only. Export as text writes it out as comma separated lines you can keep in a file. A cleared browser clears the log, and the export is the only copy. Clear my log asks for a second press before it deletes anything.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Practice that feels faster is faster@The first|Counting every reading@The second|Testing on a passage you've read@And the third"
The first slip is believing practice that feels faster is faster. The second is counting every reading. And the third is testing on a passage you've already read, which measures memory as well as reading. Deleting a low reading is a slip too: it makes every later verdict too generous.
