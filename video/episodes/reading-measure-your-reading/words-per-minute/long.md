@chapter The question
@title text="Words per Minute" sub="Reading Faster · Measure Your Reading" art=thumb
How fast do you read a story you've chosen to read? Not a story you're racing through, and not one you're studying. Just ordinary reading. The only honest way to find out is to time yourself.

@art stopwatch-page
So that's the drill. You press Start, read a four hundred and ninety one word story called The Church Clock at Wexley at the pace you'd read any story, and press Done the moment you reach the last word. Then you answer six questions.

@chapter Turning time into a rate
@math heading="Rate" items="rate = words × 60 / seconds@words times sixty;;491 × 60 / 120 = 245.5@two minutes"
The drill counts the words and times you to the millisecond. Your rate is the words, times sixty, divided by your seconds. If you took exactly two minutes, that's four ninety one times sixty over a hundred and twenty: two hundred and forty five point five words a minute.

@words text="Look only at the rate" sub="the questions come next lesson"
The questions were scored too, but leave them for now. The next lesson is about them. Here, look only at the rate.

@chapter Where does it sit?
@art bell-curve
Where does your number sit? Brysbaert, in twenty nineteen, pooled the studies of adult silent reading in English.

@list heading="Brysbaert (2019), in words a minute" items="Silent, nonfiction: about 238@nonfiction|Silent, fiction: about 260@fiction|Aloud: about 183@reading aloud"
He estimated about two hundred and thirty eight words a minute for nonfiction, and about two hundred and sixty for fiction. Reading aloud averages about a hundred and eighty three. Your passage is a story, so two sixty is the nearer comparison.

@words text="300 is an overestimate"
And the figure most people have heard, around three hundred? In Brysbaert's analysis, that's an overestimate. These are averages, with many readers on each side. A first reading below one is a starting point, not a verdict.

@chapter A second unit
@art long-short-words
Here's a catch. A rate in words depends on the words. A page of long words takes longer per word than a page of short ones, so two rates on two texts aren't really comparable.

@words text="Standard-length word = 6 characters" sub="letters, spaces and punctuation · Carver (1990)"
Carver, in nineteen ninety, fixed that with a standard length word: six character spaces of text, counting letters, spaces and punctuation together. A text's standard words are its characters divided by six.

@chapter A worked example
@math heading="Steady preset: 400 words, 2,640 characters, 96 s" items="400 × 60 / 96 = 250@four hundred times sixty;;2,640 / 6 = 440@divided by six;;440 × 60 / 96 = 275@standard words a minute"
Take a passage of four hundred words and two thousand six hundred and forty characters, a text of long words. A reader takes ninety six seconds. Four hundred times sixty over ninety six is two fifty words a minute. Divided by six, the characters give four hundred and forty standard words. So that's two seventy five standard words a minute.

@art two-dials
Same reading, two numbers. On long words, the standard rate is the higher one. The slow preset, the same passage in a hundred and sixty seconds, gives a hundred and fifty words a minute and a hundred and sixty five standard.

@math heading="The Church Clock at Wexley" items="2,652 / 6 = 442 standard words@four hundred and forty two;;442 × 60 / 120 = 221@two minutes"
Your story goes the other way. It has two thousand six hundred and fifty two characters, about five point four to a word, so it has four hundred and forty two standard words, fewer than its words. At two minutes, that's two hundred and twenty one.

@words text="Match the unit to the figure" sub="Brysbaert: words · Carver: standard words"
So which number do you use? It depends on what you compare it with. Brysbaert's averages are in words. Carver's figures, which a later lesson uses, are in standard length words.

@chapter The protocol
@art protocol-steps
For any timed reading later, use prose you have never seen, a few hundred words long. Read as you would by choice: not racing, not studying. That's the step people get wrong. Start at the first word and stop at the last word. Then write down both rates, with the date and the text.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking 300 is normal@The first|Comparing word rates across texts@The second|Reading for the clock@And the third"
The first slip is thinking most adults read three hundred, so you're slow. The second is comparing rates in words across different texts. And the third is reading for the clock. A running clock makes many readers hurry, and that measures something else.

@art baseline-mark
Your first reading is logged as a baseline. It's one measurement of one passage on one day, not your reading speed. The next lessons show how far it can move with nothing changed.
