@chapter The question
@title text="Typical Rates and Reading Gears" sub="Reading Faster · Measure Your Reading" art=thumb
You've got a baseline. Is it fast? Slow? That depends on what you compare it with, and in which unit. This lesson places your number twice: once against an average, and once against five reading gears.

@art two-rulers
The lab takes the median of your three baselines, in words a minute, and also in standard length words, from the characters you read. Then it sets the first beside Brysbaert's average, and the second beside Carver's gears.

@chapter Carver's five gears
@art five-gears
Carver, in nineteen ninety, described reading as five processes. Each has its own purpose, and its own typical rate for college students, measured in standard length words a minute.

@list heading="Carver's gears (standard words a minute)" items="Scanning, about 600@Scanning|Skimming, about 450@Skimming|Rauding, about 300@Rauding|Learning, about 200@Learning|Memorising, about 138@memorising"
Scanning, about six hundred: looking for one word or number. Skimming, about four fifty: getting the drift. Rauding, about three hundred: ordinary reading, understanding each sentence as you go. Learning, about two hundred: reading to keep what a text says. And memorising, about a hundred and thirty eight.

@art gear-ladder
Rauding is Carver's own word, made from reading and auding. It's the gear you're in when you read each sentence for its sense. The gears below it are slower, to keep what you read. The ones above are faster, and don't try to understand every sentence.

@chapter Convert first
@math heading="Average preset: 238 words a minute, 6.6 characters a word" items="238 × 6.6 / 6 = 261.8@six point six;;300 − 261.8 = 38.2@to rauding;;261.8 − 200 = 61.8@to learning;;nearest gear: rauding@nearest gear"
Take Brysbaert's average, two thirty eight words a minute, on text whose words average six point six characters with their space. In standard words, that's two thirty eight times six point six over six, two sixty one point eight. That's thirty eight point two to rauding, and sixty one point eight to learning. So the nearest gear is rauding.

@math heading="Short-words preset: same 238, 5.4 characters a word" items="238 × 5.4 / 6 = 214.2@five point four;;nearest gear: learning@becomes learning"
Now the same two thirty eight, on text like this course's passages, about five point four characters a word. Two thirty eight times five point four over six is two fourteen point two. And the nearest gear becomes learning.

@art same-reading
Nothing about the reading changed. The unit did. So read the nearest gear as a comparison of numbers, not as a description of what you were doing. If you were reading each sentence for its sense, you were rauding, at your own rate.

@chapter Two more readers
@list heading="Two presets" items="190 wpm → 209 standard · learning · about 79.8%@one ninety|1,000 wpm → 1,100 standard · above every gear@one thousand"
The slower preset is one ninety words a minute on the long word text, two oh nine standard. Nearest gear: learning, at about seventy nine point eight percent of the average. The claim preset is one thousand words a minute, eleven hundred standard. That's above every gear in Carver's table.

@chapter Do readers shift gear?
@art stuck-gear
If there are five gears, you'd think a good reader moves between them. But Carver, in nineteen ninety two, found that most readers don't. They hold a fairly constant rauding rate across what they read, and seldom shift gear.

@words text="Shift on purpose" sub="Carver (1992)"
So his advice was to shift deliberately, by purpose. Slow down to learn or memorise. Speed up to skim or scan. A later course, Shifting Gears, practises exactly that.

@chapter The routine
@list heading="Four moves" items="Count the characters@Count the characters|Convert before you compare with a gear@Convert|Compare words with words@words with words|Name the purpose@name the purpose"
Count the characters, spaces included, as well as the words. Convert to standard words before you compare with a gear. Compare words with words, against Brysbaert's two thirty eight for nonfiction or two sixty for fiction. And name the purpose. The gear is what you were reading for.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="One right speed, faster is better@The first|Words beside Carver's gears@The second|The nearest gear as a diagnosis@And the third"
The first slip is thinking there's one right speed, and faster is always better. Memorising is the slowest gear, and it's the right one when you need to recall a text. The second is putting a rate in words beside Carver's gears without converting. And the third is reading the nearest gear as a diagnosis.

@art short-words-page
Your own number may well land nearer learning than rauding on these passages, because their words are short. That's the unit at work. From here on, the comparison that matters is with your own baselines.

@chapter Try it

Your turn. A reader reads two fifty words a minute on text averaging five point four characters a word. What's the rate in standard words? Multiply by five point four, divide by six. You should get two twenty five.
