@chapter The puzzle
@title text="The Ship of Theseus" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
A wooden ship has a thousand planks. Each year, one is replaced. After a thousand years, none of the original planks remains. Is it still the same ship?

@art ship-planks
It seems to have been the same ship throughout. At no year did anyone say a different ship had appeared. But a ship with no original plank isn't the original ship, at least on the face of it. Both seem right, and they can't both be.

@chapter The chain
@math heading="F(k): with k original planks, still the original" items="F(1000)@starting ship;;F(1000) → F(999)@one plank;;F(1) → F(0)@all the way down;;∴ F(0)@The conclusion"
Write it as a chain. The starting ship has all its planks, F of a thousand. The tolerance claim, one plank doesn't matter, gives a conditional for each step: if F of a thousand, then F of nine hundred and ninety nine, and so on, all the way down to F of zero. The conclusion is F of zero.

@art plank-chain
That's the structure of a sorites, the same chain as the slippery slope. A thousand steps, and a thousand conditionals. And it's valid. If the conclusion is unacceptable, a premise or the logic has to go.

@chapter Three treatments
@words text="Classical: all 1000 true" sub="F(0) true: the all-new ship is the original"
Under the classical treatment, every conditional is true, and so is the conclusion. The ship with no original plank is the original. That's the first exit, and it's not as silly as it sounds. It says what makes a ship the same is continuity of repair, not material.

@art cutoff-plank
Under a cutoff, one conditional is false. With the cutoff at five hundred, the ship with five hundred original planks is the original, and the one with four hundred and ninety nine isn't. The conclusion is false. But nothing chose five hundred. Any number from one to a thousand is consistent. Picking one is making an input, not finding a fact.

@art fading-ship
The third treatment says identity comes in degrees.

@math heading="Degrees" items="each conditional:  999/1000@nine hundred and ninety nine;;chained 1000 times:  F(0) guaranteed 0@a thousand of them"
Under degrees, each conditional is almost true. Each loses one thousandth, so each has value nine hundred and ninety nine thousandths. And a thousand of them chained lose everything. The chain guarantees the all new ship none of the identity.

@chapter The reassembled ship
@art two-ships
Now the twist. The old planks, as they were removed, were stored and later reassembled in their old order. The reassembled ship has every original plank, and was never repaired. Now there are two candidates. They're two ships, so at most one is the original.

@list heading="Who keeps the title?" items="Classical: the repaired ship@Classically|Cutoff at 500: lost at one replacement@With a cutoff|Degrees: the repaired ship ends at 0@With degrees"
The reassembled ship isn't a step in the chain. What the lab computes is how much the repaired ship keeps. Classically, it keeps the whole title. With a cutoff, it loses the title at one replacement, and someone who thinks material matters gives it to the reassembled ship. With degrees, the repaired ship ends at zero, and the reassembled one has the only claim left.

@words text="Continuity of repair, or the parts?" sub="the philosophical question"
Which criterion should carry the weight, continuity of repair or the parts, is the philosophical question. The lab counts only the first. The arithmetic doesn't find the original ship. It shows what each treatment delivers.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Believing the arithmetic can find the original@The first|Calling the conclusion absurd, so the argument broken@The second|Thinking the reassembled ship settles it@And the third"
The first slip is believing there's a fact about the original that arithmetic can find. A cutoff at five hundred, or five hundred and one, is equally consistent. The second is calling the conclusion absurd, and the argument broken. The chain is valid, so something must go. And the third is thinking the reassembled ship settles it. If parts make the ship, every ship with a replaced plank is new.

@chapter Try it
@art rebuilt-hull
Run the thousand plank chain under all three treatments. Count the steps, then read off what classical, a cutoff at five hundred, and degrees each say about F of zero. Then decide what the reassembled ship shows, and which criterion you accept.
