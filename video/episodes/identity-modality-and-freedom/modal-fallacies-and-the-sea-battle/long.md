@chapter The fatalist's argument
@title text="Modal Fallacies and the Sea Battle" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
Here's an old argument for fatalism. Tomorrow, either there's a sea battle or there isn't. That's not up to anyone. It's a matter of logic, and logic is necessary.

@art sea-battle
So either it's necessary that there will be a battle, or necessary that there won't. If the first, nothing can prevent it. If the second, nothing can produce it. Either way, deliberating is idle. It sounds compelling. Let's find the step that fails.

@chapter Writing it down
@math heading="p: there will be a sea battle tomorrow" items="premise:  □(p ∨ ¬p)@The premise;;conclusion:  □p ∨ □¬p@The conclusion"
Write p for, there will be a sea battle tomorrow. The premise is box of p or not p: necessarily, either a battle or not. The conclusion the fatalist wants is box p, or box not p. Either a battle is necessary, or its absence is.

@words text="Where the box sits is part of what is said" sub="one box round the whole, or one on each side"
Look at where the box sits. The premise puts one box around the whole disjunction. The conclusion puts a box on each side. They're different sentences, and the second is much stronger.

@chapter The counterexample world
@art branching-futures
Build a model. World one is today. It sees world two, where the battle happens, and world three, where it doesn't. So p is true at world two and false at world three.

@math heading="At w1" items="p ∨ ¬p:  true at w2,  true at w3@at both;;□(p ∨ ¬p):  true@the premise is true;;□p:  false,  fails at w3@fails at world three;;□¬p:  false,  fails at w2@fails at world two;;□p ∨ □¬p:  false@the conclusion is false"
At world one, the premise asks whether p or not p holds at both seen worlds. It does at both, so the premise is true. The first disjunct of the conclusion asks whether p holds at both, and it fails at world three. The second asks whether not p holds at both, and it fails at world two. So the conclusion is false.

@words text="Premise true, conclusion false: invalid" sub="the counterexample is a world, not a row"
The premise holds and the conclusion fails, at one world. That's a counterexample, so the form is invalid, however natural the English sounds.

@chapter The scope fallacy
@art box-split
This is the modal scope fallacy. It moves from a box over a disjunction to a disjunction of boxes, treating the box as if it distributed over or. It distributes over and, but not over or.

@art settled-future
When is the conclusion true? If world one sees only worlds where the battle happens, box p is true, so the conclusion is true. That's a model of a settled future. And it's true for a reason unrelated to the premise. The seen worlds agree.

@words text="Read the value at w1" sub="a point about scope, not a theory of time"
Two features of the model need a word. Worlds two and three see nothing, so any box is vacuously true there. The value to read is the one at world one. And the model says nothing about whether the future has a truth value today. It's a point about a scope, not a theory of time.

@chapter What the fatalist can say instead
@art fixed-track
Take the repair at its strongest. The fatalist drops the disjunction, and asserts that what's true is already necessary: if p, then box p. If p is true today, and that makes it necessary, there's no open future.

@art open-future
But that's a different argument. Its premise is false at any world that has p and sees a world without it. Accepting the premise is accepting that the future is settled. Then the conclusion isn't argued for. It's assumed.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the necessary disjunction as a necessary disjunct@The first|Thinking an open future denies either-or@The second|Concluding that fatalism is refuted@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that necessarily, battle or not, entails that one of them is necessary. The second is thinking an open future requires denying that the battle either will or won't happen. The model keeps that true. And the third is concluding fatalism is refuted. One argument has failed. The premise that truth today makes the future necessary still needs judging.

@words text="w1 sees w2 (p) and w3 (¬p)" sub="then set p at both"
Rebuild the sea battle model. Evaluate the premise and the conclusion at world one, and name the shift. Then make p true at both seen worlds, a settled future, and evaluate both again.
