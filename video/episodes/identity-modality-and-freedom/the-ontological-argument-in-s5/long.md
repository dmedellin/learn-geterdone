@chapter The argument
@title text="The Ontological Argument in S5" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
There's a modern version of the ontological argument that's short, and valid in a standard modal logic. So the dispute isn't over the reasoning. It's over a premise, and over the choice of logic. Here's how to see exactly where.

@art great-being
A being is maximally great if it's as excellent as a being can be, in every possible world. Such a being, if there is one, exists necessarily. Otherwise there'd be a world where it's missing, and it wouldn't be maximally great there. So claiming there could be such a being is claiming it's possible that something exists necessarily.

@math heading="G: a maximally great being exists" items="premise:  ◇□G@The first premise;;logic:  ◇□G → □G@The second;;conclusion:  □G@the conclusion"
Write G for, a maximally great being exists. The first premise is diamond box G: possibly, necessarily G. The second is a fact about the logic: if diamond box G, then box G. And the conclusion is box G.

@chapter Why it holds in S5
@art euclidean-arcs
The second line is valid on a frame exactly when the frame is euclidean. If a world sees two worlds, the first of them sees the second. Suppose w sees v, where box G holds, and w sees u. Euclidean says v sees u, and box G at v gives G at u. So every world w sees has G, and box G holds at w.

@words text="Reflexive + euclidean = S5" sub="every world sees every other in its group"
A frame that's reflexive and euclidean is an equivalence relation, where every world sees every other in its group. That's S five, and on it the conditional holds.

@chapter A frame where it fails
@art missing-return-arc
Weaken S five by one property. Let world one see itself and world two, and let world two see only itself. That's reflexive and transitive, the system S four, but not symmetric. Put G at world two only.

@math heading="Without the return arc" items="□G at w2:  true@At world two;;◇□G at w1:  true@At world one;;□G at w1:  false,  G fails at w1@But box G;;conditional at w1:  false@So the conditional"
At world two the box holds, since the only world it sees has G. At world one, diamond box G is true, because world one sees world two. But box G is false at world one, since it sees itself, and G is false there. So the conditional fails.

@art return-arc-added
Add the missing arc from world two back to world one. Now world two sees a world without G, so box G fails there. The antecedent fails at world one too, and the conditional is true. It's not that the conclusion became true. The premise became false. That's the sense in which S five does the work.

@chapter What the argument costs
@math heading="The parallel premise" items="◇□¬G → □¬G@the same logic;;◇□G and ◇□¬G:  not both, in S5@both premises"
There are two exits. The first is to deny the premise. The same logic proves, if diamond box not G, then box not G. In S five, both premises can't be true at one world. So granting it's possible God necessarily exists isn't a modest concession. It carries the conclusion with it.

@art two-exits
The second exit is to reject S five as the logic of the necessity in question. Metaphysical necessity may not be symmetric, for example. Then the argument has no valid form to stand on.

@art backwards-argument
The lab can't choose for you. It shows the argument is valid on one frame and invalid on another. Which frame suits God, or necessity, or the word possible, comes after the logic, not instead of it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Believing the argument is invalid@The first|Treating the conditional as a claim about God@The second|Taking a valid argument as a proof of its conclusion@And the third"
The first slip is believing the argument is invalid. In S five, it's valid. The second is treating the conditional as a claim about God. It holds for any sentence at all. And the third is taking a valid argument as a proof. You can run it backwards: if box G is false, so is diamond box G.

@chapter Try it
@words text="w1 → w1, w2;  w2 → w2;  G at w2" sub="then add w2 → w1"
Rework the frame without symmetry. Evaluate box G at world two, diamond box G at world one, and box G at world one. Then add the arc from world two to world one, and say which part of the conditional changed.
