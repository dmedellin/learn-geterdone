@chapter The worry
@title text="Free Will, Determinism and Compatibility" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
Hard determinism, libertarianism, and compatibilism are usually presented as rival answers. But written as three sentences, they're simply the three ways to delete one of them. Let's see how.

@art causal-chain
Determinism is the thesis that the state of the world at one time, together with the laws of nature, entails the state at every later time. It's a thesis about entailment. It says nothing about compulsion, or what anyone feels.

@art raised-hand
Now take free will in its ordinary sense. When I raise my hand, it's up to me, and I could have kept it down. The old worry is that these don't fit. If the past and the laws entail everything, my raising my hand was already entailed. It doesn't look as if it was up to me.

@chapter The triad
@math heading="Three sentences" items="1.  d:  determinism is true@One, d;;2.  f:  some people act freely@Two, f;;3.  d → ¬f:  incompatibilism@Three"
Write the worry as a conditional, beside the two claims it threatens. One, d: determinism is true. Two, f: some people act freely. Three, if d, then not f. That third one is incompatibilism, and it's the one in dispute.

@art three-way-clash
The lab finds no model for the three. From one and three comes not f, and sentence two is f. The smallest inconsistent subset is the whole set, so every sentence is needed for the clash, and each is a place to give way.

@chapter Three positions
@list heading="Three deletions" items="Drop f: hard determinism@hard determinist|Drop d: libertarianism@libertarian|Drop the conditional: compatibilism@compatibilist"
The hard determinist drops f, and accepts that no one acts freely. The libertarian drops d, and holds that some choices aren't entailed by the past and the laws. The compatibilist drops the conditional.

@art three-costs
Each pays. The first gives up praise and blame, or has to reinterpret them. The second needs causes that aren't entailed, which physics may or may not supply. And the third has to say what freedom is, given that it isn't the ability to do otherwise whatever the past.

@chapter What drops the conditional
@math heading="The compatibilist set" items="a:  she acts on her own desires@Let a be;;o:  she could have done otherwise, whatever the past@and o be;;d,  f,  f ↔ a,  d → ¬o@The compatibilist set;;a model:  d true,  f true@finds a model"
The compatibilist can't just deny the conditional. It's plausible on one reading of f. So redefine free will. Let a be, she acts on her own desires, and o be, she could have done otherwise, whatever the past. The compatibilist set is d, f, f if and only if a, and if d then not o. The lab finds a model, with d true and f true.

@words text="Compatibilism keeps determinism" sub="it changes what f means"
So compatibilism doesn't need determinism to be false anywhere. It holds both claims at once, which is why it's called what it is.

@art meaning-switch
The cost is on the other side. If the incompatibilist insists free will does require the ability to do otherwise, then f if and only if o replaces f if and only if a. With if d then not o, the set is inconsistent again. The dispute has moved from the logic to the meaning of f. A consistent set doesn't tell you the compatibilist's a is the freedom anyone wanted. It tells you the position is coherent.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking compatibilism denies determinism@The first|Treating a determined act as a compelled act@The second|Believing consistency is a win for the compatibilist@And the third"
The first slip is thinking compatibilism denies determinism. Determinism is the thing it keeps. The second is treating a determined act as a compelled one. Compulsion is a fact about a particular act, like one made under threat, or against your own desires. An act can be determined, and uncompelled. And the third is believing consistency is a win. It shows only that the position is coherent.

@chapter Try it
@art compelled-cage
Work through the triad. Drop each sentence in turn, read the model, and name the position. Then swap in the compatibilist definition of f, acting on your own desires, and then the other one, the ability to do otherwise, and watch the clash disappear and return. Finally, say what each position pays, and whether you'd pay it.
