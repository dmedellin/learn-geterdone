@chapter Locke's criterion
@title text="Personal Identity and Psychological Continuity" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
What makes you, now, the same person as a child years ago? Locke proposed that a person is the same person as far back as their consciousness extends. And the plainest evidence of that reach is memory. If I remember doing something, I'm the one who did it.

@chapter Reid's brave officer
@art boy-officer-general
Thomas Reid answered with a case. A brave officer, in his first campaign, took a standard from the enemy. As a boy he'd been flogged for robbing an orchard. When he took the standard, he remembered the flogging. Late in life, as a general, he remembers taking the standard, and has quite lost the flogging.

@list heading="Who remembers whom" items="Officer remembers boy: yes@the officer is the boy|General remembers officer: yes@the general is the officer|General remembers boy: no@the general is not"
So by Locke's criterion, the officer is the boy, since he remembers being flogged. The general is the officer, since he remembers the standard. And the general is not the boy, since he remembers nothing of the orchard.

@words text="Identity is transitive" sub="so the criterion has contradicted itself"
But identity is transitive. If the general is the officer, and the officer is the boy, the general is the boy. A criterion that says all three things has contradicted itself.

@chapter Connected and continuous
@art direct-ties
The repair is to separate two relations. Two stages are psychologically connected when there's a direct tie, the kind memory gives. They're psychologically continuous when there's a chain of stages from one to the other, each connected to the next. Continuity is the transitive closure of connectedness.

@words text="Boy and general: continuous, not connected" sub="the officer stands between them"
Now let the tie be as weak as Reid likes. The general and the boy aren't connected, but they are continuous, since the officer stands between them. Make continuity the criterion, and the contradiction is gone. The three are one person, and the general needn't remember the boy.

@chapter Five stages
@art stage-grid
The lab draws this on five stages. Read a pair, a and b, as stage b directly remembers stage a. Take the pattern where each stage remembers only the one just before it. The grid holds four pairs.

@math heading="Each stage remembers the one before" items="pairs:  (1, 2),  (2, 3),  (3, 4),  (4, 5)@The pairs are;;(1, 2) and (2, 3),  but no (1, 3)@One two and two three;;not transitive@not transitive"
The pairs are one two, two three, three four, and four five. The property table finds the failing triple at once. One two and two three are in the relation, and one three isn't. So it's not transitive. That's Reid's finding, on five stages.

@art filled-closure
Now take the transitive closure. The lab marks six more cells, from one three up to three five. With the original four, that's ten pairs, every pair with a less than b. Every stage is continuous with every other. The closure doesn't invent a memory. It adds the pairs a chain of memories supplies.

@chapter What the repair leaves open
@art faint-link
Two limits. First, the chain needs enough strength at each link. A link so faint that nothing remains is no tie at all, and the lab has only a pair or no pair, where a real tie comes in degrees. 

@art forked-chain
Second, continuity doesn't say the chain is unique. If one stage could be continuous with two later stages, which aren't continuous with each other, a person would be divided. Identity is one to one, so it can't follow both branches. That's the next lesson.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Believing memory connects every stage to every other@The first|Thinking identity depends on how many links there are@The second|Assuming the closure settles personal identity@And the third"
The first slip is believing memory connects every stage of a life to every other. A life is joined by overlapping memories. The second is thinking the chain criterion depends on how many links there are. A longer chain is no weaker. What can weaken is a link. And the third is assuming the closure settles personal identity. It settles Reid's contradiction, and only that.

@chapter Try it
@words text="remove (3, 4)" sub="then read the closure"
Try it in the lab. Start with five stages, each remembering the one before, and take the transitive closure. Then remove the pair three four, and read the closure again. You should find two chains, stages one to three, and stages four and five.
