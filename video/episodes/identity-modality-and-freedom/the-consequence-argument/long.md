@chapter The argument
@title text="The Consequence Argument" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
If determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the laws of nature and the remote past. And no one has power over those. This is the strongest case an incompatibilist can offer. And it turns out to be valid.

@art distant-past
Let h be the state of the world long before anyone was born, l the laws of nature, and a some act of mine tomorrow. Suppose determinism: the remote past and the laws entail the act.

@list heading="Three lines" items="No one has power over the past and the laws@Nobody has any power over the remote|No one has power over their entailing the act@over the fact|So no one has power over the act@So nobody"
The argument runs in three lines. Nobody has any power over the remote past and the laws. They were fixed before anyone could act. Nobody has any power over the fact that they entail the act. That's just what the laws are. So nobody has any power over the act.

@chapter The transfer rule
@math heading="N: no one has, or ever had, power over whether" items="premise:  N(h ∧ l)@The premises;;premise:  N((h ∧ l) → a)@and N of;;conclusion:  N a@the conclusion"
Write N for, no one has, or ever had, any power over whether. The premises are N of h and l, and N of h and l implies a. The conclusion is N of a. Peter van Inwagen called the step the transfer of powerlessness.

@art open-worlds
Read N as a box. A world w sees another world when that world is still open, given what anyone can do. Then the transfer rule is box p, and box of p implies q, together imply box q. That's the K axiom.

@words text="□p ∧ □(p → q) → □q" sub="valid on every frame"
And K is valid on every frame. If p holds at every seen world, and p implies q does too, then q holds at each of them. Nothing was assumed about the arcs. So the argument is valid, given the reading of its box.

@chapter Two premises to push
@art miracle-world
To resist, you have three choices. The first denies that the past and laws are beyond everyone's power. On David Lewis's compatibilism, had I done otherwise, a small miracle would have broken the laws shortly before. I can't cause it, but I can act so that, were I to, it would have occurred. In the model, world one sees a world where the laws fail.

@art undetermined-world
The second choice denies that the past and laws entail the act. That's denying determinism. In the model, every seen world keeps the past and the laws, and in one of them the act doesn't happen. The second premise is false. That's the libertarian's move.

@chapter Is N a box?
@words text="Every box agglomerates" sub="from □p and □q comes □(p ∧ q)"
The third choice is subtler. It says no one has power over doesn't behave like a box. Every box agglomerates. From box p and box q, you get box of p and q. N has been argued not to.

@art untossed-coin
A coin I could toss lies untossed. I have no power over whether it fails to land heads, since even tossing it can't make it land heads. Same for tails. But I do have power over whether it fails to do both, since tossing it would make one happen. If that's right, N isn't a box, and K doesn't apply.

@art transfer-chain
The argument's defender has replied by redefining N, so it is a box after all. Then the two premises have to be argued for under that definition. So the logic is secure. Whether the argument has the logic's shape is a claim about meaning.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Escaping by denying the logic@The first|Taking validity to show the premises true@The second|Drawing N's arcs to every world there is@And the third"
The first slip is thinking you can escape by denying the logic. K holds for every box. The second is taking the validity of the step to show the premises are true. And the third is drawing the arcs of N to every possible world. Then the first premise fails before anyone has argued. The arcs are narrower: worlds someone could still bring about.

@chapter Try it
@math heading="w1 sees w1, w2" items="h ∧ l at both:  □(h ∧ l) true@the past and laws;;a at both:  □a true@the act;;add w3 without a:  □((h ∧ l) → a) fails@add a world"
Rework the lesson's model. World one sees itself and world two, the past and laws hold in both, and so does the act. Check both premises and the conclusion. Then add a world without the act, and see which premise fails.
