@chapter The problem
@title text="The Problem of Evil as an Inconsistent Set" sub="Identity, Modality and Freedom" art=thumb
That God is all powerful, all knowing, and wholly good. That evil exists. And that a being like that eliminates evil. These can't all be true. That's the logical problem of evil, and it can be written down exactly.

@art five-pillars
J. L. Mackie's version had three claims, joined by two principles he called quasi logical. Later statements settle on five sentences. They add omniscience, since a being that couldn't know of an evil couldn't be faulted for leaving it. And they fold the principles into one bridge.

@chapter Five sentences
@math heading="The set" items="o:  God is omnipotent@o, God is omnipotent;;k:  God is omniscient@k, God is omniscient;;g:  God is wholly good@g, God is wholly good;;e:  evil exists@e, evil exists;;(o ∧ k ∧ g) → ¬e@the bridge"
Here they are. o, God is omnipotent. k, God is omniscient. g, God is wholly good. e, evil exists. And the bridge: if o and k and g, then not e. Such a being eliminates evil.

@art clash
From the first three, the bridge gives not e, which contradicts e. The lab confirms no row satisfies all five. And the smallest inconsistent subset is the whole set. Drop any one, and a model appears.

@chapter The deletions
@list heading="Each deletion is a position" items="Drop o or k: a limited God@a God who is limited|Drop g: a God who is not good@isn't good|Drop e: evil is an illusion@an illusion|Drop the bridge: keep everything else@the interesting move"
Dropping o or k gives a God who is limited in power or knowledge. Dropping g gives a God who isn't good. Dropping e says evil is an illusion, which most readers think denies a fact. And dropping the bridge is the interesting move. It keeps every attribute, and the fact.

@chapter The free-will defence
@art free-creatures
The bridge isn't a logical truth. It's plausible only if a good being could have no reason for permitting some evil. The free will defence supplies one. Perhaps a world with free creatures is better than one without, and any free creature might misuse its freedom. Then some evil is necessary for the greater good.

@math heading="The weakened bridge" items="u:  there is evil not necessary for any greater good@Write u;;(o ∧ k ∧ g) → ¬u@The weakened bridge;;a model:  o, k, g, e true,  u false@finds a model"
Write u for, there's evil that isn't necessary for any greater good. The weakened bridge is, if o and k and g, then not u. Such a being eliminates the unnecessary evil. And with that bridge, the lab finds a model, with o, k, g and e true, and u false.

@art possible-story
That's all a defence claims. Alvin Plantinga's version doesn't assert the story about freedom is true, or even likely. Only that it's possible. And a possible way for all the sentences to hold is exactly what a model is. It shows the contradiction isn't forced. It doesn't show that God exists.

@chapter The evidential problem
@art forest-fire
This is where the evidential problem enters. Instead of the bridge, it asserts u. There are evils, like the suffering of an animal in a forest fire no one sees, that look as if no greater good required them. Add u to the weakened bridge and the attributes, and there's no model again.

@words text="Support for u is an estimate" sub="the lab does not compute it"
The logic of the clash is the same as before. What differs is the support for u. It's an estimate of how likely it is, given what we can see, and that's exactly what the lab doesn't compute. The reply that we're poorly placed to see what an all knowing being would see is a reply to the estimate, not the logic.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the problem is only probabilistic@The first|Thinking a defence shows God exists@The second|Treating the bridge as a logical truth@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the problem of evil is only a probabilistic argument. The logical problem has no probabilities in it. The second is thinking a defence shows God exists, or that evil is justified. It shows consistency, nothing more. And the third is treating the bridge as a logical truth. It's an assumption about what a good being does.
