@chapter The question
@title text="Stock Lent at Interest" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 4" art=thumb
When someone lends you money, what have they actually given you? It sounds obvious. Smith thinks the obvious answer is wrong, and his answer explains why interest falls and why land gets dearer.

@quote text="He expects that in due time it is to be restored to him, and that, in the mean time, the borrower is to pay him a certain annual rent for the use of it." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 4"
To the lender, it's a capital. He expects that in due time it is to be restored to him, and that, in the mean time, the borrower is to pay him a certain annual rent for the use of it.

@words text="Capital, or a prodigal's spending"
The borrower can use it as capital too, on productive labourers who reproduce its value with a profit. Or he can spend it, and act the part of a prodigal. Smith thinks the first is much commoner than people suppose.

@chapter The money's worth
@art deed-and-goods
Here's the key point. Almost all loans are made in money, but what the borrower really wants isn't the money. It's the money's worth, the goods it can buy. The lender assigns him a right to a portion of the year's produce. The money is only the deed of assignment.

@chapter One coin, three loans
@art three-loans
That's why the stock lent isn't set by the quantity of coin. A lends a thousand pounds to W, who buys goods from B. B doesn't need the money, so he lends the same pieces to X, who buys from C. And C lends them to Y.

@math heading="The same pieces, three times" items="3 loans · £1,000 = £3,000 lent@three loans;;coin used: £1,000@coin"
Three loans of a thousand pounds each, on the same thousand pounds of coin. The stock lent is three thousand, three times greater than the money the purchases were made with.

@chapter Why interest falls
@art competing-capitals
Now the rate. As the stock to be lent increases, the interest paid for it diminishes. There's a reason peculiar to this case. As capitals grow, their owners compete for employments and for labourers. Their competition raises the wages of labour, and sinks the profits of stock. Interest is paid out of profit, so it falls too.

@art falling-rate
So the rate falls as stock grows, and wages rise with it. Locke, Law and Montesquieu had another explanation. They thought the discovery of the Spanish West Indies lowered interest by making gold and silver plentiful.

@math heading="If silver halved in value" items="£100 now = £50 then@a hundred pounds;;£10 now = £5 then@ten pounds;;10% → 5%: interest worth one fourth@one fourth"
Smith answers with numbers. If a hundred pounds are now worth what fifty were, then ten pounds are worth what five were. The capital and its interest halve together, and the rate stays put. Cutting the rate from ten to five per cent would give an interest worth only one fourth of the old one. The cause has to be more stock.

@chapter Interest and the law
@art rate-cap
Smith's view of usury laws isn't the simple one. A ban increases usury, because something can everywhere be made by the use of money. Where interest is allowed, a legal maximum ought always to be somewhat above the lowest market price.

@list heading="Where to fix it" items="At the lowest price: shuts out all but the best security@At the lowest|At eight or ten per cent: money goes to prodigals and projectors@eight or ten|Britain: five per cent, perhaps as proper as any@five per cent"
At the lowest price, it ruins the credit of everyone who can't give the very best security, and sends them to exorbitant usurers. At eight or ten per cent, most of the money goes to prodigals and projectors. In Britain, with good security at four or four and a half, five per cent is perhaps as proper as any.

@chapter The price of land
@math heading="Years' purchase" items="years = 100 / rate@a hundred divided;;at 10%: 100 / 10 = 10 · price 100 · 10 = 1,000@ten per cent;;at 5%: 100 / 5 = 20 · price 100 · 20 = 2,000@five per cent"
Last, land. A years' purchase is how many years of rent a buyer pays. Price land like a loan, and it's a hundred divided by the rate. Take a rent of a hundred pounds, written for the lesson. At ten per cent, ten years, so a thousand pounds. At five per cent, twenty years, so two thousand. Halve the rate, double the price.

@art estate-price
Smith's own figures sit above that bare rule at low rates. At six per cent he gives twenty years where the rule gives sixteen and two thirds. At four, thirty where the rule gives twenty five. He puts the gap down to land's superior security, which makes a buyer content with a smaller revenue.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a lender lends money@The first|Explaining low rates by plentiful gold@The second|Thinking a low legal rate protects borrowers@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a lender lends money, rather than a claim on goods. The second is explaining a low rate by plentiful gold and silver. And the third is thinking a low legal rate protects borrowers. Bentham, writing to Smith, argued the law should leave the bargain alone entirely.
