@chapter The claim
@title text="Parsimony and the Prodigal" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 3" art=thumb
What makes a nation's capital grow? Most of us would say hard work. Smith gives a different answer, and it's worth seeing exactly why.

@quote text="Capitals are increased by parsimony, and diminished by prodigality and misconduct." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 3"
Capitals are increased by parsimony, and diminished by prodigality and misconduct. Whatever a person saves from his revenue he adds to his capital, and either employs it himself on productive hands, or lends it to someone who will.

@chapter Parsimony, not industry
@quote text="Parsimony, and not industry, is the immediate cause of the increase of capital." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 3"
Then comes the line readers quote. Parsimony, and not industry, is the immediate cause of the increase of capital.

@art harvest-eaten
But Smith doesn't leave industry out. Industry provides what parsimony accumulates. Think of a farmer with a good harvest who eats the whole of it. He's been industrious, and his capital is the same as last year's. Without the saving, the capital would never be the greater.

@chapter Saving isn't hoarding
@art two-tables
Here's the part a modern reader may doubt. What's saved each year is consumed as regularly as what's spent, and nearly as fast. It's just consumed by different people. The spender feeds idle guests and servants. The saver sets labourers to work who reproduce the value of what they consume, with a profit.

@words text="The consumption is the same, but the consumers are different."
Smith's saver doesn't put money under a mattress. What he saves is employed as capital at once, by him or by a borrower.

@art perpetual-fund
And he takes a longer view. The frugal man, like the founder of a public workhouse, sets up a perpetual fund for productive hands in all times to come. The prodigal encroaches on his capital, and pays the wages of idleness with the funds his forefathers set aside for industry.

@chapter Ten years
@math heading="£1,000 at 10%, half saved" items="factor = 1 + 1/10 · 1/2 = 21/20@add one;;1,000 · 21/20 · 21/20 = 1,102.50@two years;;ten years: ≈ 1,628.89 · ≈ 65 hands@ten years"
Let's run a decade, with figures written for the lesson: a thousand pounds, ten per cent a year, twenty five pounds to keep a hand. Saving half, multiply the return by the half, and add one. That's twenty one twentieths. After two years, one thousand one hundred and two fifty. After ten years, about sixteen twenty eight eighty nine, and about sixty five hands.

@math heading="The prodigal" items="factor = 1 − 1/10 · 1/2 = 19/20@minus a half;;1,000 · 19/20 · 19/20 = 902.50@two years;;ten years: ≈ 598.74 · fewer than 24 hands@a decade"
The prodigal spends his whole revenue and half as much again out of capital. That's a share of minus a half, so the factor is nineteen twentieths. After two years, nine hundred and two fifty. After a decade, about five hundred and ninety eight seventy four, and fewer than twenty four hands.

@art diverging-paths
The gap between the two paths is about forty one hands. And notice the method. Each year multiplies last year's capital. You don't add the saving to the original thousand each time, because the saving earns a return too.

@chapter Public enemy, public benefactor
@words text="Two values instead of one"
Doesn't the prodigal's money stay in the country, if he buys home made goods? Smith answers in values. What the idle consumed should have kept productive hands, who'd reproduce its value with a profit. There would have been two values instead of one. So every prodigal is a public enemy, and every frugal man a public benefactor.

@chapter Private and public
@art public-purse
Then the reassurance. A great nation is seldom much affected by private prodigality, because the desire of bettering our condition is steadier than the passion for present enjoyment. Great nations are never impoverished by private, though they sometimes are by public prodigality. Courts, fleets and armies produce nothing that repays them.

@words text="Four French wars · more than £145,000,000 of debt"
He counts the cost. In four French wars England contracted more than a hundred and forty five million pounds of debt, and the whole cost couldn't be computed at less than two hundred million. Yet England still grew richer. Kings and ministers, he says, are themselves the greatest spendthrifts in the society.

@art lasting-house
Even a rich man's spending can lean one way. A table and servants are consumed at once. A house, books and pictures last, and pass down. What was once a seat of the Seymour family is now an inn upon the Bath road.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading parsimony as hoarding@The first|Blaming the private prodigal@The second|Thinking industry doesn't matter@And the third"
The first slip is reading parsimony as hoarding. The second is thinking Smith blames the private prodigal for national decline. He says the frugality of others compensates. And the third is thinking industry doesn't matter. It provides what is saved. Malthus and Keynes later disputed whether every saving finds an employer.

@chapter Try it
@words text="£2,000 at 10% · half saved" sub="What is the capital after one year?"
Your turn. A capital of two thousand pounds returns ten per cent a year, and the owner saves half the revenue. What is the capital after one year? Then try a second year, multiplying, not adding.
