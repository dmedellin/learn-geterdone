@chapter The question
@title text="Productive and Unproductive Labour" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Two people work hard all day. One makes something in a workshop. The other serves a household. Smith says only one of them is productive. That sounds harsh, so let's see what he actually means.

@quote text="The former as it produces a value, may be called productive, the latter, unproductive labour." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 3"
He starts with a definition. One sort of labour adds to the value of the thing it works on. Another has no such effect. The former as it produces a value, may be called productive, the latter, unproductive labour.

@chapter A workman and a servant
@art workbench-and-tray
His first example is a manufacturer's workman and a menial servant. The master advances the workman's wages, but he isn't out of pocket. The wages come back, with a profit, in the improved value of the thing the workman made. The servant's keep never comes back.

@words text="Rich by manufacturers, poor by servants"
Hence the sentence people remember. A man grows rich by employing a multitude of manufacturers, and poor by keeping a multitude of menial servants.

@chapter Not a judgement of worth
@quote text="The labour of the latter, however, has its value, and deserves its reward as well as that of the former." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 3"
But Smith isn't calling the servant's work worthless. The labour of the latter, however, has its value, and deserves its reward as well as that of the former.

@art stored-labour
The difference is in what's left. The workman's labour fixes itself in a vendible commodity. It's a quantity of labour stocked and stored up, because the price of the thing can later buy an equal quantity of labour. The servant's services perish in the very instant they're performed.

@chapter The uncomfortable list
@art crowned-and-masked
Then Smith makes the list uncomfortable. The sovereign, the officers of justice and war, the whole army and navy are unproductive. So are churchmen, lawyers, physicians, men of letters, and with them players, buffoons, musicians and opera singers.

@words text="Does something remain to be exchanged later?"
What joins the judge and the buffoon? The work of each perishes in the very instant of its production. However honourable or necessary the service, it leaves nothing behind that could later buy an equal quantity of labour. That's the whole test.

@chapter Capital and revenue
@art two-funds
Now the two funds. A year's produce divides in two. One part replaces a capital, and Smith says it pays the wages of productive labour only. The other part is revenue, as profit or rent, and its owner may keep either sort of hand on it.

@math heading="A capital of £1,000" items="revenue = 1,000 · 1/10 = 100@ten per cent;;hands = 1,000 / 25 = 40@twenty five pounds"
Put numbers on it. These figures were written for the lesson, not taken from Smith. A capital of a thousand pounds returns ten per cent a year, so its revenue is a hundred pounds. If a year's keep of one productive hand is twenty five pounds, the capital maintains forty.

@chapter Three owners
@math heading="Two years on" items="all spent: 1,000 → 1,000 · 40 hands@Spend the whole;;half saved: 1,050 → 1,102.50 · 42 → 44.1@Save half;;all saved: 1,100 → 1,210 · 44 → 48.4@Save it all"
Spend the whole hundred on servants, and the capital stays at a thousand, with forty hands. Save half, and it grows to one thousand and fifty, then one thousand one hundred and two fifty: forty two hands, then forty four point one. Save it all, one thousand one hundred, then twelve hundred and ten: forty four, then forty eight point four.

@art growing-capital
Notice that the owner who spends his revenue doesn't impoverish himself. His capital is right where it was. He just doesn't add to it. Every pound saved is a pound added to the fund for productive hands.

@chapter Industry and idleness
@art town-and-court
Smith then reads character from the proportion between the two funds, and this is the most arguable part. Wherever capital predominates, industry prevails. Wherever revenue, idleness. Towns that live by capital, he says, are industrious, sober and thriving. Towns that live on a court tend to be idle, dissolute and poor.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading unproductive as useless@The first|Thinking spending revenue shrinks the capital@The second|Taking it as the last word@And the third"
The first slip is reading unproductive as useless or disreputable. The second is thinking that spending the revenue shrinks the capital. And the third is taking this as the last word. Say counted services as products, Marx changed the test, and modern accounts count services as output.
