@chapter The question
@title text="The Four Ways of Employing Capital" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 5" art=thumb
Put the same thousand pounds into a farm, a factory, a warehouse or a shop. Each one is productive. But does each set the same amount of labour going? Smith says no, and he ranks them.

@art four-employments
A capital can be employed in four ways. First, in raising the rude produce. Second, in manufacturing it. Third, in transporting it from where it abounds to where it's wanted. And last, in dividing it into small parcels. Those are the capitals of farmers, manufacturers, wholesale merchants and retailers.

@chapter A defence of shopkeepers
@art whole-ox
Each of the four is necessary to the others, and Smith takes trouble to defend the last. Without retailers, you'd have to buy a whole ox or a whole sheep at a time. A poor workman buying months of provisions at once would have to pull stock out of his own trade.

@words text="Twenty grocers, not two"
The prejudices against shopkeepers, he says, are altogether without foundation. Competition is the check. With twenty grocers instead of two, they're less likely to combine and raise the price. Their rivalry might ruin some of them, but it won't hurt the buyer.

@chapter The ranking
@quote text="The retailer himself is the only productive labourer whom it immediately employs." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 5"
Now the ranking. Equal capitals set very different quantities of productive labour going. Start at the bottom. The retailer himself is the only productive labourer whom it immediately employs.

@list heading="From the bottom up" items="Retail: the retailer himself@Start|Wholesale: sailors and carriers@The wholesale|Manufacture: a great part paid to workmen@manufacturer|Agriculture: servants, cattle and Nature@farmer"
Start with the shop, which employs the shopkeeper. The wholesale merchant's capital also employs sailors and carriers. The manufacturer spends some on instruments, but a great part goes to his workmen. And at the top, the farmer. Not only his labouring servants, but his labouring cattle, are productive labourers.

@chapter Nature works too
@art nature-works
Why is the farm first? Because in farming, Nature labours along with man. Her share is the landlord's rent, the work that remains after everything done by man is deducted. Smith says it's seldom less than a fourth, and frequently more than a third, of the whole produce. In manufactures, Nature does nothing, and man does all.

@chapter Counting the hands
@math heading="£1,000 in each, £25 a hand" items="farm: 700 / 25 = 28@seven hundred;;factory: 400 / 25 = 16@four hundred;;wholesale: 100 / 25 = 4@a hundred;;retail: 25 / 25 = 1@twenty five pounds"
Let's count. Smith gives the order but no figures, so these shares were written for the lesson to follow his order. Of a thousand pounds, the farm pays seven hundred to productive hands, so twenty eight. The factory, four hundred, sixteen. The warehouse, a hundred, four. The shop, twenty five pounds, one.

@art hands-bars
That's twenty eight, sixteen, four and one, in Smith's order. The most to the least is a ratio of twenty eight. But be careful what that shows. It's computed from shares written to follow his order, so it can't test the ranking.

@chapter The order is a premise
@art bars-reversed
The second preset makes the point. Give the factory heavy machinery that leaves only a hundred and fifty pounds for wages, and the wholesale trade a coal fleet with three hundred for sailors. Now it's six hands to twelve, and the order of those two reverses. The order is a premise about the shares.

@art wandering-capital
Smith draws consequences. Farm and shop capitals stay where they work, while a wholesale merchant's capital may wander from place to place. A country short of capital should take the employments in his order. The American colonies grew fast, he says, because almost their whole capitals went into agriculture.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the ranking as a payroll count@The first|Taking the count as a test of the order@The second|Reading it as advice to farm@And the third"
The first slip is reading the ranking as a count of the payroll. The second is taking the lab's count as a test of Smith's order. And the third is reading most advantageous to society as advice to farm. Owners choose by profit alone. Ricardo and List later disputed the premise and its use.

Your turn. An employment lays out five hundred pounds a year in wages to the hands it directly employs, and a hand costs twenty five pounds a year to keep. How many hands does it set going?
