@chapter The question
@title text="The Home, the Foreign and the Carrying Trade" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 5" art=thumb
Two merchants each have a thousand pounds. One trades between two English towns. The other trades with Portugal. Smith claims the first can support twenty four times as much home industry. Where does a number like that come from?

@list heading="Three sorts of wholesale trade" items="The home trade@the home trade|The foreign trade of consumption@the foreign trade|The carrying trade@carrying trade"
Wholesale trade comes in three sorts. There's the home trade, buying in one part of the country and selling in another. There's the foreign trade of consumption, buying foreign goods for use at home. And there's the carrying trade, moving one foreign country's surplus to another.

@chapter Two capitals or one
@art two-home-capitals
The first factor. A home trade, buying in one part of the country to sell in another, replaces two capitals each time, both employed in the farms or manufactures of that country.

@art one-and-one
Now send British goods to Portugal and bring Portuguese goods back. That replaces only one British capital. The other is a Portuguese one. So even if the returns were just as quick, the foreign trade would give only half the encouragement to home industry.

@chapter How often it returns
@art fast-and-slow
The second factor is speed. Home returns usually come in before the end of the year, sometimes three or four times a year. Foreign returns seldom come in within a year, and sometimes not for two or three. So a home capital may be sent out and returned twelve times before a foreign one has made one.

@math heading="Four-and-twenty to one" items="4 / (1/3) = 12@four returns;;12 · 2 = 24@twice as many"
Four returns a year, against one in three years, is a ratio of twelve. And each home return replaces twice as many home capitals. Twelve times two is Smith's four and twenty.

@chapter Counting the hands
@math heading="£1,000 each, £25 a hand" items="home: 2 · 1,000 · 4 = 8,000 → 320 hands@two capitals;;foreign: 1,000 · 1/3 = 1,000/3 → 40/3 hands@one third;;8,000 / (1,000/3) = 24@the ratio"
The lesson puts hands on it, with a capital and a keep written for the lesson. The home trade enters two capitals, two thousand pounds, four times a year. That's eight thousand, three hundred and twenty hands. The foreign trade is a thousand times one third, about thirteen point three hands. And the ratio is twenty four.

@words text="Foreign returns once a year → 8"
But that twenty four isn't a constant. It follows from the returns Smith chose. If the foreign trade returned once a year, the speed ratio would be four, and the whole ratio eight.

@chapter Round-about and carrying
@art round-about
The round about trade is slower still. If you buy the hemp of Riga with the tobacco of Virginia, which you bought with British goods, you wait on two foreign trades before your capital comes home.

@art carrying-ship
And the carrying trade comes last. A Dutch merchant carrying Polish corn to Portugal replaces two capitals, neither of them Dutch. Only his profits return to Holland. With a hundred pounds of freight and profit, the lesson's figure, that's four hands against the home trade's three hundred and twenty.

@chapter Not a call to ban them
@art tobacco-hogsheads
But Smith doesn't call the remoter trades bad. About ninety six thousand hogsheads of tobacco a year are bought in Virginia and Maryland, and Britain needs perhaps fourteen thousand. The other eighty two thousand must be sent abroad, or the trade, and the labour behind it, stops.

@words text="An effect and symptom of wealth, not its cause"
The carrying trade, he says, is the natural effect and symptom of great national wealth, but not its cause. So policy should neither force nor allure capital into any branch beyond what would flow there of its own accord.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading twenty-four as a constant@The first|Thinking Smith wants foreign trade discouraged@The second|Reading private profit as proof of harmony@And the third"
The first slip is reading twenty four as a constant. The second is thinking Smith wants the foreign and carrying trades discouraged. And the third is reading his last page as proof that private profit always serves the public. He says it doesn't in Europe, and promises to explain why.
