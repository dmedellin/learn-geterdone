@chapter The idea
@title text="Equations with Fractions" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
An equation full of fractions looks harder than it is. One multiplication turns it into an equation with no fractions at all, and nothing about the answer changes, as long as you do it properly.

@art clear-fractions
The move is just the multiplication rule. Multiply both sides by a nonzero number and you get an equivalent equation. The trick is to pick a number that every denominator divides into, so every fraction disappears at once.

@chapter The least common denominator
@words text="LCD of 2 and 3 is 6" sub="of 4 and 6: 12, not 24"
That number is the least common denominator: the smallest positive number that every denominator divides exactly. For two and three, it's six. For four and six, it's twelve, not twenty four. Twenty four is the product, and the product is a common multiple, but usually not the least.

@art lcd-meet
For small denominators, list the multiples until they first meet. Four, eight, twelve. Six, twelve. They meet at twelve, so that's the least common denominator.

@math heading="Prime powers" items="6 = 2·3    8 = 2³    12 = 2²·3@six is two times three;;LCD = 2³·3 = 24@twenty four;;24/6 = 4,  24/8 = 3,  24/12 = 2@check it"
For a longer list, factor each denominator into primes. Six is two times three, eight is two cubed, and twelve is two squared times three. Take every prime at its highest power: two cubed times three, which is twenty four. Then check it by dividing: four, three and two. Every one divides exactly.

@chapter Clearing an equation
@math heading="x/2 + x/3 = 5" items="6·(x/2) + 6·(x/3) = 6·5@multiply every term by six;;3x + 2x = 30@three x plus two x;;5x = 30@five x equals thirty;;x = 6@x equals six"
Here's x over two plus x over three equals five. The least common denominator is six, so multiply every term by six, on both sides. That gives three x plus two x equals thirty. So five x equals thirty, and x equals six. Check: six over two plus six over three is three plus two, which is five.

@art every-term
Every term means every term, including the five, which has no denominator at all. Leave it alone and you get five x equals five, so x equals one, and that fails the check straight away.

@words text="Using 12: 6x + 4x = 60 → x = 6" sub="same answer, bigger numbers"
And the least common denominator is a convenience, not a requirement. Multiply by twelve instead and you get six x plus four x equals sixty, so ten x equals sixty, and x is still six. Bigger numbers are the only penalty.

@chapter Numerators with two terms
@math heading="(x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/4 = 1" items="4(x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 12@multiply by twelve;;4x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 12@expand;;x + 10 = 12@collect;;x = 2@x equals two"
Now a harder one. x plus one over three, minus x minus two over four, equals one. The least common denominator of three and four is twelve. Multiply by twelve, keeping the brackets: four times x plus one, minus three times x minus two, equals twelve. Expand, then collect: x plus ten equals twelve. So x equals two.

@art keep-bracket
Two things happened in that expansion. Four times x plus one is four x plus four, not four x plus one. And minus three times x minus two is minus three x plus six, because the minus reaches both terms. The fraction bar was acting as a bracket, and it vanishes at the very moment you need the bracket.

@chapter Decimals are fractions
@math heading="0.2x + 1.5 = 3.1" items="2x + 15 = 31@times ten;;2x = 16@two x equals sixteen;;x = 8@x equals eight"
Decimals are fractions too. Zero point two is one fifth, and one point five is three halves. Multiply by ten for one decimal place. Zero point two x plus one point five equals three point one, times ten, is two x plus fifteen equals thirty one. So two x equals sixteen, and x equals eight.

@art decimal-shift
Use ten for one decimal place, a hundred for two, and so on, for the longest one present. Check it: zero point two times eight is one point six, plus one point five is three point one.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Multiplying only the fractions@The first|Dropping the bracket on a numerator@The second|Losing the sign of a subtracted fraction@The third"
Three slips, all from this lesson. The first is multiplying only the terms that have denominators. The second is dropping the bracket on a two term numerator. And the third is losing the minus sign in front of a subtracted fraction.

@art reversible
Why is all this safe? The multiplier is a fixed nonzero number, so you can always divide back. When a denominator contains x, the multiplier can be zero for some value, and that's a different story, told in a later course.

@chapter Try it
@words text="x/6 + x/3 = x/2" sub="clear it, and read what's left"
For practice, try x over six plus x over three equals x over two. Multiply through by six, and look carefully at what's left. It's a preview of the lesson on identities and contradictions.
