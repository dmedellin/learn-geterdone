@chapter Two inequalities, one word
@title text="Compound Inequalities" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
A compound inequality is two inequalities joined by a single word, and or or. That one word decides everything. The rest is just the last lesson, done twice.

@art overlap
Join two inequalities with and, and you keep the numbers that satisfy both. That's the intersection of the two solution sets, the numbers lying in both.

@art union
Join them with or, and you keep the numbers that satisfy at least one. That's the union. And mathematical or is inclusive: a number that satisfies both is kept too.

@words text="Solve each, draw both, read off" sub="overlap for and · combination for or"
So the method is the definition. Solve each inequality on its own. Draw both solution sets on the same number line. Then read off the overlap, or the combination. Almost every error here happens before the drawing, or instead of it.

@chapter The three-part form
@math heading="3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9" items="2 < 2x ≤ 8@subtract one from every part;;1 < x ≤ 4@divide every part by two"
When both statements trap x between two numbers, they become one. Three is less than two x plus one, which is at most nine. Subtract one from every part: two is less than two x, which is at most eight. Divide every part by two: one is less than x, which is at most four.

@math heading="Check" items="x = 2:  3 < 5 ≤ 9  ✓@at two;;x = 1:  3 < 3  false@at one;;x = 4:  9 ≤ 9  ✓@at four"
Check it. At two, two x plus one is five, and five is between them. At one, it's three, and three is not less than three. At four, it's nine, and nine is at most nine. So one is out, and four is in.

@art three-part
Every operation goes to all three parts, because the compact form stands for two statements, and both of them need it. Do two parts and forget the third, and nothing on the page will warn you.

@words text="x < −2 or x ≥ 3  ≠  3 ≤ x < −2" sub="the three-part form always means and"
The three-part form only works for and. Take x less than minus two, or x at least three. Writing it as three is at most x, which is less than minus two, asks for a number that's at least three and below minus two at once. There isn't one.

@chapter What an intersection can be
@list heading="An and can give" items="An interval@an interval|One of the two, entire@one of the two|A single number@a single number|Nothing: ∅@nothing at all"
An and gives four shapes. An interval: x at least two and less than four is from two up to, but not including, four. One of the two, entire: x greater than one and greater than five is just x greater than five. A single number: at least three and at most three is only three. Or nothing at all.

@art empty-overlap
That last one is x greater than five and x less than two. There's no overlap, so there are no solutions. The empty set is a perfectly good answer.

@chapter What a union can be
@list heading="An or can give" items="Two separate pieces@two separate pieces|One piece@one piece|Every real number@every real number"
An or can give three shapes. Two separate pieces, like x less than minus two or x at least three. One piece: x less than four or x less than one is just x less than four. Or every real number.

@art all-covered
That happens with x less than four or x greater than one. Take any number. If it's below four, the first statement holds. If not, it's at least four, so it's above one, and the second holds. Nothing is left out.

@chapter Same statements, different word
@math heading="2x + 1 ≥ 5 and/or x − 4 < 0" items="x ≥ 2@x is at least two;;x < 4@x is less than four;;and:  2 ≤ x < 4@joined by and;;or:  every real number@joined by or"
Two x plus one is at least five solves to x is at least two. x minus four is less than zero solves to x is less than four. Joined by and, the answer is two is at most x, which is less than four. Joined by or, it's every real number. The connector alone decides.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Writing an or as a three-part statement@The first|Operating on two of the three parts@The second|Reading or as exclusive@The third"
Three slips. The first is writing an or as a three-part statement, which turns two infinite pieces into the empty set. The second is operating on only two of the three parts. And the third is reading or as exclusive, as if it dropped the numbers that satisfy both.

@chapter Try it
@art separated-pieces
Two separate pieces is where most absolute value inequalities land, so it's worth the practice. Take x greater than one and x greater than five. Draw both sets, and read off the answer for and. Then change the word to or, and read it again.
