@chapter The question
@title text="What It Means to Solve an Equation" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
You've solved equations before. But what are you actually looking for when you do? It turns out the answer is one short sentence, and once you have it, every move you make later has a reason.

@words text="3x − 4" sub="an expression: it names a number"
Start with three x minus four. That's an expression. As soon as you know x, it names a number. But it doesn't claim anything, so it can't be true or false.

@art two-sides
Now write three x minus four equals eleven. That's an equation: a statement that two expressions are equal. Until x has a value, it isn't true and it isn't false. It's waiting.

@chapter What a solution is
@words text="A solution makes the statement true" sub="the solution set is all of them"
A solution is a number which, written in place of every x, makes the statement true. The solution set is all such numbers. And to solve an equation is to find that whole set, all of it, and nothing that doesn't belong.

@art substitute
So how do you tell whether a number is a solution? There's only one test. Substitute it. Put the number in for x, in the original equation, and see what happens.

@chapter Checking a value
@math heading="Test x = 5" items="3x − 4 = 11@three x minus four equals eleven;;left: 3·5 − 4 = 15 − 4 = 11@three times five;;right: 11@right side is just;;11 = 11   TRUE@eleven equals eleven"
Take three x minus four equals eleven, and test five. On the left, three times five is fifteen, minus four is eleven. The right side is just eleven. Eleven equals eleven, so it's true. Five is a solution.

@art check-pass
Notice how that worked. Each side is worked out on its own, down to a single number, and then you compare. Nothing crosses the equals sign during a check, because a check isn't a solve.

@math heading="Test x = 4" items="left: 3·4 − 4 = 12 − 4 = 8@three times four;;right: 11@still eleven;;8 ≠ 11   FALSE@eight is not eleven"
Now test four. Three times four is twelve, minus four is eight. The right is still eleven. Eight is not eleven, so it's false.

@art check-fail
And that's just as useful. It tells you something definite: four is not a solution. Not a solution is a result, and most of the values you'll ever test turn out that way.

@chapter Exact answers
@math heading="3x = 1" items="3 · 333/1000 = 999/1000 ≠ 1   FALSE@three times three hundred;;3 · 1/3 = 1   TRUE@three times one third"
Here's why exact arithmetic isn't fussiness. Zero point three three three and one third are different numbers. The first is three hundred and thirty three thousandths. Put each into three x equals one. Three times three hundred and thirty three thousandths is nine hundred and ninety nine thousandths, not one. False. Three times one third is one. True.

@words text="x = 0.333 is not a solution" sub="write answers as exact fractions"
So zero point three three three is not a solution, and no amount of rounding makes it one. A decimal that ends on its own, like zero point two five, is exact and fine. A decimal that's been cut short is a different number wearing the answer's clothes.

@chapter Why solving works
@art chain-of-equations
Two equations are equivalent when they have exactly the same solution set. Three x minus four equals eleven, three x equals fifteen, and x equals five are all equivalent. The last one is only useful because it's equivalent to the first.

@list heading="Moves that keep the solutions" items="Add or subtract any number@add or subtract any number|Multiply or divide by a nonzero number@by any nonzero number"
Two kinds of move keep the solution set the same. You can add or subtract any number on both sides. And you can multiply or divide both sides by any nonzero number. Each one can be undone by a move of the same kind, so it can't lose a solution or invent one.

@art multiply-zero
Multiplying by zero fails that test, loudly. Start from two x equals six, multiply both sides by zero, and you get zero equals zero, which is true for every x. The original was true for one value only. Nothing can undo it, and the information is gone.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Checking against your own working@The first|Reporting a rounded decimal@The second|Chaining equals signs between steps@The third"
Three slips. The first is checking against your own working, not the original equation. If line three has a mistake, your answer will satisfy line three anyway. The second is reporting a rounded decimal. The third is writing every step on one line joined by equals signs, which claims eleven, fifteen and five are the same number.

@words text="One check proves one solution" sub="not that there are no others"
And one more thing. Getting eleven equals eleven proves five is a solution. It says nothing about whether there are others. That's a separate argument, and it comes from the chain of equivalent equations.
