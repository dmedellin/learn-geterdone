@chapter The recipe
@title text="One- and Two-Step Equations" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
Every equation in this video is solved by one idea: undo what was done to x, in the reverse order. Get that, and the steps choose themselves.

@art recipe-forward
Read three x minus four as a recipe. Start with x. Multiply by three. Then subtract four. That's how the expression was built, one operation after another.

@art recipe-backward
Solving runs the recipe backwards. The subtraction happened last, so you undo it first, by adding four. Then you undo the multiplication, by dividing by three. It's the order of operations, run in reverse.

@chapter One step
@math heading="One instruction, one undo" items="x + 7 = 2  →  x = −5@subtract seven;;x − 7 = 2  →  x = 9@add seven"
The four one step equations are a recipe with a single instruction, undone by the one operation that reverses it. For x plus seven equals two, subtract seven from both sides, and x is minus five. For x minus seven equals two, add seven, and x is nine.

@math heading="One instruction, one undo" items="7x = 2  →  x = 2/7@divide both sides by seven;;x/7 = 2  →  x = 14@multiply both sides by seven"
For seven x equals two, divide both sides by seven, and x is two sevenths. For x over seven equals two, multiply both sides by seven, and x is fourteen.

@art fraction-answer
That third answer is the one people rewrite. Two sevenths is a finished answer. Turn it into zero point two eight five seven and you've got a different number, one that fails the check.

@art both-sides
And every step is one named operation applied to both sides. Add four to both sides. Divide both sides by three. If you can't name the move, you can't check it's a legal one.

@chapter Two steps
@math heading="Constant first" items="3x − 4 = 11@three x minus four equals eleven;;3x = 15@three x equals fifteen;;x = 5@x equals five;;check: 3·5 − 4 = 15 − 4 = 11@three times five"
With both a coefficient and a constant, deal with the constant first. Three x minus four equals eleven. Add four to both sides: three x equals fifteen. Divide both sides by three: x equals five. And check it in the original. Three times five is fifteen, minus four is eleven.

@words text="Divide first? Legal, but messier." sub="x − 4/3 = 11/3"
You could divide first. That's legal, and it gives x minus four thirds equals eleven thirds, then x equals fifteen thirds, which is five. Same answer, worse arithmetic. Constant first is a convention because it postpones fractions, not because the other route is wrong.

@chapter Brackets
@math heading="2(x + 3) = 14" items="÷ 2:  x + 3 = 7  →  x = 4@divide both sides by two;;distribute:  2x + 6 = 14  →  x = 4@distribute first"
Two times the bracket x plus three equals fourteen can start in two ways. Divide both sides by two to get x plus three equals seven, so x is four. Or distribute first, to get two x plus six equals fourteen, and again x is four.

@art bracket-unit
What doesn't work is subtracting three to strip the bracket. The three is inside the bracket, so it isn't a term of the left side. The left side is a single product. Subtract three and you get two x plus three, not two x.

@chapter Negative coefficients
@math heading="The sign belongs to the coefficient" items="7 − 2x = 19@seven minus two x;;−2x = 12@minus two x equals twelve;;x = −6@x is minus six;;check: 7 − 2·(−6) = 7 + 12 = 19@seven minus two times"
Seven minus two x equals nineteen. Subtract seven from both sides: minus two x equals twelve. Now divide by minus two, sign and all, and x is minus six. Check: seven minus two times minus six is seven plus twelve, which is nineteen.

@art sign-flip
The coefficient is the number multiplying x, and its sign belongs to it. Divide by two instead of minus two and you get six, not minus six. The check catches that, but only if you do the check.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Operating on one side only@The first|Dropping the coefficient's sign@The second|Rounding an exact answer@The third"
Three slips. The first is operating on one side only, going from three x minus four equals eleven to three x equals eleven. The second is dropping the coefficient's sign, the most common slip of all. The third is rounding: x equals seven thirds is the answer, and two point three three is a nearby number that fails.

@chapter Try it
@words text="(3/4)x + 1/2 = 5/4" sub="constant first, then the coefficient"
For practice, try three quarters x plus one half equals five quarters. Undo the constant first, then the coefficient. You should land on x equals one, and then check it in the original.
