@chapter The problem
@title text="Variables on Both Sides" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
So far, x has lived on one side of the equation. Now it's on both. You need one new move, and it turns out you already own it.

@art both-sides-x
Take five x plus two equals three x minus eight. Undo the plus two and you get five x equals three x minus ten. There's still an x in two places. No amount of arithmetic on one side will finish the job. Something has to remove one of them.

@chapter The move
@words text="If A = B, then A − C = B − C" sub="C can be an expression, like 3x"
Here's the move. If A equals B, then A minus C equals B minus C, whatever expression C is. Why is that safe? Pick any value of x, and A, B and C are just numbers. So it's the ordinary rule, and it can be undone by adding C back.

@art remove-term
In other words, subtract three x from both sides is exactly the same licence as subtract four from both sides. The rule never asked where the number came from.

@chapter Collecting on the left
@math heading="5x + 2 = 3x − 8" items="2x + 2 = −8@subtract three x;;2x = −10@subtract two;;x = −5@divide by two;;left: 5·(−5) + 2 = −23@five times minus five;;right: 3·(−5) − 8 = −23@three times minus five"
Subtract three x from both sides, and you get two x plus two equals minus eight. Subtract two: two x equals minus ten. Divide by two: x equals minus five. Check it. On the left, five times minus five plus two is minus twenty three. On the right, three times minus five minus eight is also minus twenty three.

@chapter Or on the right
@math heading="Subtract 5x instead" items="2 = −2x − 8@subtract five x;;10 = −2x@add eight;;−5 = x@divide by minus two"
Now collect on the right instead. Subtract five x from both sides: two equals minus two x minus eight. Add eight: ten equals minus two x. Divide by minus two: minus five equals x.

@art two-routes
Same answer, with one extra sign to keep track of. And minus five equals x says exactly what x equals minus five says. An equation reads in either direction. The side you collect on is your choice.

@chapter Choosing the side
@art smaller-coefficient
Subtract the smaller coefficient, and the one that survives is positive. In three x minus seven equals eight x plus three, subtracting three x leaves minus seven equals five x plus three, and you divide by plus five. Subtracting eight x means dividing by minus five. Both work. One has fewer places to drop a sign.

@chapter Simplify first
@math heading="2(x − 3) + 4 = 3(x + 1) − 5" items="2x − 2 = 3x − 2@two x minus two;;−2 = x − 2@subtract two x;;0 = x@add two"
Brackets and like terms come before any collecting. Two times x minus three, plus four, simplifies to two x minus two. Three times x plus one, minus five, simplifies to three x minus two. Subtract two x from both sides: minus two equals x minus two. Add two, and zero equals x.

@words text="x = 0 is a solution" sub="not the same as no solution"
Both sides come to minus two when x is zero, so it checks. And x equals zero is a solution like any other. It names one specific number that works. That is not the same as no solution.

@art minus-bracket
One trap deserves its own picture. Three x minus the bracket x minus four. The minus reaches every term inside, so it's three x minus x plus four, which is two x plus four. Write minus four instead and the answer you get looks perfectly ordinary, which is why this slip survives.

@chapter When every x disappears
@art x-vanishes
Sometimes every x goes. Six x plus one equals six x minus four becomes one equals minus four. Nothing went wrong: one is never minus four, so there's no solution. But six x plus one equals six x plus one collapses to zero equals zero, true for every number.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Adding on one side, subtracting on the other@The first|Distributing a minus over only the first term@The second|Reading x = 0 as no solution@the third"
Three slips. The first is adding three x on one side and subtracting it on the other: five x plus two equals three x minus eight becomes eight x plus two equals minus eight. The second is a minus sign reaching only the first term in a bracket. And the third is reading x equals zero as no solution.

@chapter Try it
@words text="3x − 7 = 8x + 3" sub="solve it both ways"
For practice, solve three x minus seven equals eight x plus three twice. Once by subtracting three x, once by subtracting eight x. Check your answer in the original, and notice which route made you divide by a negative.
