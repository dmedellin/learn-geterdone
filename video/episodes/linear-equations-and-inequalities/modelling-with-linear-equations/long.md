@chapter What's new here
@title text="Modelling with Linear Equations" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
There's no new algebra in this video. Every equation here gets solved with moves you already have. What's new is everything around the algebra: getting from a paragraph of English to an equation, and then back again.

@words text="A model = an equation + what each letter means" sub="and the unit it's measured in"
A model is an equation whose letters stand for named quantities, together with a statement of what each letter means, and its unit. That statement isn't a courtesy. Without it, you can't check the equation against the situation.

@chapter Naming the unknown
@art rectangle-width
Pick as your unknown the quantity the others are described relative to. If the length is three centimetres more than twice the width, name the width. Then the length is two w plus three, and you don't have to invent anything else.

@list heading="Naming it" items="Let w = the width in centimetres@let w be the width|Write every other quantity in w@write every other quantity|Keep one unit throughout@keep one unit"
So, in a sentence: let w be the width in centimetres. The unit is part of the sentence. Then write every other quantity in terms of w, before you write any equals sign. And keep one unit throughout.

@art name-unknown
And notice that the unknown is a number. Write let m be the number of months, not let m be months. If m is a thing rather than a count, an expression like twenty plus zero point one five m means nothing.

@chapter Finding the equals sign
@art two-plans
An equation says two expressions have the same value. So look for one quantity the situation describes twice. Plan A charges twenty up front and zero point one five for each unit. Plan B charges thirty five up front and zero point one zero for each unit. At how many units do they cost the same?

@math heading="Let m = the number of units" items="20 + 0.15m = 35 + 0.10m@twenty plus zero point one five m equals;;20 + 0.05m = 35@subtract zero point one zero m;;0.05m = 15@subtract twenty;;m = 300@three hundred"
That question supplies the equals sign. Twenty plus zero point one five m equals thirty five plus zero point one zero m. Subtract zero point one zero m: twenty plus zero point zero five m equals thirty five. Subtract twenty, then divide by zero point zero five. m is three hundred.

@math heading="Check" items="20 + 0.15(300) = 20 + 45 = 65@plan A;;35 + 0.10(300) = 35 + 30 = 65@plan B"
Check it in both. Plan A is twenty plus forty five, sixty five. Plan B is thirty five plus thirty, also sixty five.

@words text="× 20:  400 + 3m = 700 + 2m" sub="the decimals were fractions"
And if the decimals bother you, they're fractions. Multiply every term by twenty and the equation becomes four hundred plus three m equals seven hundred plus two m, with no decimals at all.

@chapter The answer the question wants
@art consecutive
Three consecutive whole numbers add to forty eight. Call the first one n. Then the three are n, n plus one and n plus two, and their sum is three n plus three. So three n plus three equals forty eight, n is fifteen, and the numbers are fifteen, sixteen and seventeen.

@words text="Asked for the largest? 17." sub="n was defined as the first"
If the question asked for the largest, the answer is seventeen, not fifteen. n was defined as the first, and that definition is what you read at the end.

@art reject-answer
Now change the total to fifty. The same model gives three n plus three equals fifty, so n is forty seven thirds. The equation is solved, but the model isn't. There's no run of three consecutive whole numbers that adds to fifty, and the fraction is the algebra telling you so.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Naming an object, not a number@The first|Answering the equation, not the question@The second|Setting two different units equal@the third"
Three slips. The first is naming an object instead of a number, like let c be chairs. The second is answering the equation instead of the question. And the third is setting two different units equal, like plan A in pounds against plan B in pence.

@art units-mismatch
That last one is easy to miss. In pounds, plan B is thirty five plus zero point one zero m. In pence, it's three thousand five hundred plus ten m. Both are correct, but you can't set pounds equal to pence. Say the unit of each side out loud before you write the equals sign.

@chapter Try it
@words text="400 + 3m = 700 + 2m" sub="the two plans, decimals cleared"
For practice, take the two plans in their cleared form, four hundred plus three m equals seven hundred plus two m. Solve it, check that you get the same number of units as before, and then say in words what that number means.
