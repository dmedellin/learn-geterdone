@chapter Within, or beyond
@title text="Absolute Value Inequalities" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
Less than gives you one interval. Greater than gives you two pieces. That's the whole topic, and you don't have to memorise it, because the number line hands it to you.

@art band
The absolute value of X is less than k says X is within k of zero. So for a positive k, it's true exactly when minus k is less than X, which is less than k. That's one band, and an and, so the three part form is fine here.

@art two-rays
The absolute value of X is greater than k says X is further than k from zero. That's true exactly when X is less than minus k, or X is greater than k. Two rays, and an or. There's no three part form for this one, and writing one is the standard error of the topic.

@chapter A worked band
@math heading="|x − 2| < 5" items="−5 < x − 2 < 5@minus five;;−3 < x < 7@add two"
Take the absolute value of x minus two is less than five. That means minus five is less than x minus two, which is less than five. Add two to every part: minus three is less than x, which is less than seven.

@math heading="Test it" items="x = 0:  |0 − 2| = 2 < 5  ✓@zero;;x = 7:  |7 − 2| = 5 < 5  false@seven;;x = −4:  |−4 − 2| = 6 < 5  false@minus four"
Test it. Zero gives a distance of two, which is less than five. Inside. Seven gives exactly five, and five isn't less than five. Out. Minus four gives six. Out. Each test agrees with the band.

@art boundary-points
Turn the relation round and the same two numbers appear on the outside. Greater than five gives x less than minus three, or x greater than seven. Together they cover every number except minus three and seven, where the distance is exactly five and both strict statements fail.

@chapter Bars alone first
@art isolate-first
As with equations, isolate the bars before you split. The two rules are stated for bars standing alone, so anything outside them has to go first.

@math heading="2|x − 1| + 5 > 11" items="2|x − 1| > 6@subtract five;;|x − 1| > 3@divide by two;;x − 1 < −3   or   x − 1 > 3@now split;;x < −2   or   x > 4@x is less than minus two"
Two times the absolute value of x minus one, plus five, is greater than eleven. Subtract five, then divide by two: the absolute value of x minus one is greater than three. Now split: x minus one is less than minus three, or greater than three. So x is less than minus two, or greater than four.

@words text="test x = 5: 13 > 11 ✓   test x = 0: 7 > 11 ✗"
Test it. Five gives two times four plus five, which is thirteen, and thirteen is more than eleven. Zero gives seven, which isn't.

@art negative-flip
The divisor there was two, a positive number, so the relation stood. If it had been minus two, dividing would reverse it, and you'd get the absolute value of x minus one is less than three. A band instead of two rays, and a different answer entirely.

@chapter Right sides that end the question
@art never-negative
Before splitting, read the right side. An absolute value is never negative, and it's zero only where the inside is zero. That settles some questions with no split at all.

@math heading="No split needed" items="|2x + 1| < 0:  nothing@less than zero;;|2x + 1| ≤ 0:  only x = −1/2@at most zero;;|2x + 1| ≥ 0:  every real number@at least zero;;|2x + 1| > 0:  all x except −1/2@greater than zero"
The absolute value of two x plus one is less than zero? Nothing. At most zero? Only where two x plus one is zero, which is x equals minus one half. At least zero? Everything. And greater than zero? Every number except minus one half.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Using or for a less-than@The first|Squeezing a greater-than into three parts@The second|Splitting before the bars are alone@The third"
Three slips. The first is using or for a less than, which gives every real number. The second is squeezing a greater than into a three part form, like three is less than x, which is less than minus three. That's the empty set. The third is splitting before the bars are alone.

@words text="Split too early: x < −7 or x > 4" sub="correct: x < −2 or x > 4"
Split two times the absolute value of x minus one, plus five, too early, and you get x less than minus seven, or x greater than four. The correct answer is x less than minus two, or greater than four. Minus five is the number that tells them apart.

@chapter Try it
@words text="|x| < 3   and   |x| > 3" sub="draw each one, then write it"
For practice, take the absolute value of x is less than three, and then greater than three. Draw each on a number line first. Then decide whether each one needs an and or an or, and write it down.
