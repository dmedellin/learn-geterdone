@chapter The puzzle
@title text="Identities and Contradictions" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
Sometimes you're solving an equation, and the x just disappears. Every x term cancels, and you're left staring at a line with no x in it. That isn't a mistake. It's the answer, and you only have to read it.

@art three-outcomes
An equation is conditional when some values make it true and some make it false. It's an identity when every value makes it true. And it's a contradiction when no value does. So the solution set is some particular numbers, every real number, or nothing at all.

@chapter Why those are the only three
@words text="Every linear equation becomes px = q" sub="p ≠ 0: one solution, q/p"
Here's why there are only three. Any linear equation can be rewritten as p x equals q, for some numbers p and q. If p isn't zero, divide by p, and there's exactly one solution: q over p.

@art x-vanishes
But if p is zero, the equation reads zero equals q. There's no x in it, so its truth doesn't depend on x at all. If q is zero, it's true for every number. If q isn't zero, it's false for every number. None, one, or all of them. Never exactly two.

@chapter Three equations, three outcomes
@math heading="Simplify, subtract, look" items="2(x + 3) = 2x + 6  →  6 = 6   identity@two x plus six;;2(x + 3) = 2x + 5  →  6 = 5   contradiction@two x plus five;;2(x + 3) = x + 5  →  x = −1   conditional@equals x plus five"
Three equations, same left side. Two times x plus three equals two x plus six. Subtract two x: six equals six, an identity. Equals two x plus five? Subtract two x: six equals five, a contradiction. And equals x plus five gives x equals minus one. Conditional.

@words text="The left is always exactly 1 more" sub="so 6 = 5 never holds"
Read the first as: whatever x is, the two sides are the same number, so the claim holds. Read the second as: whatever x is, the left side is exactly one more than the right, so the claim never holds. The third pins x to a single value.

@chapter The two misreadings
@list heading="Read the last line" items="0 = 0 means every real number@zero equals zero|0 = 5 means no solution@zero equals five"
Now the two classic misreadings. Zero equals zero is not x equals zero. It's a true statement with no x in it, and the answer is every real number. And zero equals five is not x equals five, or x equals zero. It's false, and the answer is no solution.

@words text="Both come from one habit" sub="expecting the last line to say x = something"
Both mistakes come from the same habit: expecting the last line of working to look like x equals something. Reporting x equals zero for an identity names one solution out of infinitely many, and rejects all the rest.

@chapter The picture
@art lines-cross
Draw each side as a graph. A conditional equation is two lines crossing at one point.

@art lines-same
An identity is one line drawn twice. The two sides were always the same expression. Two times x plus three and two x plus six are the same thing, written two ways.

@art lines-parallel
And a contradiction is two parallel lines that never meet. The graphs are only a preview. The argument doesn't depend on them.

@chapter Don't judge by looks
@art looks-alike
Here's an equation that only looks like an identity. Three x plus five equals three x plus five plus x. The sides start the same way, which invites the answer every number. But subtract three x plus five from both sides, and you're left with zero equals x. It's conditional, with the single solution x equals zero.

@math heading="Check it" items="x = 0:  5 and 5@at zero;;x = 1:  8 and 9@at one"
Check it. At zero, both sides are five. At one, the left is eight and the right is nine. That extra x on the right is the whole story, and only simplifying finds it.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Writing x = 0 for 0 = 0@The first|Reading a contradiction as a value@The second|Deciding by appearance@The third"
So, three slips. The first is writing x equals zero when the working ends at zero equals zero. The second is reading a contradiction as a value, like six equals five meaning x equals one. And the third is deciding before you simplify.

@chapter Try it
@words text="3x − (x − 4) = 2(x + 2)" sub="identity, contradiction, or conditional?"
