@chapter One equation in disguise
@title text="Ratio, Proportion and Percent" sub="Linear Equations and Inequalities" art=thumb
Ratios, proportions and percents feel like three different topics. They're really one equation, wearing three different outfits. The hard part isn't the algebra. It's setting it up the right way round.

@art ratio-bars
A ratio of a to b is just the quotient, a over b, and it needs b not to be zero. It can compare two things of the same kind, or form a rate from two different kinds, like kilometres per litre. A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal.

@chapter Cross-multiplying
@math heading="If b ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0" items="a/b = c/d@a over b equals c over d;;(a/b)·bd = (c/d)·bd@multiply both sides by b d;;ad = bc@a d equals b c"
You've probably met cross multiplying as a rule. It's really one multiplication. Start with a over b equals c over d. Since b and d aren't zero, neither is b d. Multiply both sides by b d, the denominators cancel, and you get a d equals b c.

@art cross-multiply
And you can divide back by b d, so the move is reversible. That proof is also where the conditions come from. The denominators must not be zero. In this lesson they're always stated nonzero numbers, so that check is immediate.

@chapter Setting one up
@art same-order
A car covers a hundred and fifty kilometres on twelve litres. How far does it go on eighteen? The two ratios must be built the same way round. If the left is kilometres over litres, so is the right.

@math heading="150/12 = d/18" items="150·18 = 12d@a hundred and fifty times eighteen;;2700 = 12d@two thousand seven hundred;;d = 225@two hundred and twenty five;;225/18 = 25/2 = 150/12@twenty five halves"
So a hundred and fifty over twelve equals d over eighteen. Multiply by both denominators: a hundred and fifty times eighteen equals twelve d. That's two thousand seven hundred equals twelve d, so d is two hundred and twenty five kilometres. Check it: both ratios come to twenty five halves.

@words text="150/12 = 18/d  ✗" sub="km per litre vs litres per km"
Write a hundred and fifty over twelve equals eighteen over d instead, and you're comparing kilometres per litre with litres per kilometre. You'd get thirty six twenty fifths, a clean answer to a question about the wrong quantity. Writing the units beside each ratio catches it.

@chapter Percent
@art percent-grid
Percent is a ratio with denominator a hundred. Fifteen percent is fifteen over a hundred, which is three twentieths. So fifteen percent of eighty means fifteen hundredths times eighty. The word is a unit of measurement for ratios, nothing more.

@math heading="part / whole = p / 100" items="15% of 80:  part = 12@what is fifteen percent;;42 of 120:  p = 35@forty two;;30 is 12% of:  whole = 250@thirty is twelve percent"
Every percent question is part over whole equals p over a hundred, with a different piece missing. What is fifteen percent of eighty? The part is twelve. Forty two is what percent of a hundred and twenty? Thirty five percent. Thirty is twelve percent of what? Two hundred and fifty.

@art part-whole
The only real difficulty is deciding which number is the whole. For a change, the whole is the original amount. A price going from sixty to seventy five rises by fifteen on a base of sixty. Fifteen over sixty is twenty five percent.

@chapter Percents don't cancel
@art base-shift
Here's the classic. A price of a hundred rises by twenty percent, to a hundred and twenty. Then it falls by twenty percent. But that fall is twenty percent of a hundred and twenty, which is twenty four, so the price lands at ninety six, not a hundred. The two percents were taken on different bases.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Building the ratios in opposite orders@The first|Taking a percent of the wrong base@The second|Treating a variable denominator as ordinary@the third"
Three slips. The first is building the ratios in opposite orders. The second is the wrong base, like dividing the rise of fifteen by the new price, seventy five, which answers a question nobody asked. And the third is treating a denominator with x in it as ordinary. That's a later course.

@chapter Try it
@words text="x/4 = 3/8" sub="cross-multiply, then check"
For practice, try x over four equals three eighths. Cross multiply to get eight x equals twelve, and finish it as an exact fraction. Then check that both ratios match.
