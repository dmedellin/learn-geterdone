@chapter Two classical accounts
@title text="The Intentional Arc" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Schneider can grasp but can't point. If his eyes aren't the whole cause, the chapter tries two classical accounts instead.

@list heading="Grasping and pointing, divided" items="Mechanistic physiology: concrete movements are reflexes@reflexes|Intellectualism: pointing needs a categorial attitude he has lost@categorial attitude"
For mechanistic physiology, his concrete movements are reflexes, nerve circuits rooted in his work. For intellectualism, pointing means holding something at a distance, which needs a categorial attitude or symbolic function, and he's lost it. Grasping and pointing become body and consciousness.

@chapter Made consistent, each spreads
@art mosquito-ruler
The chapter's reply: each half, made consistent, spreads to cover everything. Between the mosquito that pricks the skin and the ruler the doctor presses on the same spot, the physical difference isn't great enough to explain why grasping is possible and pointing impossible.

@art reflex-chain
If nerve circuits guarantee the grasp, the same circuits and muscles should guarantee the pointing. And if pointing needs consciousness of start and finish, so does grasping. Goldstein himself came to say grasping in normal subjects still demands a categorial attitude.

@chapter Fur is to cat
@words text="fur is to cat as plumage is to bird"
Now his thinking. He doesn't understand even simple analogies, like fur is to cat as plumage is to bird, until he's worked them out.

@words text="only by a quite explicit subsumption"
The intellectualist expects him unable to subsume. It's the reverse. He can relate things only by a quite explicit subsumption. He sees the eye and ear as alike only after saying both are sense organs.

@chapter The scoreboard
@art score-table
The lab codes four observations against the two accounts, each made consistent. The coding is the lesson's reading.

@math heading="All preset" items="concrete movements: y / n@concrete movements;;cannot point: n / y@point;;abstract after preparation: n / n@preparation;;analogies: − / n@analogies;;survivors: none · split: the first two@split"
Concrete movements: physiology absorbs, intellectualism fails. The failure to point: the reverse. Abstract movements after preparation of the whole body: both fail. Analogies: physiology silent, intellectualism fails. No survivors, and the split is the first two, the two halves each account claimed.

@words text="Surviving by silence"
Keep only the analogies, and physiology survives, by silence. Saying nothing about analogies isn't absorbing them. That's a reason to read the record, not just the survivors.

@chapter What is damaged?
@art fountain-pen
Intellectualism is less false than abstract. If consciousness has no degrees, every illness becomes the same illness, and aphasia, apraxia and agnosia can't be told apart. And his trouble wasn't metaphysical: a shell splinter struck him. Shown a fountain pen, he reasons his way to it from black, shiny and long. Told a story, he keeps it as a series of facts.

@art errand-path
He never goes for a walk, only on an errand. He never recognises Goldstein's house as he passes, because he didn't go out to go there. He's tied to actuality. He's lost the concrete liberty of putting himself into a situation.

@chapter The intentional arc
@quote text="is subtended by an ‘intentional arc’ which projects round about us our past, our future, our human setting, our physical, ideological and moral situation" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 3, pp. 156–7"
Here's the chapter's term. Beneath intelligence and perception, the life of consciousness is subtended by an intentional arc.

@art arc-limp
It's this arc that unifies the senses, intelligence, sensibility and motility. And it's this that goes limp in illness. That's why one disturbance can reach seeing, moving and thinking at once.

@words text="not a matter of ‘I think that’ but of ‘I can’"
So consciousness is in the first place not a matter of I think that, but of I can. The body has its world without passing through a symbolic function. Motility is basic intentionality.

@words text="An open system of equivalent positions"
Here's a sign. Some patients can copy the doctor's movements in a mirror, right hand to right ear, but not when they face him, though they can say exactly what they have to do. In normal imitation the body does the change of coordinates itself. It has itself as an open system of equivalent positions. That's the body schema.

@chapter Mistakes and practice
@list heading="Don't say" items="Every movement is a chain of reflexes@chain of reflexes|The chapter is intellectualist@intellectualist|The arc wins the table@wins"
Don't say every directed movement is a chain of reflexes. Don't take the chapter for intellectualist about Schneider. And don't read the arc as the table's winner. It isn't a column, and no column wins. Your turn: on the all preset, name the split, and say why the preparatory movements aren't in it.
