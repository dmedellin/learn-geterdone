@chapter An arm that isn't there
@title text="The Phantom Limb" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 1" art=thumb
A man loses an arm, and goes on feeling it. Often it's in the posture it had at the moment of injury. Chapter one takes objective thought at its word, and asks physiology to explain this.

@words text="partes extra partes" sub="parts outside one another"
An object exists with its parts outside one another, in mechanical relations. To fit the organism into that world, physiology looked for a line from stimulus, to receptor, to the one who feels.

@chapter The physiological explanation
@art cut-nerve
Here it is at its strongest. Stimulate the path from the stump to the brain, and the patient feels a phantom leg, because the soul is linked to the brain and to it alone. And it has a fact on its side. Cut the nerves to the brain, and the phantom is abolished.

@list heading="But modern physiology adds" items="Cocaine doesn't remove the phantom@cocaine|Brain injury can produce one without amputation@brain injury|It keeps the posture of the injury@posture"
But modern physiology has more facts. Anaesthesia with cocaine doesn't do away with the phantom. Brain injury can produce one without any amputation. And it often keeps the posture of the injury. A man wounded in battle can still feel the shell splinters in his phantom arm.

@chapter The psychological explanation
@art phantom-outline
Then the psychic factors, from the neurologist Lhermitte. An emotion, or a circumstance that recalls the wound, can make a phantom appear in a patient who had none. And the phantom may be enormous after the operation, then shrink into the stump as the patient consents to accept his mutilation.

@words text="So is it a memory?"
So is the phantom a memory, a volition, a belief? Then cutting the nerves should leave it. No psychological explanation can overlook that.

@chapter The scoreboard
@art score-table
The lab sets four observations against the two explanations, coded by the lesson. Each cell says whether an explanation absorbs an observation, fails it, or says nothing.

@math heading="All preset" items="physiological: absorbs 1, fails 2, silent 1@physiological;;psychological: absorbs 3, fails 1@psychological;;survivors: none@survivors;;split: nerves, emotion, posture@split"
The physiological explanation absorbs one of four, fails two, and is silent on the shrinking. The psychological absorbs three and fails one. The survivors: none. The split, where they part, is the cut nerves, the emotion, and the posture.

@words text="Short tables make survivors"
Keep only the cut nerves, and physiology survives. Keep only the emotion and the posture, and psychology survives. Each can be made to look sufficient by choosing its observations. The chapter refuses that choice. The full table, with every reported observation, is the one its argument rests on.

@chapter Why not add them?
@quote text="they would need an identical point of application or a common ground, and it is difficult to see what ground could be common to ‘physiological facts’ which are in space and ‘psychic facts’ which are nowhere" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 1, p. 89"
So why not add the two? Lhermitte himself said neither alone will do. But two components make a resultant only if they act at one point.

@art no-common-ground
Nerve impulses belong to the in itself, the being of things. Consent, refusal and emotion belong to the for itself, the being of consciousness. The phantom limb is not the mere outcome of objective causality. No more is it a cogitatio, an act of thought.

@chapter A third term
@art insect-leg
Here's a hint from his earlier book. An insect whose leg is cut off substitutes a sound leg in an instinctive act. But not when the leg is merely tied. The tied leg still counts in its world.

@art open-path
That's being in the world, in the chapter's sense: a practical belonging to a situation that calls for movement, the way the first notes of a melody call for a resolution. It's neither a third person process nor an act of consciousness, and it's what can join the two orders.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="A mixture of the two suffices@mixture|A survivor on a short table is a verdict@short table|The chapter sides with psychology@siding with psychology"
Don't say a mixture suffices: a hybrid may state the facts, but it's obscure. Don't take a survivor on a short table for a verdict. And don't read the chapter as siding with psychology. It rejects it by name, for the cut nerves.
