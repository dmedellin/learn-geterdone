@chapter Through the doorway
@title text="Habit and the Blind Man's Cane" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 3" art=thumb
You go through a doorway without measuring it against your shoulders. The chapter's account of habit is full of things like that.

@words text="a rearrangement and renewal of the corporeal schema"
It takes the acquisition of habit as a rearrangement and renewal of the corporeal schema. That's the same French term this course calls the body schema. And habit has always embarrassed philosophies that think every synthesis is an intellectual one.

@chapter Two accounts refused
@list heading="Two accounts" items="Mechanistic: stimuli and responses welded by repetition@welded|Intellectual: an understanding that then withdraws@withdraws"
First, the mechanistic account: stimuli and responses, welded by repetition. Second, the intellectual one: an understanding that organises the elements and then withdraws. Learning a dance would mean analysing its formula and rebuilding it from walking and running.

@words text="the motor grasping of a motor significance"
The chapter refuses both. Learning is systematic. You acquire the power to meet a general form of situation, often with a different organ each time. It's the body that catches and comprehends a movement. Acquiring a habit is the grasping of a significance, but the motor grasping of a motor significance.

@art dance-steps
So to learn a dance, the movement must first be caught by the body, before any formula could use your general power of moving.

@chapter The feather and the car
@quote text="A woman may, without any calculation, keep a safe distance between the feather in her hat and things which might break it off. She feels where the feather is just as we feel where our hand is." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 3, p. 165"
Here's the passage.

@art feather-hat
And a driver used to his car sees that he can get through a narrow opening without comparing the width of the opening with the wings. The hat and the car have become potentialities of volume, a demand for a certain amount of free space.

@chapter The stick
@art cane-tip
Then the blind man's stick. It's stopped being an object for him. Its tip has become an area of sensitivity, extending touch the way sight would. The length of the stick doesn't enter as a middle term. He's aware of the stick through the position of things.

@words text="our power of dilating our being-in-the-world"
Habit expresses our power of dilating our being in the world, of changing our existence by appropriating fresh instruments.

@chapter The calculation habit skips
@math heading="Cane preset" items="reach with the cane: 70 + 100 = 170 cm@plus a cane;;kerb at 150 cm: within the cane's reach@kerb"
The lab does exactly the arithmetic the chapter says habit does without. The figures are illustrative. An arm's reach of seventy centimetres, plus a cane of a hundred, is a hundred and seventy. So a kerb at a hundred and fifty is within the cane's reach, not the hand's.

@math heading="Hat and car presets" items="with the feather: 165 + 30 = 195 cm@the feather;;under the door: 200 − 195 = 5 cm, clears@clears by five;;car at the gateway: 200 − 180 = 20 cm, clears@gateway"
The woman is a hundred and sixty five tall, with the feather a hundred and ninety five. Under a two hundred centimetre door she clears by five. A car a hundred and eighty wide at a two hundred gateway clears by twenty.

@art car-gateway
Every line compares two lengths. And every one of the chapter's examples is of someone who doesn't compare them. The lab computes the calculation a habit makes unnecessary.

@chapter Typist and organist
@art blank-keys
You can type without being able to say where the letters are on the keys. So it's not knowing each letter's place, and not a reflex for each letter. It's knowledge in the hands, there only when the effort is made. The typist incorporates the space of the keys into the space of the body.

@art organ-stops
An experienced organist can play an organ he doesn't know, with the stops differently placed. He needs only an hour's practice. That's too short for new reflexes, and he doesn't draw up a plan. He settles into the organ as one settles into a house.

@words text="A new meaning of the word meaning"
The body, the chapter goes on, is an expressive space, and habit is only one form of its power. So Part One has found a new meaning of the word meaning: a meaning that clings to certain contents, in a body that can fall ill.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Habit is a reflex learned by repetition@reflex|Habit is an understanding that withdraws@understanding|The lab's comparisons are what habit does@comparisons"
Don't say habit is a reflex learned by repetition. Don't say it's an understanding that withdraws. And don't take the lab's comparisons for what a practised body does quickly. They're what it makes unnecessary.
