@chapter A puzzling patient
@title text="Schneider: Concrete and Abstract Movement" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Schneider was a soldier wounded in June nineteen fifteen, a shell splinter at the back of the head. The psychologist Gelb and the neurologist Goldstein published on him from nineteen eighteen.

@words text="A case later questioned"
Be clear about one thing. The case was questioned afterwards. In nineteen forty nine, examiners doubted the reliability of the reports, and modern reviews still differ. The book argues about the case as Gelb and Goldstein reported it, and so does this lesson.

@chapter What he can and can't do
@quote text="he takes his handkerchief from his pocket and blows his nose, takes a match out of a box and lights a lamp." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 3, p. 118"
Even with his eyes shut, he makes the movements of his daily life with extraordinary speed and precision, as long as he's in the habit of making them.

@art leather-work
At work, making wallets, his output is three quarters of a normal workman's. His space is the world at this moment: the leather to be cut, the lining to be sewn.

@list heading="But with eyes shut, he can't" items="Move an arm or bend a finger to order@to order|Say where his limbs are@limbs|Say where he was touched@touched"
But with his eyes shut, he can't make abstract movements, ones that answer no actual situation, like bending a finger to order. He can't say where his limbs are, or where he was touched. He manages only by watching the limb, or by preparatory movements of the whole body.

@chapter Grasping and pointing
@art mosquito-spot
Then the famous dissociation. The same patient who can't point to a part of his body on command quickly moves his hand to where a mosquito is stinging him. He can point to his nose only if he's allowed to take hold of it.

@words text="Grasping is not pointing"
So grasping is different from pointing. Bodily space may be given in an intention to take hold, without being given in an intention to know. And asked to salute, Schneider adds all the other marks of respect. He can't play act. A normal person saluting to order knows it's an experiment, plays the soldier for a moment, and keeps to the essentials.

@chapter The lab's two frames
@art handkerchief
The lab is the lesson's model. A target is either given in the body's situation, the way the itch places the spot to be scratched, or given as a position in objective space, a point on a grid.

@math heading="Tasks preset, facing east" items="handkerchief: in the situation, no conversion@handkerchief;;mosquito bite: in the situation, no conversion@bite;;nose on command: position (1/10, 0)@nose on command;;converted: ahead 1/10, left 0@converted"
The handkerchief and the bite are given in the situation, and need no conversion. The nose on command is a position, at one tenth, nought. It has to be converted: ahead one tenth, left nought.

@art command-point
Turn the body to face north, and the nose's room position becomes nought, one tenth. The conversion has to be done again, and comes out the same. The model is a simplification. Schneider doesn't fail for want of arithmetic. Pointing asks for a body placed among places.

@chapter Given and built up
@art centripetal-centrifugal
Beckon a friend, and you beckon over there, where he is. Make the same movement for nobody, and the body becomes the end of the movement. The background to concrete movement is the world as given. The background to abstract movement is built up. Concrete movement is centripetal, abstract movement centrifugal.

@words text="Motor intentionality" sub="the power of projection"
So what Schneider lacks is neither motility nor thought, but something between them: a motor intentionality. The normal power that makes abstract movement possible, the chapter calls projection.

@chapter Is it his eyes?
@art match-and-lamp
The strongest objection: his injury was at the back of the brain, where sight is processed. His abstract movements improve when he watches. Mill's methods of induction would conclude that pointing depends on sight, and grasping on touch.

@list heading="The chapter's reply" items="Cerebellar patients have the same disorders@cerebellar|Touch seems affected too@touch|No explanations without comprehension@comprehension"
The reply comes in stages. Cerebellar patients have the same disorders, with no primary disorder of sight. There are signs touch is affected too. And there are no explanations without comprehension. Sight, touch and motility are three expressions of one disturbance, not three causes.
