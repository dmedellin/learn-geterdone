@chapter Set free
@title text="The Home Market for Industrial Capital" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 30" art=thumb
The phrase set free is ironic, but Marx also means it literally. The cleared land brought forth as much produce as before, or more, with better methods, larger farms and labourers worked harder. So the food the peasants used to grow still exists.

@quote text="With the setting free of a part of the agricultural population, therefore, their former means of nourishment were also set free. They were now transformed into material elements of variable capital." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 30"
What changed is who it goes to. With the setting free of a part of the agricultural population, therefore, their former means of nourishment were also set free. They were now transformed into material elements of variable capital.

@art food-to-wages
The expropriated peasant must buy their value back in the form of wages, from his new master, the industrial capitalist. The capitalist advances wages, and the wages buy back the food.

@chapter A new social soul
@art flax-to-mill
Raw materials go the same way. Marx imagines Westphalian peasants of Frederick the second's time, who all spun flax, driven off their land, while great spinning and weaving mills arise and employ them.

@quote text="The flax looks exactly as before. Not a fibre of it is changed, but a new social soul has popped into its body." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 30"
The flax looks exactly as before. Not a fibre of it is changed, but a new social soul has popped into its body. It's constant capital now. The extra labour spun into it, once income for many peasant families, is now profit for a few capitalists.

@art workshops-into-one
In Mirabeau's France, the great workshops were still called manufactures reunies, workshops thrown into one, as fields were thrown into one. Looking at a large factory, you don't see the many small centres of production it was built from. The name remembered it.

@chapter A market from nothing
@art household-goods
Formerly the peasant family produced its own subsistence and raw materials, and mostly consumed them itself. Now those are commodities. The large farmer sells them, and finds his market in manufactures.

@math heading="A household's day" items="food: 1s a day@a shilling;;cloth: 3s / 12 days = 3d@cloth;;fuel and candles: 3s / 12 days = 3d@fuel and candles;;subsistence: 1s 6d a day@one and six"
The lab makes it a sum, with illustrative figures. A household needs a shilling of food a day. Cloth costs three shillings every twelve days, threepence a day, and fuel and candles the same. That's one and six a day.

@math heading="A hundred households" items="self-supplied: 100 × 0 = 0s@nothing;;separated: 100 × 1s 6d = 150s a day@a hundred and fifty;;cottage food kept: 100 × 6d = 50s@fifty shillings"
A hundred households who grow, spin and gather everything themselves buy nothing: a market of nothing. Separate them from their land, and each must buy the whole one and six: a market of a hundred and fifty shillings a day. Let them keep cottage acres for their food, and it's fifty shillings.

@words text="Nobody eats more"
Nobody eats more or dresses better. The same use-values are consumed. They've become commodities.

@chapter Market and wage bill
@art market-is-wages
Read the hundred and fifty shillings from the other side. If each household sends one wage earner, paid the value of its subsistence, the wage bill is a hundred wages of one and six, a hundred and fifty shillings, spent on exactly those goods. The market created and the variable capital that circulates are one sum, seen from two sides.

@chapter Forced or chosen?
@words text="Smith · de Vries: an industrious revolution"
The strongest rival starts from the same households. Smith tied the division of labour to the extent of the market. Jan de Vries argues households chose to work more for the market and buy what they'd made at home, because they wanted new goods. Marx has the change forced and consumption flat. The lab holds consumption flat, so it can't show which.

@art slow-process
Marx says the process was slow. Manufacture destroyed rural domestic industry in some branches and called it up again in others. Only modern industry, with machinery, completes the separation, and conquers the whole home market for capital.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The market grew because people got richer@The first|Market and wage bill are separate sums@The second|The land produced less after the clearing@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the home market grew because people got richer. The second is thinking the market and the wage bill are separate sums. And the third is thinking the land produced less after the clearing. Marx says as much or more.
