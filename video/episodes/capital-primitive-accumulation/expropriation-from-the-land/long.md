@chapter A free peasantry
@title text="Expropriation of the Agricultural Population" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 27" art=thumb
The chapter starts with people who weren't poor. By the late fourteenth century, Marx writes, serfdom had practically disappeared in England. In the fifteenth, the immense majority were free peasant proprietors.

@art cottage-and-common
Even farm labourers had cottages, four or more acres, and a share in the common, which gave pasture to their cattle and furnished them with timber, firewood and turf. That world allowed a wealth of the people, Marx says. But it excluded the possibility of capitalistic wealth.

@chapter The stages
@art sheep-walks
At the end of the fifteenth century, the bands of feudal retainers were broken up, and the great lords drove peasants off the land to turn arable into sheep walks, because Flemish wool manufacture had raised the price of English wool. Parliament legislated against it for a hundred and fifty years, in vain.

@list heading="Stages of expropriation" items="Retainers disbanded, arable to sheep|Church lands seized at the Reformation@Reformation|State lands given away after 1688@sixteen eighty eight|Commons enclosed by Act of Parliament@Acts of Parliament|Estates cleared@clearing of estates"
Then the Reformation threw the church's lands to royal favourites and speculators. After sixteen eighty eight, state lands were given away or sold for almost nothing. In the eighteenth century the law itself became the instrument of theft, through Acts of Parliament enclosing the commons. The last stage was the clearing of estates.

@art enclosure-fence
Marx counts three million, five hundred and eleven thousand, seven hundred and seventy acres of common land enclosed between eighteen oh one and eighteen thirty one. The landlords, in his phrase, granted themselves the people's land.

@chapter Sutherland
@quote text="From 1814 to 1820 these 15,000 inhabitants, about 3000 families, were systematically hunted and rooted out. All their villages were destroyed and burnt, all their fields turned into pasturage." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 27"
The classic case is the Highlands, where the clan's land, held by the chief only as titular owner, became his private property. In Sutherland, from eighteen fourteen to eighteen twenty these fifteen thousand inhabitants, about three thousand families, were systematically hunted and rooted out. All their villages were destroyed and burnt, all their fields turned into pasturage.

@art highland-clearance
The people were given about six thousand acres of waste on the shore, two acres a family, at a rent. The clan land became twenty nine sheep farms, and by eighteen thirty five, Marx reports, a hundred and thirty one thousand sheep had replaced them. Then the sheep walks became deer forests.

@chapter What Marx sets aside
@quote text="We leave on one side here the purely economic causes of the agricultural revolution. We deal only with the forcible means employed." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 27"
In the middle of this history, Marx says what he isn't claiming. We leave on one side here the purely economic causes of the agricultural revolution. We deal only with the forcible means employed.

@art historians
That's where critics begin. Chambers argued in nineteen fifty three that enclosure raised demand for farm labour, and that the industrial workforce came from population growth. Robert Allen argued in nineteen ninety two that enclosure mostly moved income to landlords, closer to Marx. Eric Richards treats several Sutherland figures as exaggerated, and reads the plan as a failed resettlement. The lab can't decide between them.

@chapter Same day, new owner
@art same-day
What the lab can do is compute the mechanism. Take a smallholder's twelve hour day. On her own land, every hour is worked for her household, so the surplus labour for anyone else is zero.

@math heading="Before and after the land is gone" items="on own land: 0 h surplus for anyone else@zero;;as wage-labour: 12 − 6 = 6 h · 6/6 = 100%@six hours;;3,000 households: 3,000 × 6 = 18,000 h a day@eighteen thousand"
Before, that's zero. Now separate her from the land and hire her for the same twelve hours, at a wage whose value takes six hours to produce. The day now holds six hours of surplus labour for an employer, a hundred percent. For three thousand Sutherland families, that's eighteen thousand hours a day.

@words text="Available, not delivered"
The work didn't change. The owner of the surplus did. And that figure is what separation makes available, not who was hired. Most Sutherland families fished, emigrated or went to Glasgow. Marx's summary is ironic: so many idyllic methods of primitive accumulation.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="What changed was the work@The first|Zero means no surplus at all@The second|The estate figure counts people hired@And the third"
The first slip is thinking what changed at the clearing was the work, not who it was done for. The second is reading the zero as a claim that smallholders had no surplus at all. It's surplus for anyone else. And the third is reading the estate figure as people actually hired.
