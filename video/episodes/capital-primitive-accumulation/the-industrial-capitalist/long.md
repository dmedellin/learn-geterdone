@chapter The snail's pace
@title text="Genesis of the Industrial Capitalist" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 31" art=thumb
The farmer rose slowly. So did some guild masters and artisans, who turned themselves into small capitalists by gradually extending wage labour. But that snail's pace couldn't meet the world market opened by the discoveries of the late fifteenth century.

@art merchant-and-usurer
Money capital existed, from usury and trade. Feudal law and the guilds kept it out of industry. When they dissolved, the new manufactures settled in sea ports and in places beyond the old towns' control.

@chapter Four levers
@list heading="Four levers" items="The colonial system@colonies|The national debt@national debt|Modern taxation@taxation|Protection@protectionist"
In England at the end of the seventeenth century, Marx says, they arrive at a systematic combination, embracing the colonies, the national debt, the modern mode of taxation, and the protectionist system.

@quote text="Force is the midwife of every old society pregnant with a new one. It is itself an economic power." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 31"
Some depend on brute force. But all employ the power of the state, to hasten, hothouse fashion, the passage to the capitalist mode of production. Force is the midwife of every old society pregnant with a new one. It is itself an economic power.

@chapter The colonial system
@art colonial-system
The colonies get the most space: the treasure of the Americas and the destruction of their peoples, the plunder of the East Indies, and the trade in captured Africans. The East India Company and its servants took six million pounds from Indians as gifts between seventeen fifty seven and seventeen sixty six. Liverpool's slave ships rose from fifteen in seventeen thirty to a hundred and thirty two in seventeen ninety two.

@words text="Slavery pure and simple, for a pedestal"
In fact, Marx writes, the veiled slavery of wage earners in Europe needed, for its pedestal, slavery pure and simple in the new world.

@art historians-debate
How much that mattered to British industry is a long argument. Eric Williams argued in nineteen forty four that slave trade and plantation profits helped finance the Industrial Revolution. Engerman and O'Brien estimated those profits as too small a share of investment to be decisive. Inikori replied that the Atlantic trade's demand for British goods was central. None denies the brutality. They dispute its weight.

@chapter The national debt
@quote text="The only part of the so-called national wealth that actually enters into the collective possessions of modern peoples is—their national debt." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 31"
Marx is at his most sardonic here. The only part of the so called national wealth that actually enters into the collective possessions of modern peoples is, their national debt.

@art bonds
The debt endows barren money with the power of breeding. The creditors give nothing away: the loan becomes negotiable bonds that work in their hands like cash. The Bank of England, founded in sixteen ninety four, began by lending to the government at eight percent.

@math heading="Pricing a claim on the state" items="price = yearly payment / rate of interest@Divide the yearly payment;;at 3%: £3 / (3/100) = £100@three percent;;at 2%: £3 / (2/100) = £150@two percent;;1694: £8 / (8/100) = £100@eight percent"
The lab computes that price. Divide the yearly payment by the rate of interest. A bond paying three pounds a year at three percent is priced at a hundred pounds. The money may have been spent on a war. Let the rate fall to two percent, and the same three pounds is priced at a hundred and fifty. And the Bank's eight pounds at eight percent is a hundred.

@chapter Taxes and protection
@art taxed-necessaries
Interest needs revenue, so taxation is the debt's complement. Taxes fell above all on necessaries, and each new loan meant new taxes.

@art protection
Protection was an artificial means of manufacturing manufacturers. Marx also marks a limit: Cobbett and others made the debt the fundamental cause of misery, and that, he says, was incorrect.

@words text="North and Weingast, 1989"
The same history has an opposite reading. North and Weingast argued in nineteen eighty nine that after sixteen eighty eight, Parliament's control of the purse made the state's promises believable, so public credit was a foundation of secure property and growth. They and Marx describe the same institutions, and disagree on whom they served.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The national debt is national wealth@The first|The debt caused capitalism@The second|The chapter is only about consols@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the national debt is part of the nation's wealth. The creditor's asset is the taxpayers' liability. The second is reading the chapter as saying the debt caused capitalism. It's one lever of four. And the third is reading it as only about bonds, when most of it is the colonial system.
