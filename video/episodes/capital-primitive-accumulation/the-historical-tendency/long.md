@chapter Four pages
@title text="Historical Tendency of Capitalist Accumulation" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32" art=thumb
In four pages, Marx says where the history of the last six chapters is going. Petty private property, based on a person's own labour, is expropriated by capitalist property. Then capitalist property, centralised into fewer and fewer hands, prepares its own expropriation.

@chapter Property from your own labour
@art peasant-and-artisan
Start with what primitive accumulation destroyed. Where it isn't simply turning slaves and serfs into wage labourers, it is the expropriation of the immediate producers. That means dissolving private property based on the owner's own labour.

Marx gives that property its due. It's the basis of petty industry and of the free individuality of the labourer himself: the peasant with his land, the artisan with the tool he handles as a virtuoso. But it scatters the means of production and shuts out co-operation. To keep it forever would be, quoting Pecqueur, to decree universal mediocrity.

@art pigmy-to-huge
So it is annihilated, and that annihilation is the history of the last five lessons. The pigmy property of the many becomes the huge property of the few.

@chapter The second expropriation
@quote text="One capitalist always kills many." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32"
Then the movement continues, with a new target. What is now to be expropriated is no longer the labourer working for himself, but the capitalist exploiting many labourers. The means is capitalist production's own laws, the centralisation of capital. One capitalist always kills many.

@list heading="Two words to keep apart" items="Concentration: each capital grows by its own accumulation@Concentration|Centralisation: capitals merge, one grows as others disappear@Centralisation"
Two words from the general law of accumulation are easy to run together. Concentration is each capital growing by its own accumulation. Centralisation is capitals already formed merging, by competition or credit, so one grows because others disappear. It changes how the social capital is distributed, without adding to it. Marx's sentence names centralisation.

@chapter Ten capitals, five periods
@math heading="Concentration" items="10 capitals × £100 = £1,000@ten capitals;;each × 1.1 for 5 periods ≈ £161.05@a little over;;total ≈ £1,610.51@sixteen hundred;;largest's share = 1/10@one tenth"
The lab starts with ten capitals of a hundred pounds each. The figures are illustrative. In the first run, every capital grows by a tenth each period for five periods. Each becomes a little over a hundred and sixty one pounds, and the total rises from a thousand to about sixteen hundred and ten. The largest still holds one tenth. That's concentration.

@art merging-capitals
In the second run nothing grows, but each period the largest capital absorbs the smallest.

@math heading="Centralisation" items="£200, £300, £400, £500, £600@two hundred;;5 capitals left, total £1,000@five capitals;;share = £600 / £1,000 = 3/5 = 60%@three fifths"
The largest goes to two hundred, then three, four, five, and six hundred pounds. After five periods there are five capitals left, the total is still a thousand pounds, and the largest holds six hundred of it, three fifths. That's centralisation.

@chapter The knell
@art knell
Around the shrinking number of magnates, Marx sees two things grow. One is the mass of misery, oppression and exploitation. The other is the revolt of a working class disciplined, united, organised by the very mechanism of capitalist production itself.

@quote text="Centralisation of the means of production and socialisation of labour at last reach a point where they become incompatible with their capitalist integument. This integument is burst asunder. The knell of capitalist private property sounds. The expropriators are expropriated." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32"
Centralisation of the means of production and socialisation of labour at last reach a point where they become incompatible with their capitalist integument. This integument is burst asunder. The knell of capitalist private property sounds. The expropriators are expropriated.

@words text="The negation of negation"
Capitalist property, Marx says, was the first negation of individual property founded on one's own labour, and capitalist production begets its own negation. What it establishes is individual property based on co-operation and the possession in common of the land and the means of production. And the first expropriation was incomparably more protracted, violent and difficult than the second, because then a few usurpers expropriated the mass of the people.

@chapter The argument since
@art debate-books
Few chapters have drawn more argument. Eugen Duhring called the negation of the negation Hegelian word play doing the work of proof, and Engels replied in eighteen seventy eight that Marx first shows the process historically, and only then names its form. Eduard Bernstein argued in eighteen ninety nine that property owners were growing in number and small firms were persisting. Karl Popper later classed such laws of history as prophecy, not science.

@art giant-firm
On the other side, Baran and Sweezy argued in nineteen sixty six that the giant corporation had become the typical unit of capital, the centralisation Marx foresaw. Defenders also point to the worldwide spread of wage labour. The lab computes a mechanism, and none of that record.
