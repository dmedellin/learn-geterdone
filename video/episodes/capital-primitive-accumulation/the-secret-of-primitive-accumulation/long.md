@chapter A circle
@title text="The Secret of Primitive Accumulation" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 26" art=thumb
Over the last courses, money became capital, capital made surplus value, and surplus value made more capital. But each step presupposes the one before. The first presupposes masses of capital and labour-power already there.

@art circle
Adam Smith met the same circle and answered it with a stock that must be accumulated first, his previous accumulation. Marx keeps the question and translates the name: primitive, in the sense of first.

@chapter Original sin
@quote text="This primitive accumulation plays in Political Economy about the same part as original sin in theology." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 26"
Then the joke. This primitive accumulation plays in political economy about the same part as original sin in theology.

@art two-sorts
In times long gone by, the story goes, there were two sorts of people. One a diligent, intelligent and frugal elite. The other, lazy rascals spending their substance in riotous living. Marx names Thiers, who defended property in eighteen forty eight with this very tale. In actual history, he says, conquest, enslavement, robbery, murder, briefly force, play the great part.

@words text="Weber, 1905 · Crouzet's founders"
The legend has serious holders, and they deserve a hearing. Max Weber argued in nineteen oh five that Protestant thrift shaped early capitalism. Crouzet's studies found most early English industrialists started small and ploughed back profits. Marx's reply is his opening circle: profits to plough back already presuppose people who work for wages.

@chapter Saving out of a wage
@art saving-jar
First, the legend in its plainest form. A labourer earns three shillings a day and saves a tenth. Can he save a factory's capital? Take three thousand pounds, the carpet factory's yearly wage bill, a hundred workers at thirty pounds.

@math heading="Saving £3,000" items="target: £3,000 = 60,000s@sixty thousand shillings;;saved a day: 3s × 1/10 = 3.6d@three point six pence;;days: 60,000 / (3/10) = 200,000@two hundred thousand;;years at 300 days: 666⅔@six hundred and sixty six"
Three thousand pounds is sixty thousand shillings. A tenth of three shillings is three point six pence a day. So the saving takes two hundred thousand working days. At three hundred working days a year, that's six hundred and sixty six and two thirds years.

@math heading="Other cases" items="save the whole wage: 20,000 days, about 67 years@whole wage;;£600 mill at a tenth: 40,000 days@six hundred pounds"
Save the whole wage and eat nothing, and it's still twenty thousand days, about sixty seven years. A smaller mill of six hundred pounds still takes forty thousand days. Thrift out of a wage doesn't make a factory's capital in a working life.

@words text="£1,000 / £200 = 5 years"
Set that beside simple reproduction. A capital of a thousand pounds yielding two hundred a year, all consumed, is replaced by capitalised surplus value in five years. Two hundred thousand days to save it. Five years to lose its origin.

@chapter The double freedom
@art double-freedom
But the sum isn't Marx's main point. Money and means of production become capital only when owners meet free labourers, free in a double sense. Not part of the means of production, as slaves are. And not owning means of production, as peasant proprietors do.

@quote text="The so-called primitive accumulation, therefore, is nothing else than the historical process of divorcing the producer from the means of production." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 26"
A peasant who owns her land need not sell her labour-power to anyone. A saved capital would still need people of the second kind. So the so-called primitive accumulation, therefore, is nothing else than the historical process of divorcing the producer from the means of production.

@chapter Two faces
@art blood-and-fire
Marx grants the history has two faces. From one side, it's the workers' emancipation from serfdom and the guilds, and this side alone exists for bourgeois historians. From the other, they became sellers of themselves only after being robbed of their means of production, a history written in letters of blood and fire.

@art england-map
How much was robbery and how much slow economic change is what historians still argue, and the next lessons give both sides. Marx takes England as his example, because there alone, he says, the process has its classic form.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The first capitals were saved from wages@The first|Money is capital in itself@The second|Emancipation and expropriation as two events@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the first capitals were saved out of wages. The second is treating money or means of production as capital in themselves. And the third is reading emancipation and expropriation as two different events, when Marx says they're one process seen from two sides.
