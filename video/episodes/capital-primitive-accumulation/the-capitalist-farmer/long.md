@chapter A fixed payment, rising prices
@title text="Genesis of the Capitalist Farmer" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 29" art=thumb
If you've paid a fixed rent or a fixed rate loan through a few years of rising prices, you know this. The payment stays the same number of pounds, everything else costs more, and the payment gets lighter. Chapter twenty nine says this happened to English landlords on a grand scale.

@chapter From bailiff to farmer
@art bailiff-to-farmer
Expropriation creates directly only great landowners, so the farmer must be traced separately. His rise is slow, over centuries, so we can, Marx says, put our hand on it. The bailiff, himself a serf, gives way to a farmer stocked by the landlord, then a metayer who shares stock and product, then the farmer proper.

@art golden-fruit
The farmer proper makes his own capital breed by employing wage labourers, and pays the landlord a rent. Two things then enriched him. The usurped commons let him keep more cattle almost for nothing. And the price revolution did the rest.

@chapter The price revolution
@art silver-mines
On Marx's theory of money, prices rose because the value of gold and silver fell. The American mines, Potosi above all, produced silver with far less labour than Europe's, so each ounce held less value, and every price in silver rose.

@quote text="The progressive fall in the value of the precious metals, and therefore of money, brought the farmers golden fruit." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 29"
The progressive fall in the value of the precious metals, and therefore of money, brought the farmers golden fruit. Prices of corn, wool and meat swelled his money capital without any action on his part, while the rent he paid, calculated on the old value of money, diminished in reality.

@chapter One farm's account
@art long-lease
Leases ran long, often ninety nine years, at a money rent fixed when they were signed. So take one farm, with illustrative figures. At old prices the product is worth a hundred pounds, wages are forty, rent twenty. The farmer keeps forty.

@math heading="Prices double, money wages too" items="product £200 − wages £80 − rent £20 = £100@a hundred pounds;;at old prices: £100 / 2 = £50, against £40@fifty;;real rent: £20 / 2 = £10@worth ten"
Now every price doubles, money wages included. The product sells for two hundred, wages cost eighty, the rent is still twenty, so the farmer keeps a hundred pounds. At old prices that's fifty, against forty before. And the rent is worth ten. Ten pounds of real value passed from landlord to farmer.

@math heading="Prices double, wages rise by half" items="keeps: £200 − £60 − £20 = £120@a hundred and twenty;;at old prices: £120 / 2 = £60@sixty;;from the landlord: £20 − £10 = £10@from the landlord;;from the labourers: £40 − £60 / 2 = £10@from the labourers"
Now let money wages rise by only half, Marx's it lowered wages. Wages cost sixty, and the farmer keeps a hundred and twenty, sixty at old prices. That's twenty pounds of real gain: ten from the landlord, whose rent halved, and ten from the labourers, whose sixty buys what thirty bought before.

@quote text="Thus they grew rich at the expense both of their labourers and their landlords." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 29"
The arithmetic splits the gain exactly as Marx's last sentence does. Thus they grew rich at the expense both of their labourers and their landlords. By the end of the sixteenth century, Marx concludes, England had a class of capitalist farmers, rich, considering the circumstances of the time.

@chapter Both halves disputed
@art treasure-ships
On the cause, Jean Bodin argued in fifteen sixty eight that American treasure raised prices because there was more money, an early quantity theory, and Hamilton's figures seemed to bear it out. Later historians noted English prices rose before much silver arrived. Outhwaite and others blamed a growing population pressing on food. The lab takes the price index as given.

@art estate-records
On the effect, Eric Kerridge's study of estate rents from fifteen forty to sixteen forty found rents, through entry fines and new leases, often rose faster than prices. The golden fruit ripened only where a lease really was long and fixed. Where it was, the arithmetic is Marx's. How often it was is a question for the estate records.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Everyone stays put when prices double@The first|The farmer grew rich by exertion@The second|The price revolution's cause is settled@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that when all prices double, every party is where it was. That holds only if every payment doubles. The second is thinking the farmer grew rich by his own exertion. And the third is taking the cause of the price revolution as settled.
