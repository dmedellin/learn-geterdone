@chapter Without a servant
@title text="The Modern Theory of Colonisation" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 33" art=thumb
In eighteen twenty nine, Thomas Peel sailed for the Swan River in West Australia. He took fifty thousand pounds in money, tools and stores, and three thousand working people. When they arrived, he was left without a servant to make his bed or fetch him water from the river.

@art swan-river
The people had found land for the taking, and they went to take it. Volume I of Capital ends with this story, told by Edward Gibbon Wakefield.

@chapter Two kinds of property
@art two-properties
Why end a book about capital in the colonies? Because, Marx says, political economy confuses on principle two very different kinds of private property. One rests on the producer's own labour. The other rests on employing the labour of others.

In Western Europe, primitive accumulation is more or less finished, so the economist can talk about the second kind in the language of the first. In the colonies he can't. There the independent producer still exists in numbers, and capital runs straight into him.

@chapter What Wakefield found
@art wakefield-desk
In the eighteen twenties and thirties, Wakefield wrote that the trouble with new colonies was not a lack of capital but a lack of labourers for hire. His ideas were put into practice in founding South Australia in eighteen thirty six, and in the New Zealand Company's settlements.

@quote text="He discovered that capital is not a thing, but a social relation between persons, established by the instrumentality of things." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 33"
Marx gives him the credit. He discovered that capital is not a thing, but a social relation between persons, established by the instrumentality of things. Tools and stores are capital only where they employ and command workers who have no means of their own. In a settler's own hands, the same tools are just his means of production.

@chapter Peel's position
@math heading="Workers for hire with free land" items="capital: £50,000 · colonists: 3,000@fifty thousand;;land price = 0 years of wages@price is zero;;years to buy land = 0 / (1/2) = 0@zero years;;workers for hire: 0@no one"
The lab computes Peel's position. He has fifty thousand pounds and three thousand colonists. Land is free, so its price is zero years of wages. A worker who saves half his wage needs zero years to buy it, so every one of them can leave wage labour at once. Workers for hire: no one.

The fifty thousand pounds is all still there, in tools and stores, and it employs no one. Wakefield himself reported that at the Swan River a great mass of capital has perished for want of labourers to use it. Marx's verdict is a joke: unhappy Mr. Peel, who provided for everything except the export of English modes of production.

@chapter A sufficient price
@quote text="The price of the soil imposed by the State must, of course, be a ‘sufficient price’—i.e., so high ‘as to prevent the labourers from becoming independent landowners until others had followed to take their place.’" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 33"
Wakefield's remedy was a price. The State should sell the land at a sufficient price, high enough to prevent the labourers from becoming independent landowners until others had followed to take their place.

@math heading="Land at two years' wages" items="years = land price / share saved;;2 / (1/2) = 4 years@four years;;periods 1 to 4: 3,000 for hire@three thousand;;period 5: 0 for hire@period five"
Divide the price by the share of the wage saved. Land at two years' wages, saving half the wage, takes four years of wage labour to buy. For those four years, all three thousand colonists are for hire. In period five they are gone, unless others have followed. And if a worker can save nothing, land at any price is out of reach, which is the condition of the old country.

@art ransom-gate
Marx calls the sufficient price the ransom which the labourer pays to the capitalist for leave to retire from the wage labour market to the land.

@chapter The case for the plan
@art compact-settlement
Wakefield had serious supporters. John Stuart Mill backed systematic colonisation as a justified exception to leaving things to the market. Settlers scattered over cheap land, he argued, couldn't build roads, schools or markets, or combine their labour, and concentrated settlement would make everyone richer.

In nineteen seventy, Evsey Domar made the observation general. Free land, free peasants, and a class of landowners who don't work cannot all exist together. Any two exclude the third, which is why, he argued, free land so often went with serfdom or slavery. Marx notes that Wakefield himself called slavery the natural basis of colonial wealth.

@chapter The old world catches up
@art westward
The scheme failed, Marx says, as completely as Sir Robert Peel's Bank Act. Emigrants went to the United States instead. Then the old world caught up with the new. Immigration outran the westward movement. The Civil War brought a national debt, public land went to railway companies, and America stopped being the promised land for emigrant labourers.

@words text="The expropriation of the labourer"
What the colonies blurted out, Marx ends, is that capitalist production and capitalist private property have for their fundamental condition the annihilation of self earned private property. In other words, the expropriation of the labourer. That closes Volume I. The next course, The Circuits of Capital, opens Volume II.
