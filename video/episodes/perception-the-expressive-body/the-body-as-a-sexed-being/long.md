@chapter Where meaning begins
@title text="The Body as a Sexed Being" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 5" art=thumb
Space and things tempt us to think of the subject as a knower facing an object. So to see how something begins to exist for us, Merleau-Ponty looks at the area of experience that matters only for us: affective life, desire and love.

@art tile-mosaic
The usual view makes affectivity a mosaic of pleasures and pains, each sealed in itself and explained by the bodily system. Where intelligence enters, representations stand in for natural stimuli. So any sexual incapacity should be either a loss of representations, or a weakened capacity for pleasure.

@chapter Schneider again
@words text="As Gelb, Goldstein and Steinfeld reported him"
The test is Schneider, as his doctors reported him, and those reports were later questioned. As reported, he no longer seeks intimacy of his own accord. Pictures and talk don't arouse desire. His reactions are local and begin only with contact, as if he didn't know what's to be done.

@art gear-and-cloud
Can either account absorb this? Not representations: touch has lost its significance too, and the weak representations look like an effect, not a cause. Not reflexes: a small wound at the back of the head should have freed the automatic responses, not dulled them. Between automatic response and representation, there's a vital zone.

@words text="Erotic perception is not a cogitatio which aims at a cogitatum"
What's changed is the structure of perception itself. He's lost the power to project a sexual world before him. Erotic perception is not a cogitatio which aims at a cogitatum, an act of thought aimed at its object. Through one body, it aims at another, in the world.

@chapter The girl who lost her voice
@art flat-wave
The psychiatrist Ludwig Binswanger reports a girl whose mother has forbidden her to see the young man she loves. She can't sleep, loses her appetite, and finally loses the use of speech. It had happened once before, in childhood, after an earthquake.

@list heading="Neither of the ready readings" items="Not a paralysis: left free to see him, she speaks again@Not a paralysis|Not a deliberate silence: one keeps silence only when one can speak@Not a deliberate silence"
Speech is the function most bound up with co-existence, so losing it stands for a refusal of co-existence. And it's not a paralysis: treated, and left free to see him, she speaks again. Not a deliberate silence either. To have lost one's voice is not to keep silence. One keeps silence only when one can speak.

@art collapsed-field
She loses her voice as one loses a memory. Will supposes a field of possibilities to choose among. What collapses when she loses her voice is the field itself.

@chapter Interfusion
@quote text="There is interfusion between sexuality and existence, which means that existence permeates sexuality and vice versa" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 5, p. 196"
Here's the passage. The reason existence can't be reduced to sexuality also keeps sexuality from being reduced to existence.

@art atmosphere
Sexuality is present as an ambiguous atmosphere, co-extensive with life. So it's impossible to label a decision or act sexual or non sexual. And that's no gap in our knowledge. No God could sound our hearts and settle what we owe to nature, and what to freedom.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Sexuality reduces to existence, or the reverse@reduces|The lost voice is a message she means@message|The chapter's Freud is Freud's own@Freud"
Don't say the chapter reduces sexuality to existence, or existence to sexuality. Don't read the lost voice as a message she means to send. And don't take the chapter's Freud for Freud's own view. It reads psychoanalytic practice against his theory, and that reading is disputed.
