@chapter Anger across the room
@title text="The Spoken Word and Thought" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 6" art=thumb
Across a cafe you see two people you can't hear. One leans forward, jaw set, a hand flat on the table. You know, before any word reaches you, that this is anger. How?

@art cafe-table
The obvious answer: you recall your own feelings when you made that gesture, and reason by analogy. But you know very little from inside about what your own face does in anger. So the analogy has nothing to work with. The gesture does not make me think of anger. It is anger itself.

@chapter Understood, not just seen
@art fireplace
Still, a gesture's meaning isn't seen like the colour of a carpet. Otherwise we'd understand a dog's or a mantis's display as easily as human gestures. The sense is understood, taken up by the spectator. It's as if the other's intention inhabited my body. I take it as a whole at once, the way I perceive a fireplace.

@quote text="The spoken word is a gesture, and its meaning, a world." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 6, p. 214"
The linguistic gesture, likewise, outlines its own meaning.

@chapter The objection
@list heading="The classical division" items="Emotional gesture: a natural sign@natural sign|The word: a conventional sign@conventional sign"
Now the objection, at length. A smile has the rhythm of joy in it. But a word's link with its meaning looks accidental, as the many languages show. So gesture is a natural sign, the same in every body, and the word is a conventional sign. Speech can't be gesture.

@art piano-keys
The first reply: conventions are a late form of relation. They presuppose an earlier way of communicating. New speech plays on a keyboard of acquired meanings, as a gesture plays on the perceived world. And words are ways of singing the world, drawing out the emotional essence of things. That's why one language never fully passes into another.

@chapter No natural sign
@quote text="The angry Japanese smiles, the westerner goes red and stamps his foot or else goes pale and hisses his words." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 6, p. 219"
The second reply denies the other half. In man there is no natural sign. Same organs and nerves aren't enough for the same emotion to produce the same signs.

@math heading="Anger preset" items="smiles: only the Japanese → varies@smiles;;goes red, stamps: only one Westerner@goes red;;goes pale, hisses: only the other@goes pale;;core: none@core"
The lab codes the sentence. Only the Japanese smiles, so that sign varies. Only one Westerner goes red and stamps, and only the other goes pale and hisses. The core, the sign every expression of anger shares, is none.

@art three-gestures
Even the two Western expressions alone share nothing: the sentence's or else already gives one culture two ways. And the difference of behaviour, the chapter says, is a difference in the emotions themselves.

@math heading="Smile preset" items="angry smile: core smile@angry smile;;smile in pleasure: lacks none of the core@pleasure"
Ask it the other way. Code the angry smile, and add a smile of pleasure, the lesson's addition, marked as not anger. The pleased smile lacks none of the core. So the sign alone doesn't decide which emotion it is.

@chapter Invented like words
@words text="Feelings and passional conduct are invented like words."
It is no more natural, and no less conventional, to shout in anger or to kiss in love than to call a table a table. Feelings and passional conduct are invented like words. That's not arbitrary at will. Everything in man is both manufactured and natural.

@art sediment-layers
What sets speech apart is that, alone of all ways of expressing, it settles into a sediment and becomes an acquisition. You can speak about speech, but you can't paint about painting. That's what gives Reason its privileged place, and the ideal of thought without words.

@chapter Speaking and spoken
@art sprout-and-chest
So the chapter distinguishes a speaking word from a spoken word. In the speaking word, a meaning is coming into being. Speech is the surplus of our existence over natural being. The spoken word enjoys meanings already available, like a fortune already made, and new expression draws on it.

@list heading="Don't say" items="Gestures are natural, words conventional@natural|Invented means arbitrary at will@arbitrary|An empty core means gestures are meaningless@no meaning"
So don't divide signs into natural gestures and conventional words. Don't hear invented like words as arbitrary at will. And don't take the empty core to mean gestures have no meaning. Anger is read in each gesture.

@chapter Try it
@words text="tears + bowed head · tears + silence · tears + wailing" sub="what is the core?"
Your turn. Three expressions of grief: tears and a bowed head, tears and silence, tears and wailing. What's the core? And would a shared sign make it grief's natural sign?
