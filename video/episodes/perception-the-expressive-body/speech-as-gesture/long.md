@chapter What is it to have language?
@title text="Speech as Gesture" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part One, Chapter 6" art=thumb
What is it to have language? The first answer the chapter takes up is the empiricist one.

@art lamp-glow
To have language is to have verbal images, traces left by words heard or spoken. A stimulus calls up the right image, and the word comes out. On that picture there's a flow of words and no one who means them. A person speaks the way an electric lamp glows.

@words text="Not a stock of words, but a way of using them"
Then the theory changed. A patient could say no to deny something in his present experience, and couldn't say it as an exercise. What he'd lost wasn't a stock of words, but a way of using them.

@chapter Sorting ribbons
@art ribbons-heap
Gelb and Goldstein studied patients who'd lost the names of colours, and found they couldn't sort colours either. Asked to sort a heap of samples by basic colour, they worked slowly, set samples side by side to compare, and didn't see at a glance what went together.

@words text="pale blue → pale green → pale pink"
Then the unaccountable mistakes. Having collected several blue ribbons, if the last was a pale shade, they carried on by adding a pale green or a pale pink to the blues.

@chapter A category, or a chain
@art ribbon-chain
The lab sets this up with the lesson's own figures, lightness from nought, the darkest, to ten. The chain follows the lesson's rule: always take the sample nearest the last, by the difference in lightness, plus one if the hue changes. No theory in the book states that rule.

@math heading="Samples preset, from pale red" items="by hue: three reds, two greens, two browns@by hue;;from pale red 9: pale green costs 1 + 1 = 2, mid red 3@pale red;;tie at 3: mid red, listed first@tie;;leaves red at: pale green, the first step@leaves red"
By hue, the sort gives three reds, two greens and two browns. From pale red at nine, pale green at eight costs one plus one, two, and mid red at six costs three. So the chain leaves red on its first step. From pale green, mid red and mid green tie at three, and mid red, listed first, comes next.

@chapter Gelb and Goldstein's reading
@list heading="Two attitudes" items="Categorial: a sample as a case of a kind@Categorial|Concrete: each sample in its individual look@concrete"
Their first reading: the patients haven't lost the words, but the ability to take a sample as a case of a category. They've fallen back from the categorial attitude to the concrete one. One deficit explains both the lost names and the failed sort.

@words text="In the first there is nobody to speak; in the second, there is certainly a subject, but a thinking one, not a speaking one."
Merleau-Ponty's reply: the new theory and the old are kin. Both hold that the word has no significance of its own. For the empiricist, it's an event thrown up by causes. For the intellectualist, it's an empty sign of a thought that could do without it.

@quote text="Thus we refute both intellectualism and empiricism by simply saying that the word has a meaning." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 6, p. 206"
And that's the refutation.

@chapter Speech accomplishes thought
@art brush-dusk
Why believe it? Look at something in the half light and say, it's a brush. There's no concept of a brush you first subsume it under. The denomination of objects does not follow upon recognition. It is itself recognition.

@quote text="Thus speech, in the speaker, does not translate ready-made thought, but accomplishes it." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part One, Chapter 6, p. 207"
The orator doesn't think before speaking. His speech is his thought. And the listener learns a word the way you do abroad, from its place in what people are doing.

@art city-horizon
The words you know aren't images you consult. They're behind you, like things behind your back, or the city's horizon round your house. You have only one way of representing a word, which is to say it.

@chapter The patients again
@art sticky-samples
Later the chapter comes back to the patients, and corrects itself. For the patient, each sample stays shut in its own existence, and the heap resists grouping with a kind of viscosity. It was wrong to say he switches between principles of classification. He never adopts one at all.

@words text="the meaning inhabits the word"
Patients can repeat colour names and still not sort. A name given to the patient tells him nothing. The word hasn't lost a concept behind it. It's been emptied, because the meaning inhabits the word.

@chapter Mistakes and practice
@list heading="Don't say" items="Gelb and Goldstein's first reading is the chapter's@first reading|Words are labels on categories still there@labels|The chain's rule is what the patient does@chain's rule"
Don't take Gelb and Goldstein's first reading for the chapter's. Don't treat words as labels on categories that are still there. And don't take the chain's rule for what the patient does. Your turn: dark green at two, pale green at nine, dark blue at three. Starting from dark green, where does the chain leave green?
