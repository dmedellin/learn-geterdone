@chapter Sensing, given back its mystery
@title text="The Phenomenal Field" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Introduction, Chapter 4" art=thumb
The Introduction's last chapter gathers the argument. And it starts by giving sense experience back its mystery.

@art wheel-and-flame
Koffka supplies the cases. A wooden wheel lying on the ground isn't, for sight, the same as a wheel bearing a load. And a candle flame changes its look for a child who's been burned, from something that draws the hand to something repulsive. Vision is already inhabited by a meaning.

@chapter Lost together
@quote text="So, ‘sensation’ and ‘judgement’ have together lost their apparent clearness: we have observed that they were clear only as long as the prejudice in favour of the world was maintained." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 4, p. 62"
Here's the passage. Each was clear only while the world was taken as finished and fully determinate. The moment either described perception as it happens, it became inconceivable.

@art window-on-things
Where did the prejudice come from? Science and philosophy lived for centuries on faith in perception, which opens a window on things and seems to promise one unbroken text. So it was said that perception is an incipient science.

@words text="classical science is a form of perception which loses sight of its origins and believes itself complete"
The chapter reverses the formula. Classical science is a form of perception which loses sight of its origins and believes itself complete.

@chapter Not an inner world
@quote text="This phenomenal field is not an ‘inner world’, the ‘phenomenon’ is not a ‘state of consciousness’, or a ‘mental fact’" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 4, p. 66"
It's easy to hear a return to phenomena as a turn inward, to private appearances known by introspection. The chapter says no.

@art hidden-shape
Bergson's immediate, known only by coinciding with it, is a lonely, blind and mute life that can't even be said. The phenomenal is understood, the way you find the rabbit in the foliage of a puzzle, or catch a slight gesture. The immediate is now the meaning, the structure, the arrangement of parts.

@chapter A physiognomy, not a law
@art circle-look
The psychologist can go one step further, and treat the phenomenal field as a transcendental field, where every object is constituted. The classical philosopher would then crown it with one thinking Ego. The chapter refuses. The Gestalt of a circle is not its mathematical law, but its physiognomy, the look it has, as a face has one.

@list heading="Two mirror mistakes" items="Bergson: the knower fuses with what it knows@fuse|Reflective philosophy: the knower absorbs it without remainder@absorb"
So the two mistakes mirror each other. Bergson thinks the knower can fuse with what it knows. Reflective philosophy thinks the knower can absorb it into thought without remainder.

@art two-mirrors
We're never the unreflective subject we try to know, and never wholly the consciousness that would know it. Reflection can't stop me seeing the sun two hundred yards away on a misty day. So a philosophy becomes radical by treating its own claim to total knowledge as the problem.

Empiricism absorbs one of the eight and fails seven. Intellectualism absorbs two, fails five, and is silent on one. The survivors, accounts with no failure: none. The split, where one absorbs and the other fails: the misprinted word, and two rows on attention.

@words text="Not a ranking"
Read that with care. Two against one isn't a ranking. It says only which cases the lesson chose to code. The claim is that both fail from one shared prejudice, and that reason appears in no cell. And Merleau-Ponty's own account isn't a column. It would score clean only because it was coded that way.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The field is a private inner world@inner world|The record is a ranking@ranking|Merleau-Ponty needs his own column@column"
Don't take the phenomenal field for a private inner world: every row is a case in the world. Don't read the record as a ranking. And don't expect Merleau-Ponty's own column. His account is in prose: meaning and arrangement given together.

@chapter Why begin with psychology
@words text="Experience anticipates a philosophy and philosophy is merely an elucidated experience."
Without the psychologist's descriptions, the transcendental problem wouldn't be understood. Without philosophy, the descriptions wouldn't be seen to be true. Experience anticipates a philosophy, and philosophy is merely an elucidated experience.
