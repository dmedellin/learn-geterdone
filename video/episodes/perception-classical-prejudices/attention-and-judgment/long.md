@chapter The other opponent
@title text="Attention and Judgment" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Introduction, Chapter 3" art=thumb
If sensations can't organise themselves, something must organise them. The obvious candidate is the mind. That's intellectualism, from Descartes and Kant to the French teachers Lagneau and Alain.

@quote text="Both take the objective world as the object of their analysis, when this comes first neither in time nor in virtue of its meaning" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 3, p. 30"
The chapter's surprising thesis is that intellectualism stands on the same level as empiricism. The answer shares the question's premise.

@chapter Two theories of attention
@art searchlight
Start with attention. For empiricism, the normal sensations are there all along, unperceived, and attention reveals them, like a searchlight showing objects already in the dark. It creates nothing, and it's the same whatever it lights.

@words text="Attention as elucidation"
Intellectualism starts from the other end. Descartes's wax was extension from the start. I grasp that clearly or confusedly as I attend more or less. If I find a circle in a round plate, it's because I put it there.

@art moon-tube
Alain's test: look at the horizon moon through a cardboard tube, and it's no bigger than at the zenith. The reply is that the tube doesn't reveal the moon of free vision. It makes a different, analytic kind of looking.

@list heading="Both fail" items="Empiricism: too poor@too poor|Intellectualism: too rich@too rich|Both: attention creates nothing@creates nothing"
So they fail in opposite ways. Empiricism's consciousness is too poor: you must know what you're looking for, or you wouldn't look. Intellectualism's is too rich: you must not know it, or you wouldn't search. And both say attention creates nothing. For the chapter, attention constitutes a new object, out of an indeterminate horizon.

@chapter Perception as judgement
@art window-hats
Now judgement. Descartes looks from his window at men in the street, and asks what he sees. Hats and coats, which might cover dummies worked by springs. He doesn't see the men. He judges them to be there.

@list heading="The objections" items="A big box feels heavier than a small one@feels heavier|A drawn cube flips, whatever you know@flips|Seeing and thinking you see become the same@thinking you see"
The objections come fast. A large cardboard box feels heavier than a small one of the same cardboard, so is weight judged and not felt? A drawn cube flips, and knowing it can flip doesn't make it. And if you see what you judge, seeing and thinking you see become the same.

@chapter The walk to the ship
@art ship-in-forest
The lab tests the judgement model on a case from page twenty. You walk along a shore towards a ship run aground. Its masts merge into the forest on the dune behind. Two interpretations: part of the forest, or the ship's masts and funnel.

@math heading="Judgement from signs" items="step 1, c1: both fit; count 2@step one;;step 2, c2: both fit; count 2@step two;;step 3, c3: only the ship; count 1@step three;;removed: part of the forest behind@removed"
At step one, the upright forms: both fit, count two. At step two, the same forms nearer: still two. At step three, they're the ship's colour and fit its superstructure. Only the ship fits, count one, and the forest is removed.

@quote text="there will be a moment when these details suddenly become part of the ship" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 2, p. 20"
The model concludes from the sign that excludes the trees. The book puts the moment elsewhere.

@art ship-revealed
After a tension like a storm in storm clouds, the details suddenly become part of the ship. Only afterwards does the walker find the colour and the fit as justifications. These reasons for correct perception were not given as reasons beforehand.

@chapter A motive, not a reason
@art steeple-motive
Hide the objects in front of a distant steeple behind a screen, and it seems to shrink. Yet there's no reason why a steeple should look farther away when you see the fields before it in more detail. There is no reason, but there is a motive.

@words text="From absolute objectivity to absolute subjectivity"
That's the thesis brought home. Intellectualism swaps a world acting on the eyes for a consciousness that thinks the world, and leaves the world as it was. The two theories stand or fall together.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Refuting empiricism confirms intellectualism@confirms|The deciding step is the moment of seeing@moment of seeing|The chapter dismisses Descartes@dismisses"
Don't say refuting empiricism confirms intellectualism. Don't read step three as the moment of seeing: on the book's account the unity arrived first. And don't say the chapter dismisses Descartes. It grants that his natural judgement may be the deepest sense of Cartesianism.
