@chapter Two lines with fins
@title text="The Constancy Hypothesis" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Introduction, Chapter 1" art=thumb
You've almost certainly seen these two lines with arrowheads, and been told they're the same length.

@art muller-lyer
One has its fins turned out, one has them turned in. Measure them and they're equal. Most people report a difference anyway. The textbook calls it an illusion. Merleau-Ponty says the mistake is in the question.

@chapter The hypothesis
@art transmitter
Here's physiology's picture at its strongest. The path from eye to brain is like a line from a receiver, through a transmitter, to a recording station. The world sends messages, and they're decoded to reproduce the original text in us.

@quote text="Hence we have in principle a point-by-point correspondence and constant connection between the stimulus and the elementary perception." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 1, p. 8"
If that's right, the same message must be decoded the same way wherever it arrives. Kohler named this the constancy hypothesis, in nineteen thirteen.

@list heading="Where it conflicts" items="A sound's intensity can lower its pitch@intensity|Added lines make equal figures unequal@Added lines|Red and green together give grey@Red and green"
And it conflicts with the data of consciousness. A sound's intensity can lower its pitch. Added lines make two equal figures unequal. Red and green shown together give grey. So the choice is plain: keep the law and add factors like attention and judgement, or give up the law.

@chapter What it predicts
@art ruler-lines
The lab computes the prediction. In the Muller-Lyer preset, both shafts measure ten centimetres. The reported difference is illustrative, because the effect varies with the fins.

@math heading="Muller-Lyer preset" items="predicted: 10 − 10 = 0 cm@predicted;;reported: 2 cm@reports;;gap: 2 − 0 = 2 cm@the gap"
The predicted difference is ten minus ten, nought. Someone reports the outward finned shaft two centimetres longer. So the gap is two minus nought, two centimetres. And that gap is an illusion only if the measured lines are the standard for what should be seen.

@art plain-shafts
The equal preset removes the fins: two plain shafts of ten centimetres, and a reported difference of nought. Every tile reads nought. Here the hypothesis and the report agree, which is why it looks so secure.

@math heading="Context preset" items="10 − 10 = 0 cm@same local stimulus;;reported: −1 cm@one centimetre shorter;;gap: −1 − 0 = −1 cm@minus one"
Now set a shaft with inward fins against the same shaft alone. It's the same local stimulus twice, so the prediction is nought. The finned one is reported one centimetre shorter, so the gap is minus one.

@art context-shaft
The minus sign says the first named line was reported the shorter. So here's one stimulus and two reports. That's exactly what a point for point correspondence forbids.

@chapter Neither equal nor unequal
@quote text="neither of equal nor unequal length; it is only in the objective world that this question arises" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 1, p. 6"
Here's the chapter's reading. The two lines aren't placed in the realm where a comparison could be made. Each is taken in its own context.

@quote text="one of the lines ceases to be equal to the other without becoming ‘unequal’: it becomes ‘different’" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 1, p. 13"
Unequal is a relation between two measured lengths in one space. Different is what a line becomes in a figure, before any measuring. Only analytic perception, which isn't natural, can identify the two.

@chapter The reply
@words text="Attention makes them equal?"
The hypothesis replies: with attention, practice or instructions, observers report the lines as equal, so the normal sensation was there all along. The chapter answers that the first appearance was just as sensory, and analytic looking may substitute a special set up for the original phenomenon.

@words text="It overlooks phenomena"
And a footnote is fair. There's no crucial experiment against it, and induction can't fully refute it. It's discredited because it overlooks phenomena and doesn't let us understand them.

@chapter Composed, not copied
@art net-knots
Even physiology doesn't fit the transmitter picture. The text of the world is not so much copied as composed. And size and colour constancy is more perfect in children than adults. Perception is like a net whose knots show up more clearly. The theory describes the most finished layer and takes it for the first.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Perception is simply mistaken@mistaken|The reported tile is what is seen@reported tile|Practice reveals the normal sensation@practice"
So don't say perception is simply mistaken. Don't take the reported tile for what's seen: a number of centimetres already lays the lines side by side. And don't say practice reveals the normal sensation.

@chapter Try it
@words text="12 cm and 12 cm" sub="outward fins reported 3 cm longer"
Two shafts of twelve centimetres each, the outward finned one reported three centimetres longer. Compute the prediction and the gap. Then say what the gap is a gap between.
