@chapter The proofreader's trouble
@title text="Association and the Projection of Memories" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Introduction, Chapter 2" art=thumb
If you've ever proofread your own writing, you know the trouble. You read the sentence you meant, and the missing letter slides right past.

@words text="I read ‘deduction’ when the word printed is ‘destruction’"
The standard explanation is that memory fills in. The theory's own example: I read deduction when the word printed is destruction. The d wasn't given to the eye, so it must have come from memory.

@chapter Association
@list heading="Two laws of association" items="Contiguity: what occurred together@Contiguity|Resemblance: what is alike@resemblance"
First, empiricism's older tool, from Locke and Hume. Impressions that occurred together, by contiguity, or that are alike, by resemblance, come to call one another up.

@words text="as evening leads one to expect night"
Followed through, knowledge becomes a system of substitutions. One impression announces others without ever justifying it, the way evening leads one to expect night. Two paths can never be understood as the same, only associated. Understanding becomes a fraud.

@art puzzle-picture
The chapter's reply: sensations only appear within a horizon of meaning already there. Your field is made of things and spaces between things. Try to see the intervals as things, and the world changes, like a puzzle picture. Not the same elements differently associated, but another world.

@chapter Lewin's syllables
@art syllable-pairs
The evidence comes from the psychologist Kurt Lewin. Subjects learn pairs of syllables: some where the second is a softened rhyme, tak and dak; some where it's the first reversed, ged and deg.

@list heading="Same drill, two tasks" items="Find a softened rhyme for ged: slower@softened rhyme|Change the vowel: no delay@change the vowel"
Asked to find a softened rhyme, they're slower for ged than for a neutral syllable. Asked to change the vowel, there's no delay. A force would act in both tasks. So association works through the meaning a past experience has, not as a cause.

@chapter The projection of memories
@quote text="How could the evocation of memories come about unless guided by the look of the strictly visible data, and if it is thus guided, what use is it then, since the word already has its structure or its features before taking anything from the storehouse of memory?" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 2, p. 23"
Now memory filling the gaps. Here's the chapter's question back. To be called up, a memory must be guided by what's visible. And once it's guided, it has nothing left to do.

@words text="nothing but a bad metaphor hiding a deeper, ready-made recognition"
The same goes for Hering's memory colour. Something present must first tell you the object is familiar. So the projection of memories is nothing but a bad metaphor, hiding a deeper, ready made recognition.

@chapter Counting the candidates
@art word-blanks
The lab runs the associationist's model, on a list of twenty remembered words. Its misprint is the lesson's, not the book's. The printed word is h, o, x, s, e, and the model counts the misprinted letter as not seen.

@math heading="Twenty remembered words" items="ho_se → house, horse: count 2@two words;;_a_e → 18 of 20 fit@eighteen;;_a_e + context door → gate: count 1@door"
So the pattern h, o, blank, s, e fits two words, house and horse, and memory can't choose. Loosen it to blank, a, blank, e, and eighteen of the twenty fit. Put that in a sentence about a door, and only gate is left.

@art memory-shelf
That's the chapter's dilemma. If memories projected themselves onto sensations, nothing in what you see could stem their flood, and eighteen is a small flood. If instead you compare memories with the present data and keep the ones that fit, you've admitted an original text beside them.

@art memory-funnel
Look at what did the selecting. The memories sat in their list the whole time. The letters on the page and the sentence around the word chose among them. And that original text is perception itself. The theory builds perception from states of consciousness, as a house is built from bricks, with nobody who sees.

@chapter The past as a horizon
@art horizon-past
None of this removes memory from perception. The past is present as a horizon, an atmosphere that gives what you see its significance. To remember is to go back into that horizon and unfold it. To perceive is not to remember.

@list heading="Don't say" items="Memory supplies what the senses leave out@supplies|Projection answers empiricism@answer to empiricism|The past plays no part@no part"
So don't say memory supplies what the senses leave out. Don't take the projection of memories for an answer to empiricism: it's a tardy correction that shares its postulate. And don't conclude the past plays no part.
