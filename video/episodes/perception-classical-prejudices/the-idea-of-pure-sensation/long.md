@chapter Two theories
@title text="The Idea of Pure Sensation" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Introduction, Chapter 1" art=thumb
The Introduction clears the ground. Merleau-Ponty names two theories of perception he'll spend the rest of the book answering.

@list heading="Two classical theories" items="Empiricism: sensations, caused by stimuli, joined by association@Empiricism|Intellectualism: a judgement the mind makes about sensations@Intellectualism"
Empiricism, from Locke and Hume to the laboratory, says perception is built from sensations caused by stimuli and joined by association. Intellectualism, from Descartes and Kant, says perception is a judgement the mind makes about such sensations. Both begin with the sensation.

@chapter A dot of nothing
@art sensation-dot
I have a sensation of red, of blue, of heat. Nothing seems more obvious, and nothing, the chapter says, is more confused. A pure sensation would be an undifferentiated, instantaneous, dotlike impact, before even red and blue.

@words text="Nothing in experience corresponds to it"
And the authors agree that nothing in experience corresponds to it. Even an ape or a hen perceives relationships, not absolute terms. So why does theory still feel entitled to a layer of pure impressions underneath?

@chapter The white patch
@quote text="Let us imagine a white patch on a homogeneous background." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 1, p. 4"
Here's the chapter's test. Imagine a white patch on an even background.

@art white-patch
Every point of the patch shares one function: forming a shape. Its colour is firmer than the background's. Its edges belong to it, not to the background. The patch lies on the background without breaking it. And each part arouses the expectation of more than it contains.

@quote text="The perceptual ‘something’ is always in the middle of something else, it always forms part of a ‘field’." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Introduction, Chapter 1, p. 4"
The empiricist objects: then sense it point by point. But each point can only be perceived as a figure on a background in its turn. There's no perceiving below this level.

@chapter Gestalt, one step further
@words text="Figure on a ground = the definition of perception"
This finding is the Gestalt psychologists': Wertheimer, Kohler and Koffka. They said a figure on a ground is the simplest thing the senses give. Merleau-Ponty goes further. It isn't a contingent fact that leaves room for impressions beneath it. It's the very definition of the phenomenon of perception. A field offering nothing to perceive cannot be perceived.

@chapter Grouping by proximity
@art dots-pairs
The lab computes Wertheimer's proximity rule on dots in a row. The rule's ratio of two is the lesson's choice: a gap at least twice the smallest gap separates two groups.

@math heading="Pairs preset" items="dots: 0, 1, 4, 5, 8, 9@nought, one;;gaps: 1, 3, 1, 3, 1@the gaps;;threshold: 2 × 1 = 2@two times one;;groups: {0, 1} {4, 5} {8, 9}@three pairs"
Put dots at nought, one, four, five, eight and nine. The gaps are one, three, one, three, one. The threshold is two times one, two, so every gap of three separates. That gives three pairs.

@math heading="Move 5 to 6" items="gaps: 1, 3, 2, 2, 1@now the gaps;;groups: {0, 1} {4} {6} {8, 9}@four groups"
Now move the dot at five to six. Now the gaps are one, three, two, two, one, and every gap of two or more separates. That makes four groups. The dot at four didn't move, and it's lost its partner.

@art dots-shift
So whatever a dot is, its group isn't in it. A pure sensation account would have to add a law of arrangement that no dot contains. That's the first sign the unit is a construction.

@chapter Qualities and the experience error
@art carpet-red
Perhaps to sense is just to have qualities. But red is the sensed, a property of the object. The red patch on the carpet is red only through the shadow across it, over an area of a certain size, and as the woolly red of a carpet.

@words text="We make perception out of things perceived."
So why does pure sensation seem obvious? Because we move familiar objects into consciousness. Psychologists call it the experience error. We make perception out of things perceived.

@art field-edge
And the edge of your visual field isn't black or grey. What's behind you isn't without some visual presence. We must recognize the indeterminate as a positive phenomenon.

@chapter Don't overread the lab
@list heading="Watch for" items="Building experience from units@units|Reading the groups as what is seen@what anyone sees|Treating the indeterminate as a failure@failure"
Don't build experience from units. Don't read the lab's groups as what anyone sees: they're computed from measured positions, which puts the dots first. And don't treat the indeterminate as a failure to perceive.
