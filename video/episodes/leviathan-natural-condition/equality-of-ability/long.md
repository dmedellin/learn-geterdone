@chapter Where the war begins
@title text="Equality of Ability" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 13" art=thumb
Leviathan came out in London in sixteen fifty one, two years after a king was tried and beheaded in the same city. The chapter everyone remembers is the war of every man against every man. And it doesn't start with fear, or greed. It starts with arithmetic.

@quote text="the difference between man, and man, is not so considerable, as that one man can thereupon claim to himselfe any benefit, to which another may not pretend, as well as he." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 13"
Hobbes allows that one man is sometimes manifestly stronger, or quicker of mind. But when all is reckoned together, the difference between man and man is not so considerable as that one man can claim any benefit to which another may not pretend as well as he.

@words text="Equality of claim" sub="and why not I?"
So equality here means equality of claim. Nobody's superiority is so large that the rest have no ground to say: and why not I?

@chapter Plot or allies
@art sleeping-giant
His proof for the body comes in two ways. The weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, because the strongest sleeps or looks away, or by confederacy with others in the same danger, by adding strengths.

@art six-bars
The lab does the second as a sum, with the lesson's own figures, not Hobbes's. Six men have strengths of ten, eight, seven, six, five and three. Aldo is the strongest.

@math heading="The open preset" items="Bea alone: 8, not more than 10@Bea alone;;Bea + Cato: 8 + 7 = 15 > 10@Bea and Cato"
In an open fight, Bea alone, with eight, can't beat Aldo's ten. But Bea and Cato together have fifteen, and they can.

@art two-beat-one
So two others are enough to bring down the strongest. And Aldo beats each of the rest alone. All six men can be overcome by two or fewer.

@math heading="The machination preset" items="factor 4@factor of four;;Finn: 3 · 4 = 12@twelve;;12 > 10@more than"
Secret machination goes in as a factor on the attacker's strength: a sleeping victim is as good as a weaker one. With a factor of four, Finn, the weakest, has three times four, twelve. That's more than Aldo's ten. The weakest kills the strongest.

@chapter What would break it
@math heading="A giant" items="Aldo 24: 8 + 7 + 6 + 5 = 26 > 24 → 4 needed@twenty four;;Aldo 30: 8 + 7 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 29 → safe@thirty"
The lab also shows what would break the claim. Give Aldo twenty four, and four of the other five must gather: eight, seven, six and five make twenty six. Give him thirty, and all five together reach only twenty nine. He's beyond every coalition.

@art giant-beyond
So Hobbes's premise is a premise about size. It says the strongest man is never so far ahead that all the rest together can't match him. Whether men are like that, he leaves to the reader.

@chapter Equality of mind
@art wit-at-hand
Of the mind, Hobbes finds even greater equality, once you set aside science and the arts of words, which few have. Prudence is just experience, and equal time gives it equally. What makes that hard to believe is vanity. Men see their own wit at hand, and other men's at a distance.

@words text="Every man is contented with his share"
And he turns the vanity into the proof. There's no greater sign that something is equally distributed than that every man is contented with his share.

@chapter From equality to enmity
@art planter-and-invader
Then the harm. From equality of ability comes equality of hope. If two men want the same thing and can't both have it, they become enemies. Someone who plants, sows and builds may expect others to come with united forces, to take his labour, his life, or his liberty. And the invader is in like danger from another.

@words text="Aristotle: some are fit to command?"
Two objections. Aristotle held some men are by nature fitter to command. Hobbes answers that it's against experience: the wise in their own conceit don't often win by force. And do the weak combine so easily? Whether allies trust each other is for chapter fourteen. The lab just adds.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Men are equally strong and clever@The first|The equality is a compliment@The second|The lab predicts the weak will band together@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Hobbes says men are equally strong and clever. He says the difference isn't enough for a title. The second is reading equality as a compliment, when it's the premise of the danger. And the third is thinking the lab predicts the weak will band together. It only says they'd be strong enough.
