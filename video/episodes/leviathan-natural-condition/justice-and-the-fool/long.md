@chapter The third law
@title text="Justice and the Fool" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 15" art=thumb
Chapter fifteen opens with a law, and then lets an opponent speak. The opponent is the Fool, and Hobbes takes him seriously.

@art fountain-of-justice
First the third law of nature: that men perform their covenants made. Without it, covenants are in vain, and but empty words. In this law lies the fountain and original of justice.

@list heading="Justice defined" items="No covenant: nothing is unjust@Where no|Injustice: not performing a covenant@not performance|Whatever is not unjust is just@not unjust"
Where no covenant's been made, no right's been transferred, so nothing can be unjust. Once a covenant's made, breaking it is unjust. The definition of injustice is no other than the not performance of covenant. And whatever is not unjust is just.

@art coercive-power
But covenants of trust are void while there's fear of non performance. So before just and unjust can have a place, there must be a coercive power, with a punishment greater than the benefit expected from the breach. That same power begins property. Where there's no own, there's no injustice.

@chapter The Fool speaks
@quote text="The Foole hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 15"
Then the Fool. The fool hath said in his heart, there is no such thing as justice. Every man's preservation is in his own care. So to keep or not keep covenants isn't against reason, when it serves his benefit.

@art crown-grab
He doesn't deny covenants exist, or that breach is called injustice. He asks whether injustice may sometimes stand with reason, especially when the gain puts a man beyond the power of others. Not the petty thief, but the one who gains a kingdom. In sixteen fifty one, Englishmen had just seen a king deposed and executed.

@chapter Hobbes's reply
@words text="Not where there is no security on either side"
Hobbes calls this specious reasoning, and false. First he draws a boundary. The question isn't about promises with no security on either side. It's where one party has already performed, or a power can make him perform. And he meets the Fool on his own ground, benefit.

@art lucky-accident
Step one. An act that tends to your destruction, as far as can be foreseen and reckoned on, isn't made wise because some unexpected accident turns it to your benefit. Reason judges by what can be reckoned on, not by luck.

@art confederates
Step two. Nobody can defend himself without confederates. A man who declares it reasonable to deceive those who help him can be received into society only by their error.

@art cast-out
And those are errors a man cannot reasonably reckon upon as the means of his security. If he's cast out, he perishes. If he's kept, it's only by others' ignorance.

@chapter The Fool's sum
@math heading="The ruin preset" items="gain 10, loss 100@ten;;chance 1/20: 10 − 5 = 5@one in twenty;;chance 1/10: 10 − 10 = 0@one in ten;;chance 1/5: 10 − 20 = −10@one in five;;threshold: 10/100 = 1/10@gain over the loss"
The lab does the Fool's sum. Breaking gains ten. Being cast out costs a hundred. At a chance of one in twenty of being found out, breaking is worth five. At one in ten, nothing. At one in five, minus ten. It pays only below the gain over the loss, one tenth.

@math heading="Perish and kingdom" items="perish: loss 1000 → threshold 1/100@a thousand;;kingdom: gain 100, loss 20 → threshold 5@kingdom"
Count perishing as a loss of a thousand, and the threshold falls to one in a hundred. The Fool's best case is the kingdom: a gain of a hundred against a loss of twenty. The threshold is five, above any chance, so breaking pays even if he's sure to be caught.

@math heading="Reckoning honestly" items="chance he may reckon with: 1@reckon with is one;;ruin: 10 − 100 = −90@minus ninety"
Hobbes doesn't deny the arithmetic. He attacks the inputs. The breach itself declares the breaker, so he may not count on escaping. The chance he may reckon with is one, and in the ruin case breaking is worth minus ninety. As for the kingdom, gaining it by rebellion can't reasonably be expected, and it teaches others to do the same.

@chapter Ever since
@words text="Hume's sensible knave, 1751"
The reply has been argued over ever since. Hume's sensible knave, often read beside the Fool, thinks he may profit by a breach that goes unseen. Hume answered that he loses the satisfaction of an honest character. Hobbes's reply is different. It doesn't say he'll be caught. It says he may not reckon on escaping.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The Fool is a fool in the ordinary sense@The first|The lab is Hobbes's reply@The second|The sovereign makes keeping reasonable@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the Fool is stupid. He argues from benefit, as Hobbes does. The second is reading the lab as Hobbes's reply. It's the Fool's sum. And the third is thinking the sovereign is what makes keeping reasonable. In the reply, it's the confederacy you need.
