@chapter Paying in advance
@title text="Contracts and Covenants" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 14" art=thumb
Think of buying a concert ticket months ahead, or paying a deposit on a room. You hand over your money now, and the other side's part isn't done yet. You're trusting more than you're trusted. That's the position this chapter is about.

@words text="The mutual transferring of right is contract"
Hobbes starts a step earlier. The mutual transferring of right is what men call contract. And transferring the right to a thing is different from delivering the thing itself.

@art ready-money
Sometimes the right passes in the same moment as the thing, as in buying and selling with ready money.

@art calendar-gap
But the thing may be delivered some time after. One party may deliver now and leave the other to perform later. Then there's a gap in the contract, and the gap is where the trouble will be.

@quote text="and in the mean time be trusted; and then the Contract on his part, is called Pact, or Covenant:" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 14"
Then the other party is to perform at some fixed time after, and in the mean time be trusted; and then the contract on his part is called pact, or covenant. His performance is keeping faith. Failing, if it's voluntary, is violation of faith.

@chapter Free gift
@art gift-box
When the transfer isn't mutual, it's not a contract but a free gift. And look at the motives Hobbes lists: hope of friendship or service, a name for charity, relief from the pain of compassion, a reward in heaven. Every one is some good to the giver.

@chapter Present and future words
@math heading="Words and the right" items="Volo hoc tuum esse cras: I will that this be thine tomorrow → transfers@I will that this be thine;;Cras dabo: I will give it thee tomorrow → transfers nothing@I will give it thee"
A right passes by signs. Words of the present or past, I give, I have granted, transfer it. Bare words of the future, in a gift, don't. I will that this be thine tomorrow gives away tomorrow's right today. I will give it thee tomorrow is a sign you haven't given yet.

@art race-prize
Two exceptions. If other signs show the will, future words can transfer, as when someone offers a prize to the first to finish a race. If he didn't mean it, he shouldn't have let them run. And in a contract, a promise binds like a covenant, since the other wouldn't have performed first otherwise.

@chapter Merit
@words text="He that performeth first merits what he is to receive" sub="and has it as due"
He who performs first in a contract merits what he's to receive from the other's performance, and has it as due. The race winner merits his prize too. But in a contract you merit by your own power and the other's need. In a gift, only by the giver's kindness.

@chapter Who should go first?
@art grain-sale
The lab takes the one who goes first, with the lesson's own figures. A seller delivers grain now, and the buyer pays later. The grain is worth five to the seller, and he's to be paid eight. The buyer values it at nine. And there's no sanction.

@math heading="The sale preset" items="buyer keeps: 9 − 8 = 1@keeps;;buyer breaks: 9 − 0 = 9@breaks;;breaking better by 8@better by eight;;seller goes first, buyer breaks: −5@minus five;;seller doesn't perform: 0@nothing"
If the buyer keeps the covenant, he's ahead by nine minus eight, one. If he breaks it, he's ahead by nine. Breaking is better by eight, the price he saves. So the seller who goes first ends at minus five, while not performing leaves him with nothing. He's not reasonable to go first.

@list heading="Same shape" items="Hire: keep 3, break 12, worker left at −6@the hire|Loan: keep 3, break 14, lender left at −10@the loan"
The hire and the loan have the same shape. In the hire, the master keeps for three or breaks for twelve, and the worker who laboured first is left at minus six. In the loan, the borrower keeps for three or breaks for fourteen, and the lender is left at minus ten.

@art sword-ahead
That's a result about a man who counts only his own gain, in one deal, with no sanction. The lab leaves out generosity, which Hobbes calls too rare to presume on, and the future dealings that depend on a man's word. What a sword does to the payoffs is the next lesson.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A promise is a transfer@The first|A covenant needs both to deliver at once@The second|The lab says covenants never bind@And the third"
The first slip is treating a bare promise as a transfer. In a gift, future words alone pass nothing. The second is thinking a covenant needs both sides to deliver at once. That's ready money. And the third is reading the lab as saying covenants never bind. It only computes the incentives.
