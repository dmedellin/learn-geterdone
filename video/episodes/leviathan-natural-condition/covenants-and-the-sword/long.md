@chapter Who performs first?
@title text="Covenants Made Void and Kept" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 14" art=thumb
Last time, a seller delivered grain and trusted the buyer to pay. And the buyer did better to break. Now Hobbes says when such a covenant is void, and what changes it.

@quote text="in the condition of meer Nature, (which is a condition of Warre of every man against every man,) upon any reasonable suspition, it is Voyd:" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 14"
If a covenant is made where the parties trust one another, then in the condition of mere nature, which is a condition of war of every man against every man, upon any reasonable suspicion, it is void.

@art weak-words
Why? The one who performs first has no assurance the other will follow, because the bonds of words are too weak to bridle ambition, avarice and anger without fear of some coercive power. And in mere nature, where all are equal, nobody's strong enough to enforce. Going first just betrays you to your enemy.

@chapter Adding a sword
@math heading="No sanction" items="buyer keeps: 9 − 8 = 1@keeping;;buyer breaks: 9@breaking;;seller goes first: −5@minus five"
The lab runs the same sale. The grain is worth five to the seller, who expects eight. The buyer values it at nine. With no sanction, the buyer is one ahead by keeping and nine ahead by breaking. He breaks, and the seller ends at minus five.

@art sword-over-two
But in a civil state, with a power set up to constrain those who'd break faith, that fear is no longer reasonable. And then the one who is to perform first is obliged to. So add a penalty for breaking that is certain to fall.

@math heading="A certain sanction" items="sanction 9: break pays 9 − 9 = 0 < 1 → keeps@sanction of nine;;seller gains 3@gains three;;least sanction: 8, the price saved@eight"
With a sanction of nine, breaking pays nothing, which is less than one, so the buyer keeps and the seller gains three. The smallest sanction that works is eight, the whole price the buyer saves. Then breaking and keeping both pay one, and the lab's tie rule says he keeps.

@art dice-sword
But size isn't everything. A sword only counts when it actually falls.

@math heading="A sword that misses" items="sanction 9 at chance 1/2: 9 − 9/2 = 9/2 > 1 → breaks@half the time;;least sanction: 8 ÷ 1/2 = 16@sixteen"
If the penalty of nine falls only half the time, it's expected to cost nine halves. Breaking pays nine halves, more than one, and he breaks again. At half the certainty, the least sanction is not eight but sixteen. Force that sometimes misses counts for less.

@words text="Some new fact, or sign of the will not to perform"
Hobbes adds a qualification. The fear that voids a covenant must arise after it was made, from some new fact, or sign of the will not to perform. What couldn't stop you promising shouldn't excuse you from performing. How that fits the first rule, the lesson leaves unsettled.

@chapter Fear doesn't void a promise
@art ransom
You'd think a promise extorted by fear must be void. Hobbes says the opposite. Covenant to pay a ransom for your life, and you're bound. Prisoners trusted with their ransom must pay it. A weaker prince who makes a bad peace for fear must keep it. Whatever you may lawfully do without obligation, you may lawfully covenant through fear.

@art shield-kept
But some covenants are always void. A covenant not to defend yourself from force by force is always void. You may covenant: unless I do so, kill me. But not: unless I do so, I won't resist you when you come to kill me. By nature, a man chooses the lesser evil, danger in resisting, over certain death in not resisting.

@chapter Only two helps
@quote text="The Passion to be reckoned upon, is Fear" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 14"
So what holds a man when nobody can compel him? Words are too weak. There are only two helps: fear of the consequence of breaking his word, or glory in appearing not to need to break it. That generosity is too rare to presume on. The passion to be reckoned upon is fear.

@art oath-altar
Between two men with no civil power, all they can do is swear by the god each fears. An oath adds nothing to the obligation. What it adds is fear.

@math heading="The oath preset" items="penalty 20, believed as likely as not: 10@twenty;;10 > 8 saved: break pays −1@minus one;;keep pays 1 → keeps@he keeps"
In the lesson's numbers, a man who thinks the penalty is twenty, and as likely as not, expects to lose ten. That beats the eight he saves. Breaking pays minus one, so he keeps.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A covenant extorted by fear is void@The first|Any sword makes a covenant safe@The second|Keep or break is what he ought to do@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a covenant extorted by fear is void. Hobbes says it binds. The second is thinking any sword makes a covenant safe. It counts only as its chance times its size. And the third is reading the lab's keep or break as what a man ought to do. The lab says what pays.
