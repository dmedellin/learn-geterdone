@chapter An argument, not a prehistory
@title text="The War of Every Man Against Every Man" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 13" art=thumb
Most people know this chapter by one phrase about the life of man, and take it for a picture of brutes in the distant past. Read in order, it's an argument, with steps.

@art anticipation
It starts with distrust, which Hobbes calls diffidence. If you can't trust anyone, the most reasonable way to secure yourself is anticipation: by force or wiles, master everyone you can, until no power is great enough to endanger you. That, he says, is no more than self preservation requires.

@art one-conqueror
Then the clever step. Some take pleasure in conquest beyond what their security needs. And others, who'd be glad to live at ease within modest bounds, can't then stay on the defensive for long. So one man who enjoys conquest is enough to make the rest act as he does.

@chapter Three causes of quarrel
@list heading="Three causes" items="Competition: invade for gain@Competition|Diffidence: invade for safety@Diffidence|Glory: invade for reputation@Glory"
Every man wants others to value him at his own rate, and pushes back at signs of contempt. So there are three principal causes of quarrel. Competition makes men invade for gain. Diffidence, for safety. And glory, for reputation, over trifles: a word, a smile, a different opinion.

@chapter What war is
@art foul-weather
War isn't only battle. It's a stretch of time in which the will to fight is sufficiently known. The nature of foul weather lies not in a shower or two of rain, but in an inclination to it over many days. So a man can be at war while home in bed. What's missing is assurance.

@art empty-fields
In such a condition there's no place for industry, because its fruit is uncertain. No farming, no shipping, no building, no arts, no letters, no society.

@quote text="And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 13"
And worst of all, continual fear and danger of violent death. And the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

@art locked-door
Doubt it? Hobbes offers a free experiment. You arm yourself for a journey, lock your doors at night, lock your chests at home, even knowing there are laws and armed officers. Don't you accuse mankind by your actions as much as I do by my words? But neither of us, he adds, accuses man's nature. Passions aren't sins until a law forbids them.

@art two-gladiators
Was it ever so? Never generally, over the whole world, he thinks. But civil war shows what men fall into. And sovereigns stand like gladiators, forts and spies pointed at each other.

@words text="Locke · Rousseau"
Locke held the state of nature and the state of war are as far apart as peace and enmity. Rousseau said Hobbes gave natural man the passions of men already in society.

@chapter No law, no injustice
@quote text="Where there is no common Power, there is no Law: where no Law, no Injustice. Force, and Fraud, are in warre the two Cardinall vertues." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 13"
Then the consequence. Where there is no common power, there is no law; where no law, no injustice. Force and fraud are in war the two cardinal virtues. Justice relates to men in society, not in solitude. Nothing is yours but what you can get, for as long as you can keep it.

@chapter Three tables
@math heading="Competition and diffidence" items="competition: attack a peaceful man 6 > peace 4 → both attack, 1 each@competition;;diffidence: lone attacker 4 < peace 5@diffidence;;keep peace if q > 2/3@two thirds"
The lab turns the causes into games, with the lesson's figures. In competition, attacking a peaceful man pays six against peace's four, so both attack and get one each. In diffidence, a lone attacker takes only four against peace's five. Peace is worth keeping if the other keeps it with a chance above two thirds.

@math heading="The pair preset" items="conqueror: attack beats peace either way@the conqueror;;content man vs attacker: attack 2, peace 0@two against nothing;;equilibrium: attack, attack, 2 each@equilibrium;;peace, peace would pay 4 each@four each"
Now a conqueror meets a content man. For the conqueror, attack beats peace whatever the other does. Against an attacker, the content man gets two by fighting, two against nothing. So the one equilibrium is both attacking, two each, though peace would pay four each. Glory over a smile gets no number at all.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="War needs a majority of aggressors@The first|War means constant fighting@The second|The tables are Hobbes's whole case@And the third"
The first slip is thinking war needs a majority of aggressors. One conqueror is enough. The second is thinking war means constant fighting, when it's a known disposition. And the third is reading the tables as Hobbes's whole case. They show the argument as incentives, not that the incentives are real.
