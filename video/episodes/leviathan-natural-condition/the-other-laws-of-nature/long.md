@chapter After the third law
@title text="The Other Laws of Nature" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 15" art=thumb
So far the chain is short. Seek peace. Lay down your right when others will. Keep the covenants you make. Now Hobbes derives more laws the same way: without each one, men would stay at war.

@art gift-returned
The fourth is gratitude. As justice depends on a covenant before it, gratitude depends on a free gift before it. If givers see their goodwill frustrated, there'll be no beginning of benevolence or trust, and men stay at war.

@art rough-stone
The fifth is complaisance. Builders cast aside a stone whose rough shape takes more room than it fills. So too the man who clings to what he doesn't need and others do.

@chapter Pardon and revenge
@quote text="That upon caution of the Future time, a man ought to pardon the offences past of them that repenting, desire it." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 15"
The sixth: that upon caution of the future time, a man ought to pardon the offences past of them that, repenting, desire it. Pardon granted to those who persist isn't peace, but fear. Refused to those who give assurance, it's a sign of aversion to peace.

@words text="Not the evil past, but the good to follow"
The seventh follows. In revenge, men look not at the greatness of the evil past, but at the greatness of the good to follow. Punish only to correct the offender or direct others. Revenge for its own sake is glorying in another's hurt, the quarrel over a smile, turned into a law against it.

@chapter A repeated dilemma
@art two-rows-of-choices
The lab plays a repeated dilemma. Each round, two players help or refuse. Both helping pays three each. A refusal against a helper pays five, and the helper nothing. Two refusers get one each. The strategies are the lab's stand-ins for dispositions, not Hobbes's.

@math heading="Ten rounds" items="gratitude: TFT vs TFT → 30 each@thirty each;;echo: TFT vs suspicious TFT → 25 each@twenty five;;pardon: TF2T vs suspicious → 27 and 32@twenty seven"
Two tit for tat players help first and copy, so both help every round: thirty each, where two refusers would get ten. Meet one who refuses first, and the refusal echoes back and forth: twenty five each. Let one refusal pass, tit for two tats, and they settle into helping. The pardoner gets twenty seven, the offender thirty two.

@math heading="Prey and once" items="prey: always help vs always refuse → 0 and 50@nothing;;once: TFT vs refuser, one round → 0 and 5@single round"
Now Hobbes's warning. A man who always helps meets one who always refuses: he gets nothing in ten rounds, the refuser fifty. And in a single round, tit for tat against a refuser gets nothing, the refuser five, and nothing follows.

@math heading="Round robin of seven" items="ten rounds: TF2T 185, TFT 184, ALLC 177, ALLD 146@ten rounds;;one round: refusers 27, helpers 15@one round"
Let all seven strategies meet. Over ten rounds, tit for two tats wins with one eighty five, tit for tat has one eighty four, the always helper one seventy seven, the always refuser one forty six. In one round, the refusers tie at twenty seven and every helper has fifteen.

@art tournament-ring
Robert Axelrod ran tournaments like this about nineteen eighty and reported tit for tat did best. Here, with a different field, it comes second by a point. So the winner depends on the field. What Hobbes needs is smaller: where there'll be another meeting, reward and pardon beat refusing. Where there won't, they don't.

@chapter The rest of the nineteen
@art equal-scale
The rest are rules for equals. Don't show contempt. Acknowledge every man your equal by nature. Against Aristotle, Hobbes argues a dilemma: if nature made men equal, admit it. If not, men who think themselves equal won't make peace except on equal terms, so equality must be admitted anyway. Then equity, lots, safe conduct for mediators, and no man a judge in his own cause.

@words text="Do not that to another, which thou wouldest not have done to thy selfe"
All of them are summed in one rule anyone can follow. Do not that to another which thou wouldst not have done to thyself.

@chapter Inner and outer court
@art two-courts
The laws oblige in the inner court always: they bind you to desire that they take place. In the outer court, to act, not always. Keep them where nobody else does, and you make yourself prey. But with sufficient security, not keeping them seeks war. They're eternal, and easy, needing only an unfeigned and constant endeavour.

@words text="Conclusions, or Theoremes" sub="laws properly only as God's word"
Last, a qualification many miss. These dictates are called laws improperly. They're conclusions, or theorems, about what conduces to preservation. Only as delivered in the word of God are they properly laws. Readers still divide over how much that takes back.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Tit for tat is Hobbes's law@The first|In foro interno means in the mind only@The second|The laws are commands@And the third"
The first slip is thinking tit for tat is the law of nature. The winner depends on the field. The second is reading the inner court as the mind only, when the laws bind to act given security. And the third is taking the laws as commands, which Hobbes calls improper.
