@chapter Liberty and curfew
@title text="The Right and the Law of Nature" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 14" art=thumb
Think of an ordinary evening. You're at liberty to go out or stay in, and nobody's bound to help you either way. A curfew is different. It doesn't leave you free. It binds you to one.

@art open-gate-and-wall
That's the contrast Hobbes draws. The right of nature is the liberty each man has to use his own power as he will, for preserving his own life. A law of nature is a rule found out by reason, forbidding what destroys life.

@words text="Law and right differ as obligation and liberty"
So law and right differ as much as obligation and liberty, which in one and the same matter are inconsistent. A right binds nobody else. It only says no one has taken the liberty away.

@chapter A right to everything
@art everything-claimed
In the war, every man is his own judge of what helps him. So every man has a right to everything, even to one another's body. That isn't praise. Its consequence is that nobody, however strong or wise, can be secure of living out his natural span.

@list heading="Reason's rule, in two halves" items="Seek peace, as far as there's hope@seek peace|Otherwise, use all the helps of war@all the helps"
From that, reason draws a rule in two halves. Every man ought to seek peace, as far as he has hope of obtaining it. And when he can't, he may use all the helps and advantages of war. The first half is the fundamental law of nature. The second is the sum of the right of nature.

@chapter The second law
@quote text="That a man be willing, when others are so too, as farre-forth, as for Peace, and defence of himselfe he shall think it necessary, to lay down this right to all things;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 14"
While every man holds his right to everything, all are at war. So from the first law comes a second. That a man be willing, when others are so too, as far as he thinks necessary for peace and his defence, to lay down this right to all things.

@art disarm-alone
That condition, when others are so too, is part of the law. If others won't lay down their right, there's no reason for you to. That were to expose yourself to prey, which no man is bound to.

@art two-shields-down
So both set the right down together. And each is content with as much liberty against other men as he would allow other men against himself. Hobbes ties it to the Gospel: whatever you require others to do to you, do to them.

@chapter As an argument
@math heading="The derivation preset" items="o → h: others willing, so hope@others are willing;;h → e: hope, so seek peace@first law;;e → l: seek peace, so lay down@lay down;;∴ o → l   valid, 16 rows@sixteen rows"
The lab sets it out with the lesson's letters. If others are willing, he has hope of peace. If he has hope, he seeks peace: that's the first law. If he seeks peace, he's willing to lay down his right. So if others are willing, he is. Sixteen rows, no counterexample. It's valid.

@math heading="Two failures" items="drop o → h: o true, h, e, l false@drop the first;;unconditional l: invalid@unconditional"
Drop the first premise, and one row refutes it: the others are willing, he has no hope, and he doesn't lay down his right. And the unconditional conclusion, he lays down his right, full stop, fails too. Nothing in the premises says the others are willing. The law is conditional.

@chapter Laying down a right
@art step-aside
What does laying down a right do? You give the other no new right, since he already had a right to everything. You just stand out of his way. You can renounce a right, not caring who benefits, or transfer it to someone in particular. Either way, you're bound not to hinder him.

@words text="Injustice: to undo what one had voluntarily done"
To hinder him then is injustice: voluntarily undoing what you voluntarily did. Hobbes compares it to absurdity in argument, contradicting what you maintained at the start.

@art shield-kept
Some rights can't be laid down. You can't give up the right to resist those who attack you by force to take your life, or to resist wounds, chains and prison. You can't be aiming at any good to yourself by it. And words alone are weak. Nothing is more easily broken than a man's word. Their strength comes from fear of what follows.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A right to everything is a licence@The first|Dropping the condition from the second law@The second|The lab proves the laws of nature@And the third"
The first slip is taking a right to everything for a licence. It's the reason there's no security. The second is dropping the condition, when others are so too. And the third is thinking the lab proves the laws of nature. It only shows the second follows from the first.
