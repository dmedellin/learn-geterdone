@chapter The compensation theory
@title text="Machinery and the Displaced Worker" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 15" art=thumb
Here's a serious argument, in its best form. A machine makes a product cheaper. Buyers have money left over and spend it elsewhere, and those makers hire more hands. Nothing is destroyed. Purchasing power has moved, and the jobs follow.

@quote text="insist that all machinery that displaces workmen, simultaneously and necessarily sets free an amount of capital adequate to employ the same identical workmen." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 15, §6"
James Mill, McCulloch, Torrens, Senior, John Stuart Mill and others, Marx says, insist that all machinery that displaces workmen, simultaneously and necessarily sets free an amount of capital adequate to employ the same identical workmen.

@words text="Ricardo, 1821: “injurious to the labouring class”"
David Ricardo believed this for years. Then, in the third edition of his Principles in eighteen twenty one, he added a chapter saying he'd been wrong: machinery could be injurious to the labouring class. Marx's opponents are the ones who didn't follow him.

@chapter The carpet factory
@art carpet-factory
Marx tests the narrow claim, that capital is set free. A carpet manufacturer employs a hundred men at thirty pounds a year, so his variable capital is three thousand pounds. He spends another three thousand on raw material. He discharges fifty men, and buys machinery for fifteen hundred pounds.

@math heading="The carpet factory" items="variable before: 100 · £30 = £3,000@three thousand;;wages saved: 50 · £30 = £1,500@fifty times thirty;;set free: £1,500 − £1,500 = £0@nothing;;after: £4,500 constant · £1,500 variable@forty five hundred"
Before, a hundred times thirty is three thousand of variable capital. The wages saved are fifty times thirty, fifteen hundred. Take away the machine's fifteen hundred, and nothing is set free. His six thousand is now forty five hundred constant and fifteen hundred variable.

@quote text="variable has been changed into constant capital." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 15, §6"
Instead of being set free, part of the capital is locked up so it no longer buys labour-power. Variable has been changed into constant capital.

@art wage-fund-locked
The six thousand pounds now employs only fifty men. The money that paid the other fifty exists as machinery.

@chapter The machine-builders
@art machine-builders
What about the men who build the machine? The fifteen hundred pays them, but also their materials and their master's surplus value. At the best, Marx says, its construction employs fewer men than its use displaces, and employs them once.

@math heading="Machine-building, c/v of 4" items="wages: £1,500 / (1 + 4) = £300@divide by five;;jobs: £300 / £30 = 10, once@ten men"
The lab gives machine building four pounds of materials for every pound of wages. So divide by five: three hundred pounds of wages. That pays ten men, for as long as it takes to build the machine. Fifty displaced, ten re-employed.

@math heading="Other cases" items="£1,000 machine: 50/3 + 20/3 = 70/3 re-employed@a thousand pounds;;all £1,500 as wages: 50 re-employed, while it's built@every penny"
If the machine had cost a thousand pounds, five hundred is set free, enough for about sixteen, plus about seven in machine building. And only if every penny of the fifteen hundred were paid as wages, with no materials and no profit, would all fifty be re-employed, and only while the machine is built.

@chapter Buyers, not capital
@art buyers-to-non-buyers
The apologists mean something else: the food and clothing the fifty used to buy is liberated to employ them. But to the workers those necessaries were never capital. They were commodities, and the workers were buyers. Thrown out of work, they stop buying, which can throw others out of work too.

@art knife
Marx parodies their last defence: the harm comes from capitalist use, not machinery itself. It's Bill Sikes before the jury. No doubt the throat of this commercial traveller has been cut. But that is not my fault, it is the fault of the knife!

@chapter Repelled and attracted
@art repel-attract
Marx grants what's true. A capital of five hundred pounds a week, two fifths constant, pays three hundred men. Mechanised to four fifths constant, a hundred remain. Grow it to two thousand, and four hundred are employed, a rise of a hundred. But under the old technique it would have employed twelve hundred.

@words text="Data over decades · mechanism in the act"
Economists mostly find employment has grown with mechanisation over decades, while particular trades were ruined. Marx's claim is narrower. Nothing in installing a machine re-employs the people it displaces. Anything that does comes from new capital, later, elsewhere.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Freed capital re-employs the displaced@The first|Workers' necessaries as capital set free@The second|Machinery must cut total employment@And the third"
The first slip is thinking freed capital re-employs the displaced. Here nothing was freed. The second is counting the workers' necessaries as capital set free. And the third is concluding machinery must cut total employment. Marx doesn't say that.
