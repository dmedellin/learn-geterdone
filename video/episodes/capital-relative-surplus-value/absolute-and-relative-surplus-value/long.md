@chapter Productive labour
@title text="Absolute and Relative Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 16" art=thumb
Chapter sixteen opens with a definition that widens and narrows at once. Work is now done by a collective labourer, so you no longer need to do manual work yourself to labour productively. Being one organ of it is enough.

@art teaching-factory
But for capital, only labour that produces surplus value counts as productive. A schoolmaster who works like a horse to enrich the school proprietor is productive. It makes no difference that the owner chose a teaching factory instead of a sausage factory. To be a productive labourer, Marx says, is not a piece of luck, but a misfortune.

@chapter Each is the other
@quote text="From one standpoint, any distinction between absolute and relative surplus-value appears illusory." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 16"
Now the question. From one standpoint, any distinction between absolute and relative surplus value appears illusory.

@art two-sides
Relative surplus value is absolute, because any surplus needs a day longer than the necessary time. Absolute surplus value is relative, because the necessary time must already be short enough to leave room. Read that way, every surplus value is both.

@words text="Ask how the rate is raised"
The appearance vanishes once you ask how the rate is raised. With labour-power paid at its value, there are only two ways. Lengthen the day, or, with the day given, change the proportion between its parts.

@chapter Classify by what moved
@art three-changes
So the rule is: a longer day is absolute, a shorter necessary time is relative, and both at once is both.

@math heading="From 12 h with 6 necessary, 100%" items="lengthen to 14: 8 / 6 = 133.33% · absolute@fourteen;;necessary to 5: 7 / 5 = 140% · relative@cut the necessary;;both: (14 − 5) / 5 = 9/5 = 180%@does both"
Start with twelve hours, six necessary, a hundred percent. Lengthen to fourteen, and it's eight over six, a hundred and thirty three percent: absolute. Keep twelve but cut the necessary time to five, and it's seven over five, a hundred and forty: relative. The third case does both: nine over five, a hundred and eighty percent.

@math heading="Where the 3 extra hours came from" items="absolute: 14 − 12 = 2 h@two hours;;relative: 6 − 5 = 1 h@one hour"
Surplus labour grew from six hours to nine. Two hours came from the end of the day, absolute. One hour came from its necessary part, relative.

@chapter The sago-cutter
@art sago-palm
Now a tempting explanation: that surplus exists because nature is generous. Marx grants the premise. Without some productivity, there's no time to work for others. Where soil is fertile and wants are few, the necessary time is short. His example is a sago cutter in the Asiatic Archipelago, where people cut their bread in the forest like firewood.

@math heading="The sago-cutter" items="alone: 2 h a day, all necessary · rate 0@two hours;;wage labourer, 12-hour day: 10 / 2 = 500%@five hundred"
Marx supposes he meets all his wants in twelve hours a week. Put that as two hours on each of six days, all necessary: a rate of zero. Make him a wage labourer on a twelve hour day. The necessary time is still two hours, so the rate is ten over two, five hundred percent.

@quote text="It explains only why his necessary labour-time would be limited to one day a week." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 16"
The bounty of nature doesn't explain why he must work six days a week, or furnish five days of surplus labour. It explains only why his necessary labour time would be limited to one day a week. Favourable conditions give only the possibility, never the reality, of surplus labour.

@chapter Mill's bird's nest
@art birds-nest
John Stuart Mill said capital yields profit because food and tools last longer than the time taken to make them. Marx's reply: then a baker could never profit like a machine maker, and if a bird's nest didn't last longer than it takes to build, birds would have to do without nests.

@art profit-vs-surplus
He corrects Mill's arithmetic too. Twenty on a hundred pounds of wages is twenty percent surplus value. But with four hundred pounds of means of production, profit is twenty on five hundred, four percent. Later, Boehm Bawerk built a careful theory of interest from time preference, which Marx never met.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Surplus needs nature's surplus@The first|Every rise is relative@The second|Surplus value rate = profit rate@And the third"
The first slip is thinking surplus value needs a natural surplus of the soil. The second is calling every rise in the rate relative because productivity is involved. And the third is equating the rate of surplus value with the rate of profit.
