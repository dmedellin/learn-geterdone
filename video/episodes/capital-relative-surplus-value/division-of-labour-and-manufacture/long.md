@chapter A mechanism made of people
@title text="Division of Labour and Manufacture" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 14" art=thumb
Manufacture, Marx says, arose in two ways. Independent wheelwrights, harness makers and gilders were gathered under one roof to make carriages, and each slowly lost his craft. Or a capitalist hired many needle makers who each made whole needles, until he split the work into twenty operations.

@art carriage-and-needle
Either way, the end is the same: a productive mechanism whose parts are human beings.

@chapter The detail worker
@art detail-worker
Why is the detail worker faster? He no longer changes place or tool, so the gaps in his day close up. A lifetime on one motion teaches him the least effort it needs. His tools are reshaped for his one task: Birmingham made five hundred kinds of hammer. But it's all still handicraft, dependent on hand, strength and skill.

@chapter Babbage's point
@art wage-hierarchy
There's a second gain. Charles Babbage, in eighteen thirty two, put it exactly. A master who divides the work can buy exactly the skill and force each process needs, where a single craftsman must be paid at the rate of the hardest operation for all of them.

@words text="In both cases the value of labour-power falls"
Marx takes the point whole. Manufacture builds a hierarchy of labour-powers with a scale of wages. It creates unskilled workers with no apprenticeship cost, and shrinks it for the skilled. In both cases the value of labour-power falls. The lab doesn't compute this. Its workers all stand at one rate.

@chapter A fixed ratio
@quote text="the founder casts 2,000 type an hour, the breaker breaks up 4,000, and the rubber polishes 8,000." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 14, §3"
Different operations take unequal times. In type manufacture there are four founders and two breakers to one rubber. The founder casts two thousand type an hour, the breaker breaks up four thousand, and the rubber polishes eight thousand.

@art type-line
If one founder fed one breaker, the breaker would stand idle half the time. The workshop balances only when every step turns out the same number an hour. So the workers at a step must be inversely proportional to its rate.

@math heading="Founders, breakers, rubbers" items="inverses: 1/2000 · 1/4000 · 1/8000@one over each rate;;× 8000: 4 · 2 · 1@times eight thousand;;workers: 4 + 2 + 1 = 7@seven;;check: 4·2000 = 2·4000 = 1·8000 = 8000 an hour@check"
Take one over each rate. Multiply by eight thousand and you get four, two and one. That's seven workers. And check: four times two thousand, two times four thousand, one times eight thousand, all eight thousand an hour.

@art glass-hole
So a type shop grows only by whole groups of seven: fourteen, twenty one, never eight. The minimum capital keeps rising. Marx's glass house shows another shape: the hole, five men at each mouth of the furnace, who can only work as a group of five.

@chapter Two divisions of labour
@quote text="in the society outside the workshop, chance and caprice have full play in distributing the producers and their means of production among the various branches of industry." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 14, §4"
Is this society's division of labour in miniature? Smith thought so. Marx says inside the workshop an iron law of proportion rules, while in the society outside the workshop, chance and caprice have full play in distributing the producers and their means of production among the various branches of industry.

@art workshop-vs-market
The tanner sells leather and the shoemaker buys it. What binds them is that their products are commodities. The founder sells nothing. He's bound to the breaker because both sold their labour-power to one capitalist, who fixes their proportions in advance. Society's proportions are found only afterwards, through prices.

@words text="“We make a nation of Helots”"
The price is paid by the worker. Smith wrote that a man on a few simple operations becomes as stupid and ignorant as a human creature can, and proposed state schooling. Ferguson exclaimed: we make a nation of Helots. Marx calls manufacture a refined and civilised method of exploitation.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The workshop as society in miniature@The first|Giving the fastest step the most workers@The second|Growing one worker at a time@And the third"
The first slip is treating the workshop as society in miniature. The second is giving the fastest step the most workers, when the slow step needs the most hands. And the third is thinking a manufacture can grow one worker at a time.
