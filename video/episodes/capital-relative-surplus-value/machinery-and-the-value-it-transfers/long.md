@chapter Lightening toil?
@title text="Machinery and the Value It Transfers" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 15" art=thumb
The chapter opens with John Stuart Mill: it's questionable if all the mechanical inventions yet made have lightened the day's toil of any human being. Marx's reply is that lightening toil was never the aim. Machinery is a means for producing surplus value.

@chapter What a machine is
@art tool-in-hand
What is a machine? Not a tool moved by something other than a person. On that test an ox plough would be a machine. A machine takes the tool out of the worker's hand and works it itself, escaping the limit of the body. A spinner has two hands. The jenny began with twelve to eighteen spindles.

@art machines-make-machines
The steam engine came afterwards, because the machines needed it. And in the end, modern industry had to make its own machines by machine, since manufacture's hand workers couldn't make them large or exact enough.

@chapter No new value
@quote text="Machinery, like every other component of constant capital, creates no new value, but yields up its own value to the product that it serves to beget." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 15, §2"
Now the key passage. Machinery, like every other component of constant capital, creates no new value, but yields up its own value to the product that it serves to beget.

@art wear-into-yards
It enters the labour process whole, but the product's value only bit by bit. And the gap between what it's worth and what it adds each day is far larger than for a tool, because it lasts longer and makes so much more. Beyond its wear, it works for nothing, like the forces of nature.

@math heading="Value per yard" items="£3,000 / 10,000 yards = £3/10 a yard@three tenths"
So the first figure is simple: the machine's value divided by everything it will make in its life. In the lab, a three thousand pound machine making ten thousand yards adds three tenths of a pound to each yard, and nothing besides.

@chapter Two tests
@quote text="this £3000 is by no means the expression in money of the labour added to the object produced by these 150 men before the introduction of the machine, but only of that portion of their year's labour which was expended for themselves and represented by their wages." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 15, §2"
Now suppose the machine costs as much as a year's wages of the hundred and fifty men it displaces. Does it save as much labour as it costs? Marx says this three thousand pounds is by no means the labour those men added, but only that portion of their year's labour which was expended for themselves and represented by their wages.

@math heading="Marx's £3,000 machine" items="labour saved: £3,000 · (1 + 1) = £6,000@six thousand;;society: £3,000 < £6,000 · adopted@society's test;;capitalist: £3,000 < £3,000 is false · no saving@the capitalist"
At a rate of surplus value of a hundred percent, the men added six thousand pounds of value a year. By society's test, the machine costs three thousand and saves six, so it's adopted. But the capitalist pays only for labour-power. Three thousand against three thousand of wages saves him nothing.

@art two-tests
So the capitalist's limit is narrower than society's. He adopts a machine only if it costs less than the wages it replaces. Between the two tests lies the unpaid labour of the displaced workers.

@chapter Cheap labour delays machines
@math heading="Same machine, other wages" items="£5,000 machine, £3,000 wages: society yes · capitalist no@five thousand;;wages £2,000: £3,000 machine fails@two thousand;;wages £4,500: £3,000 machine passes@forty five hundred"
A five thousand pound machine saving the same six thousand of labour passes society's test and fails the capitalist's. Lower the wages to two thousand, and even the three thousand pound machine fails. Raise them to forty five hundred, and it passes.

@art stone-breaker
That's why machines invented in England were used only in North America. A Yankee stone breaking machine went unused in England, where the stone breaker was paid for so little of his labour.

@art canal-horses
And women still sometimes hauled canal boats instead of horses. Profit comes not from a diminution of the labour employed, but of the labour paid for.

@words text="Common ground, and the dispute"
That cheap labour delays mechanisation is textbook common ground. What's Marx's is the second measure, and the claim that the gap is unpaid labour. An economist who prices labour by its marginal product would say the wage is the value of what the worker adds, and there's no gap to find.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Machines are used whenever they save labour@The first|A machine as a source of new value@The second|Wages displaced equal labour saved@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a machine is used whenever it saves labour. That's society's test, not the capitalist's. The second is treating a machine as a source of new value. And the third is equating the wages a machine displaces with the labour it saves.
