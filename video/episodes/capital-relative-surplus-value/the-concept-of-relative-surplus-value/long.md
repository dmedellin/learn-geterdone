@chapter Two ways to get more
@title text="The Concept of Relative Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 12" art=thumb
Marx draws the day as a line from a through b to c. From a to b the worker replaces the value of his labour-power. From b to c he works for the capitalist. In the last course, c moved, as the day was pushed out.

@art fixed-day
Now c is fixed, by law or custom, at twelve hours. Can b be pushed back towards a? Marx's figures: ten hours necessary, two surplus. Move b back by an hour, and surplus labour grows by half, from two hours to three, though the day hasn't changed by a minute.

@quote text="the surplus-value arising from the curtailment of the necessary labour-time, and from the corresponding alteration in the respective lengths of the two components of the working day, I call relative surplus-value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 12"
Surplus value from lengthening the day he calls absolute. And the surplus value arising from the curtailment of the necessary labour time, and from the corresponding alteration in the respective lengths of the two components of the working day, I call relative surplus value.

@chapter Not a wage cut
@words text="5s → 4s 6d?" sub="labour-power below its value"
The obvious way to move b is to pay less. Pay four and six instead of five shillings, and nine hours replace the wage. But that's buying labour-power below its value. Marx grants it happens in practice, and sets it aside. On his assumption, necessary time falls only if the value of labour-power itself falls.

@chapter Cheapening the worker
@art cheaper-necessaries
That value is the value of the worker's necessaries. So bread, shirts and boots must take less labour to make, and so must the leather, thread and tools behind them.

@art diamonds-vs-bread
Cheaper diamonds leave the value of labour-power alone. And each cheapened article lowers it only in proportion to its place in what the worker buys.

@math heading="A 12-hour day" items="given: 2 / 10 = 20%@twenty percent;;cheaper necessaries: 3 / 9 = ⅓@a third"
In the lab, ten hours necessary in a twelve hour day is two over ten, twenty percent. Cheapen the necessaries so nine hours replace the wage, and it's three over nine, a third.

@chapter The pioneer
@words text="He “by no means necessarily aims” at it"
Nobody who brings in a better loom is thinking about the national value of labour-power. Marx agrees. The general tendencies of capital must be distinguished from how they show up. So he tells the story from inside the firm.

@art pioneer
An hour of labour is embodied in sixpence, so a twelve hour day adds six shillings. Twelve articles are made, each with sixpence of materials, so each is worth a shilling. One capitalist doubles productivity and makes twenty four. Each costs him ninepence. But the social value is still a shilling.

@math heading="Twenty-four articles at 10d" items="sales: 24 · 10d = 20s@twenty shillings;;materials: 24 · 6d = 12s@twelve shillings;;new value: 20s − 12s = 8s@eight shillings;;surplus: 8s − 5s = 3s, was 1s@three shillings;;wage replaced in: 12 h · 5/8 = 7½ h@seven and a half"
To sell twice as many, he shades the price to tenpence. Sales are twenty shillings. Materials, twelve shillings. So eight shillings of new value. Take away the five shilling wage, and his surplus is three shillings, where it was one. His worker replaces the wage in seven and a half hours instead of ten.

@chapter Competition closes the gap
@art competitors-catch-up
That gain is the motive, and it's temporary. The same law of value, acting as a coercive law of competition, forces his rivals to adopt the method. Then the social value falls to ninepence, and his extra is gone.

@quote text="Hence there is immanent in capital an inclination and constant tendency, to heighten the productiveness of labour, in order to cheapen commodities, and by such cheapening to cheapen the labourer himself." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 12"
What remains, if the article is one workers live on, is a lower value of labour-power for every capitalist at once. Hence there is immanent in capital an inclination and constant tendency, to heighten the productiveness of labour, in order to cheapen commodities, and by such cheapening to cheapen the labourer himself.

@math heading="The method spreads" items="labour-power: 5s → 3s@five shillings to three;;necessary: 6 h · rate 6 / 6 = 100%@a hundred percent;;pioneer's extra: 0@zero"
In the lab's general case, labour-power falls from five shillings to three, Marx's own later figure. Necessary time is six hours, the rate is a hundred percent, and the pioneer's extra is zero.

@chapter Quesnay's riddle
@art innovator-lead
Why does the capitalist, who cares only for exchange value, keep lowering it? Because only the surplus value in a commodity interests him. Schumpeter, in nineteen eleven, also traced profit to the innovator's lead and its erosion by imitators, without surplus labour behind it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Innovating to lower labour-power's value@The first|A wage cut as relative surplus value@The second|Expecting the pioneer's lead to last@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the capitalist innovates in order to lower the value of labour-power. His motive is the extra. The second is counting a wage cut as relative surplus value. And the third is expecting the pioneer's advantage to last.
