@chapter Same day, two numbers
@title text="Formulae for the Rate of Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 18" art=thumb
Suppose you're told a company's workers receive a quarter of what they produce, and its owners three quarters. That sounds like seventy five percent. Expressed Marx's way, it's three hundred percent. Both describe the same day.

@chapter Marx's formulae
@list heading="One ratio, three ways" items="s / v@surplus value over variable capital|s / value of labour-power@value of labour-power|surplus labour / necessary labour@necessary labour"
Marx first lists his own formulae. Surplus value over variable capital. Surplus value over the value of labour-power. Surplus labour over necessary labour. They're the same ratio stated three ways, and the classical economists used them substantially, but not consciously.

@list heading="The derivative formulae" items="surplus labour / working day@over the working day|surplus value / value of product@value of the product|surplus product / total product@total product"
What the economists wrote down instead were the derivative formulae. Surplus labour over the working day. Surplus value over the value of the product, meaning only the new value created. Surplus product over total product.

@chapter The ceiling
@art part-of-whole
A derivative formula divides a part of the day by the whole. The surplus labour is always a part, so the ratio is always below one. To reach a hundred percent, surplus labour would have to be the whole day. Then necessary labour would be zero, and with it the worker, and with him the surplus.

@quote text="If they are to be treated as direct expressions of the degree of self-expansion of capital, the following erroneous law would hold good: Surplus-labour or surplus-value can never reach 100%." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 18"
These formulae, Marx says, only show how the day is divided. If they are to be treated as direct expressions of the degree of self-expansion of capital, the following erroneous law would hold good: surplus labour or surplus value can never reach a hundred percent.

@art no-ceiling
The rate of surplus value has no such ceiling. It divides surplus by necessary, and as necessary labour shrinks, the rate grows without bound.

@chapter Lavergne's quarter
@art lavergne-field
Marx's example is the French economist Lavergne, who estimated that the English farm labourer gets a quarter of the product and the farmer three quarters. By the derivative formula, that's seventy five percent. By the rate of surplus value, it's three to one, a rate of exploitation of three hundred percent.

@math heading="Converting" items="rate = d / (1 − d)@d over one minus d;;¾ / ¼ = 3 = 300%@three quarters over one quarter;;share = r / (1 + r)@r over one plus r"
The two numbers are tied together. If d is the share taken as surplus, the rate is d over one minus d. Three quarters over one quarter is three. And back the other way: from a rate r, the share is r over one plus r.

@chapter Four days
@math heading="A 12-hour day" items="6 h necessary: 100% · share ½@six hours;;4 h necessary: 200% · share ⅔@four hours;;3 h necessary: 300% · share ¾@three hours;;1 h necessary: 1,100% · share 11/12@a single"
The lab computes both for a twelve hour day. Six hours necessary: a hundred percent, and a share of a half. Four hours: two hundred percent, two thirds. Three hours, Lavergne's quarter: three hundred, three quarters. And with a single necessary hour, the rate is eleven hundred percent, while the share is eleven twelfths, still short of a hundred.

@art four-days
Look at the middle case. Two thirds sounds moderate, and two hundred percent doesn't. They're the same day. The share creeps toward a hundred percent and never reaches it. The rate climbs without limit.

@chapter An association?
@quote text="Instead of the real fact, we have the false semblance of an association, in which labourer and capitalist divide the product in proportion to the different elements which they respectively contribute towards its formation." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 18"
Why does the choice matter? Because fractions of the value created hide the transaction. Instead of the real fact, we have the false semblance of an association, in which labourer and capitalist divide the product in proportion to the different elements which they respectively contribute towards its formation.

@art partnership
A share looks like a partnership. Economists who prefer the share would answer that it is one: labour and capital each contribute, and each is paid. That's the dispute the whole book is about. The formula doesn't settle it. It only decides whose language the number is written in.

@art command
Marx's last formula is unpaid labour over paid labour, with a warning: the capitalist pays for labour-power, and its use in the surplus hours comes to him free. Capital, he says, is not only the command over labour, as Adam Smith said. It is essentially the command over unpaid labour.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The share as the measure of exploitation@The first|Two formulae, two different days@The second|Unpaid over paid as payment for labour@And the third"
The first slip is treating the share as the measure of exploitation. The second is thinking the two formulae describe different days, when they convert exactly. And the third is reading unpaid over paid labour as payment for some hours and not others.
