@chapter The verb loses its endings
@title text="After a Modal, the Verb Is Bare" sub="Helping Verbs" art=thumb
Take a verb and put can in front of it. The verb loses its endings. She walks, but she can walk. He went, but he could go.

@art helper-takes
The helping verb has taken the work, and the main verb is left bare. A bare verb is the base form, with no ending and no to: go, be, have, see.

@chapter The nine modals
@list heading="The nine modals" items="can · could@can, could|may · might@may, might|must@must|shall · should@shall, should|will · would@will and would"
Nine words do this, and they're called modals: can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would. They add a meaning to the verb after them: whether something can, may or must happen.

@art no-forms
A modal has no forms of its own. It never takes s, e-d or ing. Do is different: it's a helping verb with its own forms, does and did. So do isn't one of the nine.

@chapter One tense is enough
@math heading="The tense is shown once" items="she could go  ✓@she could go;;she can goes  ✗@she can goes;;he must went  ✗@he must went"
A clause shows its tense once. In she could go, the tense is in could. Add an ending to go, and the tense is shown twice. That's the mistake in she can goes, and in he must went.

@art two-tenses
In she can goes, the speaker has put the he, she, it ending on a verb that follows a word which has already taken the tense. One word carries it. The other carries nothing.

@words text="She must to go ✗" sub="no to after a modal"
And there's no to after a modal either. Not she must to go, but she must go. Ought to and have to look similar, but they're a separate group.

@chapter Small words between
@math heading="Not and short adverbs" items="you could not do better@you could not do better;;we shall often meet@we shall often meet"
Not and short adverbs can come between a modal and its verb. You could not do better. We shall often meet. The bare form holds right through the gap.

@art small-between
So when you check a sentence, look past the small words in between. The modal is on one side, the bare verb is on the other, and the gap doesn't change either of them.

@list heading="Four questions" items="Find the modal@Find the modal|Skip not and short adverbs@skip not|Is the next word a bare verb?@Is the next word|If it's a pronoun, check the order@a pronoun"
So here's the method. Find the modal. Skip not and any short adverb. Is the next word a bare verb? Then the rule holds. And if the next word is a pronoun, check the order, because it may be a question.

@chapter Counting it
@math heading="Pride and Prejudice, Chapter XXVI" items="76 modals@seventy-six;;65 followed by a bare verb  ·  85.5%@sixty-five;;0 followed by a verb with an ending@zero"
The rule was checked on one chapter of Pride and Prejudice. There are seventy-six modals. A bare verb follows sixty-five times, eighty-five point five percent. And the row that would break the rule, a modal followed by a verb with an ending? There are zero.

@chapter The eleven others
@math heading="Questions and stops" items="will you come  ·  how can I promise@will you come;;he should not. I see@he should not;;will, I am sure, be@I am sure"
So what about the other eleven? Two are questions: will you come, and how can I promise. The bare verb comes after the subject, so the rule holds. In he should not, full stop, I see, the modal ends its sentence. And in will, I am sure, be, a remark stands in between.

@art gaps
The other seven are gaps. Two bare verbs the word list doesn't know. One behind but, in could not but be. One verb left out. And three with no longer or at present in the way. None of the eleven has an ending on the verb.

@chapter The whole novel
@math heading="Whole novel, quoted" items="2,738 modals@two thousand seven hundred and thirty-eight;;bare verb directly after: 58.5%@fifty-eight point five;;a verb with an ending: 3, which is 0.1%@just three"
Over the whole novel, counted once, there are two thousand seven hundred and thirty-eight modals. A bare verb comes directly after fifty-eight point five percent. A verb with an ending follows just three, and in each one the modal ends its clause, like as soon as we can, said Jane.

@art clause-boundary
The verb after the comma belongs to the next clause. So the rule has no true exception in the whole book. On two modern documents, too, no verb with an ending follows a modal.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="she can goes ✗  she can go ✓@The first|she must to go ✗  she must go ✓@The second|Reading 85.5% as a failure rate@the third"
The first slip is putting the tense on both words, she can goes. The second is adding to after a modal, she must to go. And the third is reading eighty-five point five percent as a failure rate. It's a limit of the scan, not of the rule.

@chapter Try it
@words text="He could not do better." sub="which word takes the tense?"
Your turn. In he could not do better, which word takes the tense? Could does. Not sits between, and do has no ending. Now try it with will, must and might.
