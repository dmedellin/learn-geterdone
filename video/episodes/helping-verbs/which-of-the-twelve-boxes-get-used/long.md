@chapter Twelve equal boxes?
@title text="Which of the Twelve Boxes Get Used" sub="Helping Verbs" art=thumb
You've been given a table of twelve boxes. Present, past and future, each one simple, progressive, perfect, and perfect progressive. It's a good table.

@art equal-grid
But it's usually taught as if every box mattered the same. Count the boxes on a real page, and they don't.

@chapter How to read a phrase
@list heading="Sort a verb phrase" items="List the helping verbs@List the helping verbs|Find the main word@Find the main word|Match the chain to a box@Match the chain|Name it by its parts@by its parts"
Here's how to sort a verb phrase. List the helping verbs in a row, skipping not. Find the main word after the last helping verb. Match the chain to a box. And name the box by its parts, not by a name you learned by heart.

@math heading="Chains and boxes" items="have + participle  →  perfect@Have and a participle;;be + -ing  →  progressive@Be and an ing word;;modal alone  →  the modal box@A modal alone;;be + participle  →  passive@Be and a participle is"
Have and a participle is a perfect. Be and an ing word is a progressive. A modal alone is the modal box. And be and a participle is the passive.

@words text="she has been acting" sub="present · perfect · progressive"
Take she has been acting. Has is present. Been after it is perfect. Acting after been is progressive. Three parts in one phrase.

@art chain-blocks
So the names are just a way to say the parts. You don't need to learn them as things on their own. Build the chain, and the name follows.

@chapter Six phrases, six boxes
@math heading="From the chapter" items="she thought  ·  past simple@she thought;;I hope  ·  present simple@I hope;;I am not afraid  ·  be as the main verb@I am not afraid;;I will try  ·  modal, simple@I will try;;you are warned  ·  passive, or an adjective@you are warned;;she has been acting  ·  present perfect progressive@she has been acting"
Here are six phrases from one chapter of Pride and Prejudice. She thought: past simple. I hope: present simple. I am not afraid: be as the main verb. I will try: the modal box. You are warned: passive, or an adjective. And she has been acting: present perfect progressive.

@chapter Where the 216 land
@art sort-arrows
Now count. Take every pronoun in one chapter, and sort the verb phrase after each one into a box.

@math heading="216 pronoun subjects, Chapter XXVI" items="simple, present and past  ·  55@fifty-five;;with a modal  ·  57@fifty-seven;;perfect  ·  4  ·  passive  ·  4@four;;progressive  ·  3@just three;;no verb found  ·  42@forty-two"
The chapter has two hundred and sixteen pronoun subjects. The simple boxes, present and past, hold fifty-five. Phrases with a modal in front hold fifty-seven. The perfect holds four, and be with a participle holds four. The progressive holds just three. And forty-two are filed as no verb found.

@art few-boxes-full
So the simple boxes and the modal box hold one hundred and twelve of the two hundred and sixteen, just over half. And be as the main verb is the largest named group of all. The progressive holds one point four percent.

@words text="the future lives in the modal box" sub="I will try"
Notice where the future went. In the table, it's a row of its own. On the page, it lives in the modal box, as will and a bare verb. I will try.

@chapter The rare boxes
@math heading="The three progressives" items="she has been acting@she has been acting;;I will not be wishing@I will not be wishing;;he was now rendering himself agreeable@he was now rendering"
The three progressives are she has been acting, I will not be wishing, and he was now rendering himself agreeable. That's three of two hundred and sixteen, and reading the rows by hand finds no more.

@art no-verb-tray
The forty-two with no verb found are the count being honest. Some are objects, like it in to use it. Five are I cannot. Some are he ought to. They're printed so you can sort them yourself.

@chapter The whole novel, and today
@math heading="Quoted and counted" items="whole novel: 8,864 pronoun subjects@eight thousand eight hundred;;all progressives: 143  ·  1.6%@one hundred and forty-three;;two modern documents: 34 subjects, 0 progressive@thirty-four"
Over the whole novel, counted once, there are eight thousand eight hundred and sixty-four pronoun subjects. All the progressive phrases together are one hundred and forty-three, one point six percent. And two modern documents give thirty-four pronoun subjects, with zero progressives.

@art then-and-now
On those modern pages, the simple boxes hold fourteen of thirty-four, and the perfect holds three. The boxes a page uses are about the same in eighteen thirteen and today.

@chapter What it does not say
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating the progressive as the ordinary present@The first|Reading the table as twelve equal tools@The second|Treating no verb found as a failure@the third"
The first slip is treating the progressive as the ordinary present. The plain present and past are far more common. The second is reading the table as twelve equal tools. And the third is treating no verb found as a failure. It's a group for what a scan can't sort.

@words text="rare on the page, still needed" sub="the count never says which box to choose"
But a count never tells you which box to choose. A writer picks a box because of what they mean. The count tells you which boxes to practise first, and which ones to know well enough to read.
