@chapter A habit from nouns
@title text="The -s Belongs to He, She and It" sub="Helping Verbs" art=thumb
Here's a habit from nouns that causes trouble on verbs. On a noun, an s means more than one: book, books. So learners see an s on a verb, read it as more than one, and write they walks.

@art noun-verb-mirror
But on a verb, the s means the opposite. In she walks, one person walks. The boy walks, and the boys walk. The ending is in opposite places.

@chapter The rule
@words text="he, she, it → walks" sub="everyone else → walk"
Here's the rule for the present tense, short enough to say in one breath. He, she, it, and any one thing named in their place, take the form with s. Everyone else takes the bare form, the verb with nothing added.

@art one-and-many
That includes names and nouns that stand for one thing. The letter says. Jane has. And you takes the bare form, even when it means one person.

@chapter Be, have and do
@math heading="They change the word, not the ending" items="he, she, it:  is  ·  has  ·  does  ·  was@he, she and it;;you, we, they:  are  ·  have  ·  do  ·  were@you, we and they;;I:  am  ·  was  ·  or the bare form@And I takes"
Be, have and do follow the same rule, but with different words. For he, she and it, it's is, has, does, and was in the past. For you, we and they, it's are, have, do, and were. And I takes am, was, or the bare form.

@art three-swaps
So these three don't add an s. They change the word. And in the past, be splits again: was for I, he, she and it, and were for everyone else.

@math heading="Same verbs, new subject" items="she was  ·  he has  ·  it does@She was;;they were  ·  you have  ·  we do@They were;;I am  ·  I was  ·  I know@I am"
Listen to the pattern. She was, he has, it does. They were, you have, we do. I am, I was, I know.

@chapter Four questions
@list heading="Ask in order" items="Is it I?  →  am, was, bare form@Is the subject I|He, she, it, or one thing?  →  is, has, does, was, -s@Is it he, she, it|You, we, they, or more than one?  →  are, have, do, were@Is it you, we, they|A modal or a past form?  →  nothing changes@a modal or a past"
Is the subject I? Then use am, was, or the bare form. Is it he, she, it, or one thing? Use is, has, does, was, or the verb with s. Is it you, we, they, or more than one? Use are, have, do, were, or the bare verb. Is the verb a modal or a past form? Then stop.

@words text="she could · they could · he walked · we walked"
A past form and a modal look the same whatever the subject. She could, they could. He walked, we walked. The rule has nothing to say about them.

@chapter Counting it
@math heading="Pride and Prejudice, Chapter XXVI" items="216 pronoun subjects@two hundred and sixteen;;75 pairs the rule can score@seventy-five;;74 hold  ·  98.7%@seventy-four"
The rule was run on one chapter of Pride and Prejudice. It has two hundred and sixteen pronoun subjects. In seventy-five of them, the next word is a present form, was, were or a bare verb, so the pair can be scored. The rule holds in seventy-four. That's ninety-eight point seven percent.

@art rows-set-aside
The other one hundred and forty-one are followed by a word the rule can't use: she thought, they went, I would. So this rule only covers the present tense, and was against were.

@chapter The row that breaks it
@quote text="if I were not afraid of judging" cite="Pride and Prejudice, Chapter XXVI"
One pair goes against the rule, and it's not a mistake. Elizabeth writes, if I were not afraid of judging. The if marks a case that isn't real, and English keeps were for that, for every subject, even I.

@art old-form
It's old, and it's rare, so the count names it and sets it aside. Over the whole novel, twenty-two of the exceptions are this same form: if it were, she were, he were.

@math heading="Whole novel, quoted" items="after he, she, it: 1,214 third person forms, 37 others@twelve hundred and fourteen;;after you, we, they: 487 forms for more than one, 5 others@four hundred and eighty-seven;;two modern documents: 14 of 14@fourteen"
Seventy-five pairs is a small count, so here's the whole novel, counted once. After he, she and it, there are twelve hundred and fourteen third person forms, and thirty-seven others. After you, we and they, four hundred and eighty-seven, and five others. And on two modern documents, the rule holds in fourteen pairs of fourteen.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="they walks ✗  they walk ✓@The first|he have ✗  he has ✓@The second|Calling the rule weak because of if I were@the third"
The first slip is putting the s on a verb for more than one, like they walks. The second is using have, do and were with he, she and it, like he have. And the third is calling the rule weak because of if I were. One exception with a reason is a good rule.
