@chapter A rule you never noticed
@title text="Where Not Goes, and When Do Arrives" sub="Helping Verbs" art=thumb
Here's a rule so small you may never have thought of it as a rule. Not follows the first helping verb.

@math heading="Not after the first helping verb" items="She is not.@She is not;;She has not seen.@She has not seen;;She could not have seen.@She could not have seen"
She is not. She has not seen. She could not have seen. In that last one there are two helping verbs, could and have, and not goes after the first: could.

@art first-slot
The rest of the verb phrase stays where it was. Find the first helping verb, and put not right after it.

@chapter No helping verb?
@words text="She liked it." sub="nowhere to put not"
Now take a sentence with no helping verb. She liked it. There's nowhere to put not. Modern English doesn't put it after liked any more.

@math heading="Do arrives" items="She liked it.@She liked it;;She did not like it.@did not like it"
So English adds a helping verb. She liked it becomes she did not like it. Did takes the past tense, and like goes back to its bare form.

@art do-arrives
It's the same pattern as the modal. One word shows the tense, and the verb after it takes nothing. Here, do has no meaning of its own. It only has the job of being there.

@chapter Step by step
@list heading="Four questions" items="Is there a helping verb? → not after the first@Is there a helping verb|Is the main verb be? → no do@Is the main verb be|Otherwise add do, does or did@Otherwise, add do|The verb after not takes no ending@Check the verb after not"
Is there a helping verb? Put not after the first one. Is the main verb be? Then it works as its own helping verb. Otherwise, add do: do, does for he, she and it, or did for the past. Then not, then the bare verb. Check the verb after not. It takes no ending.

@words text="My visit was not long." sub="be needs no do"
Be is the exception. As a main verb, it needs no do. My visit was not long. You are not serious. I am not afraid. Never I do not be ready.

@art be-own-helper
Be is its own first helping verb, so not just goes straight after it. That's one thing to remember, and every other verb gets do.

@chapter Counting it
@math heading="Pride and Prejudice, Chapter XXVI" items="39 uses of not@thirty-nine;;29 after a helping verb  ·  74.4%@twenty-nine"
One chapter of Pride and Prejudice has thirty-nine uses of not. In twenty-nine, seventy-four point four percent, the word before it is a helping verb: I am not afraid, you could not do better, she did not regret, do not think me obstinate.

@art ten-sorted
Ten are not after a helping verb. Five are filed after a main verb, three after a joining word, and two as something else. None of them is a mistake.

@chapter The old order
@math heading="After a main verb" items="she said not a word  ·  old order@said not a word;;you need not be  ·  I need not explain@need not;;determined not to@determined not to;;and not a note, not a line@not a note"
Only one of the five is the old order. She said not a word. Today it's she did not say a word. Need not still works as a modal, in you need not be and I need not explain. Determined not to, and not a note, not a line, put not before a word that isn't the verb.

@words text="said not a word → did not say a word"
So the old order shows up once in thirty-nine. Over the whole novel, counted once, not sits after a helping verb seventy-eight point eight percent of the time, and after a main verb six point three percent.

@art old-new-order
In the play counted for this course, the share after a helping verb is eighty-six percent, and more than half of all its nots are the short form, n apostrophe t, on a helping verb. In two modern documents, it's eight of ten. Today, do is required.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="I know not ✗  I do not know ✓@The first|I do not be ready ✗  I am not ready ✓@The second|she did not regretted ✗  she did not regret ✓@the third"
The first slip is using the old order. I know not was English in eighteen thirteen. Today it's I do not know. The second is adding do to be: I do not be ready. And the third is keeping the tense on the main verb as well: she did not regretted.

@art bare-after
Did and does have already taken the tense, so the main verb is bare. Caroline did not return. He does not know.

@chapter Try it
@words text="She liked the house." sub="make it negative"
Your turn. Make this negative: she liked the house. There's no helping verb, so did arrives and takes the tense. And like goes back to its bare form. She did not like the house.
