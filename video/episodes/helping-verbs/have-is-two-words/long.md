@chapter Same word, two jobs
@title text="Have Is Two Words" sub="Helping Verbs" art=thumb
She has seen the letter. She has a letter. The same word, the same spelling, and two different jobs.

@art two-jobs
In the first, has is a helping verb. It stands in front of seen and carries the tense. In the second, has is the verb itself, and it means that she holds the letter.

@chapter The test
@math heading="Look at the next word" items="have + participle  →  helping verb  ·  seen, been, written@a participle follows;;have + noun phrase  →  main verb  ·  a letter, no pleasure, much to say@a noun phrase follows"
The word after have decides which one it is. If a participle follows, like seen, been or written, have is a helping verb. If a noun phrase follows, like a letter, no pleasure, or much to say, have is the main verb.

@words text="have to + verb" sub="I have to go"
And there's a third thing. Have to, followed by a verb, says that something is needed. I have to go. It's not quite the helping verb, and not quite the main verb, so it's counted apart.

@art next-word
You don't need to know the meaning of the sentence first. You only need to look one word to the right, and sometimes two.

@list heading="Look right, one word, then two" items="Skip not and short adverbs@skip not|A participle? → helping verb@a participle|a, the, no, my, some, or a noun? → main verb@a, the, no|to? → have to@Is the next word to"
So here's the method. First, skip not and short adverbs: had not, has already. Second, is the next word a participle? Then it's a helping verb. Third, is it a, the, no, my, some, or a noun? Then it's the main verb. Fourth, is the next word to? Then it's have to.

@chapter Row by row
@math heading="From the chapter" items="had been quitted  ·  has been so@had been quitted;;had already written  ·  she had seen@had already written;;you have sense  ·  he had the fortune@you have sense;;she had no pleasure  ·  I have nothing to@she had no pleasure"
Here are rows from one chapter of Pride and Prejudice. Had been quitted, has been so. Had already written, she had seen. In these, a participle follows, so have is helping. Now: you have sense, he had the fortune, she had no pleasure, I have nothing to. A noun phrase follows, so have is the verb itself.

@art fork-of-roles
Every row goes one way or the other. The helping verb does a job for another verb. The main verb is about having something.

But read the rows by hand. She might have foreseen. The event has proved you right. Each has a participle, but the scan's word list didn't know it. In had by some accident been lost, a phrase stands in between.

In had fortune permitted it, the subject has moved in behind had. That's an old way of saying if. Add these seven, and by hand, the split is twenty-four helping verbs, sixty-four point nine percent.

@chapter A larger count
@math heading="Whole novel, quoted" items="2,339 forms of have@two thousand three hundred and thirty-nine;;59.5% helping  ·  32.1% main  ·  1.1% have to@fifty-nine point five"
Thirty-seven is a small count. Over the whole novel, counted once by the same scan, have appears two thousand three hundred and thirty-nine times. Fifty-nine point five percent are helping verbs, thirty-two point one percent main verbs, and one point one percent have to.

@art three-texts
On two modern documents, the scan finds sixteen helping verbs in twenty-three forms, sixty-nine point six percent. The share moves from text to text, but in every one, the helping verb is common.

@chapter Phrases that are one unit
@words text="We had better not mention it." sub="had better · advice"
Some phrases work as one unit. We had better not mention it. Had better gives advice, and the scan can't place it.

@words text="I would have you be on your guard." sub="have + person + bare verb · make or let"
And I would have you be on your guard is a third use. Have with a person and a bare verb after it means make or let. The label says what the scan read. It doesn't say what the writer meant.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking have always means to own@The first|Reading the scan's count as the answer@The second|Taking a fixed phrase for its parts@the third"
The first slip is thinking have always means to own. Most forms of have in the novel are helping verbs. The second is reading the scan's count as the answer. Read the rows. And the third is taking a fixed phrase, like had better, for its parts.
