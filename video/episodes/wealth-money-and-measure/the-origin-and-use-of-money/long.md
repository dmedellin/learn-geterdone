@chapter The question
@title text="The Origin and Use of Money" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 4" art=thumb
Once people divide their work, each one lives by exchanging. Smith says every man becomes, in some measure, a merchant. But at first, that exchanging must often have been clogged and awkward. Chapter four asks why, and what fixed it.

@art butcher-ring
The butcher has more meat than he can eat, and the brewer and the baker would each buy some. But they've only got beer and bread to offer, and the butcher already has all the bread and beer he needs. He can't be their merchant, nor they his customers.

@chapter Counting the cost
@math heading="n goods" items="no measure:  n · (n − 1) / 2 rates@each pair;;one good as measure:  n − 1 prices@one good serves;;3 goods:  3 rates, or 2@three goods"
The lesson counts what that costs. With no common measure, each pair of goods needs its own rate, and the number of pairs is n times n minus one, over two. If one good serves as the measure, every other good needs just one price in it, n minus one. For three goods, that's three rates, or two.

@art pairs-web
In a town's market with twelve goods, that's sixty six rates without a measure, and only eleven with one. For a hundred goods, four thousand nine hundred and fifty against ninety nine. The gap widens as the market grows.

@words text="Butcher → bread · Baker → beer · Brewer → meat" sub="no pair can trade directly"
Now who can trade. In the lesson's ring, the butcher wants bread, the baker wants beer, and the brewer wants meat. Take any two, and one has what the other wants, but not the other way round. No pair can trade directly. Yet all three can trade through a money.

@chapter A commodity few refuse
@art early-moneys
Smith's answer is the prudent man who keeps some one commodity that few people would refuse. Cattle, in the rude ages: Homer says Diomede's armour cost nine oxen, and Glaucus's a hundred. Salt in Abyssinia, shells in India, dried cod at Newfoundland, tobacco in Virginia, and nails in a Scottish village.

@chapter Why metals
@art divide-metal
In every country, the choice ended with metals. Scarce anything is less perishable, and they can be divided into any number of parts without loss. A man with only cattle who wants salt must buy a whole ox's worth at a time. With metal, he can match the amount to exactly what he needs.

@art stamped-coins
At first metal passed in rough bars, which had to be weighed and assayed. Nobody wants to weigh a farthing's worth of goods. So a public stamp was fixed on certain quantities. Notice the order: money comes first, chosen by custom, and the stamp is a guarantee added to it.

@words text="£1 = 240d" sub="once a pound weight of silver"
The names were weights at first. In Edward the First's time, the English pound held a pound, Tower weight, of silver, and a penny was a real penny weight, a two hundred and fortieth of a pound. That's why a pound was two hundred and forty pence.

@quote text="the avarice and injustice of princes and sovereign states, abusing the confidence of their subjects, have by degrees diminished the real quantity of metal, which had been originally contained in their coins" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 4"
But the avarice and injustice of princes and sovereign states, abusing the confidence of their subjects, have by degrees diminished the real quantity of metal, which had been originally contained in their coins. The English pound, he says, now holds about a third of its first value.

@chapter Two kinds of value
@art water-and-diamond
Then Smith defines a word. Value has two meanings: value in use, and value in exchange. Nothing is more useful than water, but it will buy scarce anything. A diamond has scarce any use, but will often buy a great many goods. Smith sets that puzzle aside. The economists of the eighteen seventies answered it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking money was invented by law@The first|Reading n − 1 as every price there is@The second|Reading water as worth little@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Smith says money was invented by law. The commodity comes first; the stamp only certifies it. The second is reading n minus one as every price in an economy. And the third is reading water as worth little. It has the greatest value in use. And the barter story itself is a conjecture that later anthropologists have doubted.
