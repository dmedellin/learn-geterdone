@chapter The question
@title text="Labour, the Real Measure" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 5" art=thumb
What is a thing really worth? Its price in money, you might say. Smith says no. Money is only the nominal price. The real price is measured in labour.

@art command-labour
Here's his argument. Once work is divided, only a small part of what you want can come from your own labour. So you're rich or poor according to the quantity of other people's labour you can command, or afford to buy.

@quote text="Labour therefore, is the real measure of the exchangeable value of all commodities." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 5"
And so: labour, therefore, is the real measure of the exchangeable value of all commodities.

@chapter Toil and trouble
@art toil-scale
He says it another way too. The real price of everything is the toil and trouble of acquiring it. And what a thing is worth to its owner is the toil it can save him, and impose on other people. Notice he means two things by labour here, without separating them.

@words text="An hour's hard work ≠ two hours' easy business"
Smith admits labour is hard to compare. Hardship and ingenuity count, not just time. There may be more labour in an hour's hard work than in two hours of easy business. The market settles it roughly, by higgling and bargaining. And in practice, people swap goods for money, so the money price is the one we use.

@chapter Why not money?
@art shrinking-silver
So why not measure in money? Because gold and silver vary in value, like any other commodity. The mines of America cut their value in sixteenth century Europe to about a third of what it had been. Something that keeps changing its own value can never measure others accurately.

@art two-rulers
Labour, Smith says, doesn't vary. In his ordinary health and skill, a labourer always lays down the same portion of his ease, his liberty and his happiness. So labour is the real price, and money is the nominal price only.

@chapter Real price in days
@math heading="Real price = money price / price of a day" items="ordinary year:  25s / 1s = 25 days@twenty five shillings;;dear year:  50s / 1s = 50 days@fifty;;money +100% · real +100%@both rise"
The lab divides the money price by the price of a day's labour. Corn at twenty five shillings a quarter in an ordinary year, and fifty in a dear one, are Smith's figures. Hold the wage at one shilling, the lesson's figure, and the real price goes from twenty five days to fifty. Both rise by a hundred per cent.

@math heading="Both double" items="before:  10 / 1 = 10 days@ten shillings;;after:  20 / 2 = 10 days@twenty;;money +100% · real 0%@didn't move"
Now cheapen silver so everything doubles in money. The good goes from ten shillings to twenty, and the wage from one shilling to two. The real price stays at ten days. The money price rose a hundred per cent, and the real price didn't move. That change was in the money.

@words text="10s → 8s, wage 1s → 6d" sub="10 days → 16 days"
And it can go the other way. The good falls from ten shillings to eight, while the wage falls from a shilling to sixpence. The real price goes from ten days to sixteen. Cheaper in money, dearer in labour.

@chapter Same time and place
@art canton-london
Smith limits his own thesis. At the same time and place, money is the exact measure. Half an ounce of silver at Canton may command more labour than an ounce in London. But a merchant who buys there for half an ounce and sells in London for an ounce gains a hundred per cent. That's all he needs to know.

@chapter Ricardo's objection
@words text="Labour commanded ≠ labour spent" sub="Ricardo, 1817"
In 1817 David Ricardo said Smith had run two things together: the labour a good costs to make, and the labour it commands. When wages rise, a good that takes the same labour to make commands less. So labour commanded can't be a fixed standard. And in the lab, a rise in the wage lowers the real price.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the real price follows the money price@The first|Saying money prices don't matter@The second|Mixing the real price of a good with that of labour@The third"
The first slip is thinking the real price moves whenever the money price does. The second is reading Smith as saying money prices don't matter. The third is mixing up the real price of a good, the labour it commands, with the real price of labour, the necessaries given for it.

@art corn-sheaf
That leaves a practical problem. The price of labour at distant times and places can scarce ever be known exactly. So Smith proposes to use the price of corn instead, and the next lesson reads what corn can and can't do.
