@chapter The question
@title text="Corn Rents and Money Rents" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 5" art=thumb
If labour is the real measure of value, but nobody records its price, what can stand in for it? Smith says the distinction between real and nominal price isn't just speculation. It can be useful in practice.

@art estate-rent
His example is a landed estate sold with a rent reserved forever. If the family wants that rent always to keep the same value, they shouldn't take it as a fixed sum of money, because money can lose value in two ways.

@list heading="Two ways a money rent falls" items="Less metal in a coin of the same name@less metal|The same metal buying less@the same weight"
First, the coin of the same name can come to hold less metal. Princes often thought they had an interest in reducing it, and seldom in raising it. Second, the same weight of metal can buy less, as when America's silver cheapened it. A rent in ounces of silver escapes the first, but not the second.

@chapter The college leases
@art corn-third
Then a real case. In the eighteenth year of Elizabeth, a law said a third of the rent of all college leases had to be paid in corn, or its current market price. According to Dr. Blackstone, as Smith reports, the corn third now brings near double the other two thirds.

@math heading="A college rent of 60s" items="corn third:  20s at 5s = 4 quarters@four quarters;;now near 80s for 4 quarters:  20s a quarter@near double;;money two-thirds:  40 / 5 = 8 quarters@eight quarters;;now:  40 / 20 = 2 quarters, down 75%@Now it buys two"
Check it with the lesson's figures. A rent of sixty shillings: twenty in corn, forty in money. At five shillings a quarter, the corn third is four quarters. Now it brings near double the money part, near eighty shillings, so corn is twenty shillings a quarter. The money forty bought eight quarters. Now it buys two, a fall of seventy five per cent.

@words text="Sunk almost to a fourth" sub="the English coin barely changed"
That's Smith's fourth. And it wasn't the coin. Since Philip and Mary, the English pound, shilling and penny held very nearly the same silver. The fall came altogether from silver's own cheapening. In Scotland and France, where the coin changed too, some old rents fell almost to nothing.

@chapter Corn as the measure
@words text="Corn, the subsistence of the labourer"
So Smith's rule: over distant times, equal quantities of labour are bought more nearly with equal quantities of corn, the labourer's subsistence, than with gold or silver. But he qualifies it at once. Even corn won't do it exactly, because subsistence is more generous in a society growing richer than in one standing still.

@math heading="A rent of 100s, three centuries" items="corn at 20s:  100 / 20 = 5 quarters@twenty shillings;;at 40s:  100 / 40 = 5/2@forty;;at 80s:  100 / 80 = 5/4@eighty;;change:  −75%@seventy five"
Here's the long run, in the lesson's figures. A money rent of a hundred shillings, while a quarter of corn goes from twenty shillings, to forty, to eighty. The rent buys five quarters, then five halves, then five quarters of a quarter. The money never changed, and the rent lost seventy five per cent of its worth in corn.

@art shrinking-rent
Each century in this example, the same money buys half the corn it did the century before. Nothing about the rent's figure warns you.

@chapter The dear year
@art dear-year-swing
But over a single year it's the other way round. Smith says corn can double from one year to the next, from twenty five shillings a quarter to fifty. A money rent of two hundred and fifty shillings buys ten quarters, then five. In corn, it halved.

@words text="250 days both years" sub="corn rent: 250 days → 500"
In labour, it didn't. The money price of labour follows the average price of corn, not this year's. With a day at a shilling, the lesson's figure, the money rent commands two hundred and fifty days both years. A corn rent of ten quarters commands two hundred and fifty, then five hundred.

@quote text="From century to century, corn is a better measure than silver" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 5"
So, from century to century, corn is a better measure than silver. And from year to year, silver is better than corn.

@chapter Where people slip
@art century-vs-year
Choosing the measure is part of the problem. Over a century, corn. Over a year, silver. And for ordinary buying and selling, Smith says, the distinction is no use at all.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Blaming the coin when silver cheapened@The first|Thinking a corn rent is fixed in value@The second|One measure for every period@The third"
The first slip is blaming the coin, when the colleges lost three quarters with the coin unchanged. The second is thinking a corn rent is fixed in value; in a dear year it can double. The third is taking one measure as right for every period.

@art basket-of-goods
Later economists offered another answer, a basket of goods: the index number, which W. S. Jevons used in 1863. It has its own difficulty, choosing what goes in the basket.
