@chapter The question
@title text="Gold, Silver and Copper Coin" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 5" art=thumb
A pound is a pound, isn't it? Not to Smith. The end of chapter five is about the coin itself, and the idea is simple: a coin is a measure only as far as it holds the metal it names.

@art three-metals
Commercial nations coin several metals: gold for large payments, silver for moderate ones, and copper for small. But they treat one as the real measure of value, usually the first they used. In England, all accounts are kept in silver. So the pound sterling is a sum of silver.

@chapter The law's ratio
@art guinea-shillings
Then the law fixes a ratio. A guinea, say, is declared worth twenty one shillings. Change that to twenty, and debts written in silver need the same silver, but more gold. Smith says which metal looks fixed is altogether owing to the custom of keeping accounts.

@math heading="A debt of 42s" items="guinea at 21s:  42 / 21 = 2 guineas@twenty one;;guinea at 20s:  42 / 20 = 2 1/10 guineas@reduced to twenty"
A debt of forty two shillings, with the guinea at twenty one, is two guineas. With the guinea reduced to twenty, it's forty two over twenty, two and one tenth guineas.

@art copper-pence
And the most valuable metal holds up the rest. Twelve copper pence hold half a pound of copper, seldom worth sevenpence in silver. Yet because the rules say twelve exchange for a shilling, the market treats them as a shilling.

@chapter The mint
@math heading="The English mint" items="1 lb gold → 44½ guineas@forty four;;44½ · 21 = 934½s = £46 14s 6d@forty six pounds;;one ounce:  £3 17s 10½d@an ounce;;1 lb silver → 62s;;  62 / 12 = 5s 2d an ounce@sixty two shillings"
At the mint, a pound weight of gold is coined into forty four and a half guineas. At twenty one shillings each, that's forty six pounds, fourteen shillings and sixpence. So an ounce of gold coin is worth three pounds, seventeen shillings and tenpence halfpenny. And a pound weight of silver makes sixty two shillings, so five shillings and twopence an ounce.

@art mint-press
In Europe's market, an ounce of gold buys about fourteen ounces of silver. In the English coin, about fifteen. So the coin rates silver a little below its worth. Restore the silver coin to full weight, Smith says, and there'd be a profit in melting it down. Later this tendency got a name, Gresham's law, from H. D. Macleod in the eighteen fifties.

@chapter The silver in a penny
@words text="6s 8d then = £1 now" sub="80 pence = a third of 240"
Here's Smith's definition. The money price of goods is the quantity of pure gold or silver they sell for, whatever the coin is called. So six shillings and eightpence in Edward the First's time is the same money price as a pound in his own. Eighty pence is a third of two hundred and forty.

@art thinning-penny
Each period, the lab takes the pence coined from one pound weight of silver. Under Edward the First, two hundred and forty, a pennyweight each. In Smith's day, the mint's sixty two shillings, which is seven hundred and forty four pence.

@math heading="240 pence, then 744" items="silver in a penny:  240 / 744 = 10/31@ten thirty firsts;;old pound:  744 pence = £3 2s@three pounds two;;£100 = 24000d:  24000 / 744 ≈ 32 lb of silver, not 100@thirty two;;creditor's loss:  1 − 10/31 = 21/31@twenty one"
Each penny now holds two hundred and forty over seven hundred and forty four, ten thirty firsts, nearly a third. The old pound comes to seven hundred and forty four new pence, three pounds two shillings. A debt of a hundred pounds, lent as a hundred pounds weight of silver, is repaid with about thirty two. The creditor loses twenty one thirty firsts, about sixty eight per cent.

@chapter Scotland and France
@art creditor-loss
Smith's fractions are rougher further north and across the Channel. A Scots pound at about a thirty sixth of its first silver, a French livre at about a sixty sixth. Such debasements let princes pay their debts in appearance only. Their creditors were really defrauded, he says, favourable to the debtor and ruinous to the creditor.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking debasement only changes a name@The first|Taking Smith's fractions as mint records@The second|Treating the standard metal as a fact@The third"
The first slip is thinking a debased coin only changes its name. It shrinks every debt in silver. The second is taking Smith's fractions as mint records. They're his estimates, and his Tower pound was a little lighter than the mint's Troy pound, so the match is rough. The third is treating the standard metal as a fact, when it matters only once the ratio changes.

@words text="240 pence, then 744" sub="a debt of £100 = 24,000 pence"
Your turn. Divide the old coinage by the new to get the silver in a penny. Then divide twenty four thousand pence by each, and say what share the creditor loses. You should find twenty one thirty firsts.
