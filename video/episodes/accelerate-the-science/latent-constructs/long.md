@chapter Can you trust a survey?
@title text="Latent Constructs" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 13" art=thumb
Every reader of survey research has the same doubt: how far can you trust what people say? Chapter thirteen of Accelerate answers it, and it turns on one idea.

@quote text="A latent construct is a way of measuring something that can’t be measured directly." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 13"
A latent construct is a way of measuring something that can't be measured directly. Before we see how, the chapter agrees that the doubt is fair.

@chapter What makes a bad survey
@art bad-survey
Much of the scepticism is earned by the surveys most of us meet: push polls built to make you agree with the pollster, quick surveys, and surveys written by people without training.

@list heading="Four common faults" items="Leading questions@Leading|Loaded questions@Loaded|Multiple questions in one@Multiple|Unclear language@Unclear"
The usual faults are four. Leading questions steer you. Loaded questions force an answer that may not be true for you. Multiple questions in one leave nobody knowing which part you answered. Unclear language uses words you may not share.

@words text="One question: narrow conclusions only"
A related weakness is resting everything on one question. The authors don't dismiss single question surveys. The Net Promoter Score, they say, is careful and well documented. But only narrow conclusions should be drawn from it.

@chapter Constructs and items
@art dial-and-items
A room's temperature or a website's response time can be read from a dial. A team's culture can't. It's measured through its parts, the manifest variables: items a person answers. Together they stand for the construct. That suits culture, the authors write, because culture is the lived experience of the people on the team.

@chapter Define it first
@art fuzzy-question
So you have to say what you mean. A question like, is your culture good, leaves unclear whose culture, what culture means, and what good means. No answer to it tells you anything.

@list heading="Westrum's six aspects of culture" items="Cooperation@Cooperation|How messengers are treated@messengers|Who is responsible@responsible|Bridging between teams@bridging|How failure is handled@failure|How novelty is received@novelty"
The authors took Ron Westrum's typology from twenty oh four: pathological, bureaucratic and generative organisations. They're told apart by six aspects. Cooperation, how messengers are treated, who is responsible, bridging between teams, how failure is handled, and how novelty is received.

@art team-not-org
From those aspects they wrote seven items, asked about a team, not the organisation. People answer more accurately about their own team, and large organisations hold different cultures in different corners. Each item is rated from one to seven, and the average is, in the authors' phrase, a culture temperature of sorts.

@chapter Score a construct
@art four-items
The lab's construct is invented for the lesson: confidence in releasing, from four items. Three are worded so agreeing means more confidence: routine, unaided and clarity. The fourth, dread, is worded the other way, so it's reverse coded.

@math heading="Preset four-items, person R1" items="answers 5, 4, 1, 5@five, four, one, five;;dread reversed: 8 − 1 = 7@eight minus one;;5 + 4 + 7 + 5 = 21, ÷ 4 = 5.25@five point two five;;construct mean = 107/24 ≈ 4.458@four point four five eight"
R one answers five, four, one, five. Reverse the dread answer: eight minus one is seven. So R one scores twenty one over four, five point two five. Over six invented people, the construct mean is a hundred and seven over twenty four, four point four five eight. Four are above the neutral line of four.

@math heading="What goes wrong" items="forget the reversal: mean 4.125@four point one two five;;drop clarity: mean 13/3 ≈ 4.333@thirteen thirds;;R3: 4.25 → 3.667@three point six six seven"
Forget the reversal and the mean falls to four point one two five, and R one drops below the line. Or drop the clarity item: the mean is thirteen thirds, four point three three three, and R three falls from four point two five to three point six six seven. One item moved one person across the line.

@art several-legs
That's why the chapter says to use several items. A single item can carry a lot of a person's score, and having more than one lets you see when it does.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="One broad question as a measure@broad question|Averaging without reversing@without turning|A score as a fact about the team@fact"
Three slips. Asking one broad question and calling it a measure. Averaging items without turning the reversed ones round. And taking a construct's score for a fact about the team. It's a mean of reported perceptions, and it still has to pass tests.
