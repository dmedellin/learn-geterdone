@chapter What an argument is
@title text="Premises, Conclusions and Standard Form" sub="Arguments and Validity" art=thumb
Before you can judge an argument, you have to find it. And everyday writing hides arguments well.

@art support-arrows
Here's the definition. An argument is a set of sentences in which one, the conclusion, is offered as supported by the others, the premises. If a passage just states claims and offers no support for any of them, it's a description, not an argument.

@art passage-to-form
Real passages don't come neatly laid out. They start with the conclusion, wander into asides, repeat themselves, and leave out the step everyone's assumed to grant. Writing a passage in standard form means deciding, sentence by sentence, which job it's doing.

@chapter A first example
@list heading="The wet streets" items="P1. The streets are wet.@the streets are wet|P2. Streets here are wet only after rain.@only after rain|C. It rained.@So it rained"
Take this one. The streets are wet. Streets here are only wet after rain. So it rained. The word so flags the last sentence. Set out, premise one is that the streets are wet, premise two is that streets here are wet only after rain, and the conclusion is that it rained.

@chapter Three kinds of sentence
@list heading="Three jobs" items="Premises give the support@Premises|The conclusion is what it's for@The conclusion|Padding does no work@everything else"
Three kinds of sentence turn up in a passage. Premises give the support. The conclusion is what the support is for. And everything else, a digression, a repetition, a flourish, is padding.

@art strike-padding
Here's the test. Delete the sentence in your head. If what the passage gives you reason to accept is unchanged, it was padding, and it stays out of the standard form.

@art conclusion-first
And don't look for the conclusion at the end. Ask what the passage wants you to believe or do. The bus will be late, since the road is closed. That puts the conclusion first and the premise last.

@chapter The four moves
@list heading="Four moves, in order" items="Find what is being defended@find what's being defended|Mark the indicator words@mark the indicator words|Strike what does no work@strike|Write it out@write it out"
So there are four moves. First, find what's being defended. Second, mark the indicator words. Therefore, so and hence point to a conclusion. Since and because point to a premise. Treat each as a clue, not a rule. Third, strike the sentences that do no work. That's the one people skip. Fourth, write it out: number the premises, draw a line, and write the conclusion once.

@chapter A table of cases
@math heading="p: the streets are wet · q: it rained" items="p T, q T@both true;;p T, q F@only the first;;p F, q T@only the second;;p F, q F@neither"
To make support exact, use a table of cases. Let p be the streets are wet, and q be it rained. Each sentence can be true or false, so two sentences give four cases: both true, only the first, only the second, and neither.

@art case-grid
Now take p alone as the premise, and q as the conclusion. Look for a row where every premise is true and the conclusion is false. There's exactly one: p true and q false. The streets are wet, and it didn't rain. In that row, the support has failed.

@art missing-premise
And premise two is exactly the sentence that rules that row out. That's why the passage needed it, and why an author who leaves it unstated is still relying on it. Add a missing premise only when the passage plainly depends on it, and say so.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking the last sentence as the conclusion@The first|Treating every sentence as a premise@The second|Reading because as the mark of an argument@the third"
The first slip is taking the conclusion to be whatever comes last. The second is treating every sentence as a premise. Add two premises that do no work, and you get four extra rows, and not one more failing row. And the third is reading because as the mark of an argument. The streets are wet because it rained may just explain a fact nobody doubts.

@chapter Try it
@words text="“Since the lease forbids pets, and Ana keeps a cat, Ana is in breach. Incidentally, the cat is orange.”"
Your turn. Since the lease forbids pets, and Ana keeps a cat, Ana is in breach. Incidentally, the cat is orange. Which sentence is the conclusion, which are premises, and which one is padding? Write it in standard form, then delete the orange cat in your head, and check that the support for the conclusion has not changed at all.
