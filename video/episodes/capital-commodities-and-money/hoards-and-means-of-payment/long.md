@chapter Pay later
@title text="Hoards and Means of Payment" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3" art=thumb
You live with this all the time. Rent is paid at the end of the month for a flat you've already lived in. A supplier delivers now and sends an invoice due in thirty days.

@art house-let
Marx uses a house let for a term. The tenant buys it before he pays for it. The seller becomes a creditor, the buyer a debtor, and money takes on a new job. But first, the money that stops.

@chapter The hoard
@art hoarder
When a sale isn't followed by a purchase, money changes from movable into immovable. It becomes a hoard. Marx is sardonic about the hoarder, whose cardinal virtues are hard work, saving and avarice, and whose labour is Sisyphus's, since any sum is limited and money's power isn't.

@art reservoir
But hoards also do a job. They're the reservoir circulation draws coin from, and where surplus coin drains, so circulation never overflows its banks. That's where the gold went that circulation didn't need, in the last lesson.

@chapter Means of payment
@list heading="Money works three times" items="Measures the debt@measures the debt|Moves the goods on a promise@on a promise|Pays on the due date@due date"
When goods change hands before money does, money works three times over. It measures the debt in the contract. It moves the goods on a promise. And on the due date it comes in as the means of payment, not to start a transaction, but to end one.

@words text="If he do not pay, his goods will be sold by the sheriff"
The debtor sold his goods in order to pay. And if he do not pay, Marx writes, his goods will be sold by the sheriff.

@math heading="Money a period needs" items="prices − credit sales@the prices;;+ payments falling due@falling due;;− payments that cancel@cancel;;÷ velocity@velocity"
Marx's sentence says the money a period needs is the prices, plus payments falling due, minus those that balance. The lab writes it as one formula: take the prices, subtract the sales made on credit, add the payments falling due, subtract the payments that cancel, and divide by the velocity. That's the lab's formula, not one Marx writes.

@chapter A circle of debts
@quote text="The debts due to A from B, to B from C, to C from A, and so on, have only to be confronted with each other, in order to annul each other to a certain extent like positive and negative quantities." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3, §3"
How do payments cancel? The debts due to A from B, to B from C, to C from A, and so on, have only to be confronted with each other, in order to annul each other to a certain extent like positive and negative quantities. Medieval merchants did exactly this at the fairs of Lyons.

@art debt-circle
In the lab, three dealers, A, B and C, each sell a hundred pounds of goods. For cash at the same moment, the three sales need three hundred pounds. On credit, with A owing B, B owing C and C owing A, all due today, the debts cancel.

@math heading="A circle of debts" items="A: −100 + 100 = 0 · B: 0 · C: 0@cancel to zero;;cash to settle: £0@no money at all;;A defaults: A +100 · B −100 · C 0@fail to pay;;cash to settle: £100, all of it B's@a hundred pounds"
Each dealer's balance comes to zero, so the debts cancel to zero, and no money at all is needed. Now let A fail to pay. B still owes C a hundred pounds and has nothing coming in. So a hundred pounds must be found in hard cash, all of it by B.

@chapter A crisis in miniature
@art broken-chain
That's a money crisis in its smallest form. Three hundred pounds of debts needed nothing while they cancelled. One failure turned a hundred pounds of them into a demand for coin.

@quote text="Commodities alone are money. But now the cry is everywhere: money alone is a commodity!" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3, §3"
In good times, money is only money of account. On the eve of crisis, Marx says, the bourgeois calls money a vain imagination. Commodities alone are money. But now the cry is everywhere: money alone is a commodity! When the chain breaks, everyone needs the means of payment at once.

@art bills-circulating
Credit money grows straight out of this: bills that record debts circulate to transfer them. And as rents and taxes turn from payments in kind into money, every country gets its settling days.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Money only as a medium of exchange@The first|Each debt needs its own coin@The second|The hoarder as outside the system@And the third"
The first slip is thinking money only matters as a medium of exchange. In the broken case every sale was on credit, and still a hundred pounds was needed. The second is adding up debts as if each needed its own coin. It's the net balances that count. And the third is treating the hoarder as outside the system.
