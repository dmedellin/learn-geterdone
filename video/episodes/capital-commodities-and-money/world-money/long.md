@chapter At the border
@title text="World Money" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Everything money does at home, it does with a national name: pounds, shillings, coins stamped by the state, paper tokens. So what happens when it leaves the country?

@quote text="When money leaves the home sphere of circulation, it strips off the local garbs which it there assumes, of a standard of prices, of coin, of tokens, and of a symbol of value, and returns to its original form of bullion." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3, §3"
Marx's answer. When money leaves the home sphere of circulation, it strips off the local garbs which it there assumes, of a standard of prices, of coin, of tokens, and of a symbol of value, and returns to its original form of bullion.

@chapter The local garbs
@art local-garbs
Every garb in that list is something earlier lessons built. The standard of price was a weight of gold given a national name. Coin was that weight, stamped. Tokens and paper stood in for it, held in place by forced currency inside the state's own territory.

@art bullion-by-weight
None of that crosses a border. A pound note means nothing to a merchant in Hamburg except as a claim on gold. So in the markets of the world, money is the metal, by weight. Only there, Marx says, does money fully become what its concept says it is.

@words text="Two measures abroad: gold and silver"
At home there can be only one measure of value. In the world market of Marx's day there were two, gold and silver, because different countries used different metals.

@chapter Three functions
@list heading="Money of the world" items="Universal means of payment@means of payment|Universal means of purchase@means of purchase|Wealth itself, moved@wealth itself"
The three functions are the home ones, at a larger scale. As a means of payment, gold settles what one country owes another once their trade is set off. As a means of purchase, it's shipped when exchange is suddenly disturbed, after a failed harvest, say. And as wealth itself, it moves for a loan, a subsidy or a war indemnity.

@quote text="Its function as a means of payment in the settling of international balances is its chief one. Hence the watchword of the mercantilists, balance of trade." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3, §3"
Of the three, its function as a means of payment in the settling of international balances is its chief one. Hence the watchword of the mercantilists, balance of trade.

@chapter The mercantilists
@art balance-of-trade
The mercantilists deserve a hearing. Thomas Mun's England's Treasure by Forraign Trade, from sixteen sixty four, argued that a country grows rich by selling abroad more than it buys, and taking the difference in gold and silver. Where only bullion settled accounts, that wasn't foolish.

@words text="Ricardo: “a redundant currency”"
Smith attacked them for confusing money with wealth. Marx turns the criticism round. It's their opponents who misunderstood world money, Ricardo above all, who held that an unfavourable balance only ever comes from a redundant currency. Marx's reply is the same as before: the balance moves the gold, not the other way round.

@chapter Settling the balance
@math heading="Settling in bullion" items="£1,000 imports − £1,000 exports = 0@no bullion moves;;£1,000 − £800 = £200 out@two hundred pounds;;+ £100 interest owed abroad@interest;;200 + 100 = £300 shipped in gold@three hundred"
The lab does the settling. Imports of a thousand pounds against exports of a thousand balance, and no bullion moves. Let exports fall to eight hundred, and two hundred pounds must be shipped abroad in gold. Add a hundred pounds of interest owed abroad, and the drain is three hundred. The names pound and shilling stay at home. What crosses is gold.

@chapter Two currents
@art two-currents
Gold moves in two currents. One runs outward from the mines into every national circulation, to fill its channels, replace worn coin and lie in hoards. The other flows back and forth between countries as their balances change.

@art full-vault
So every country needs a reserve for world payments as well as home ones. And Marx adds a sign worth remembering. Bank reserves far above their usual level are, with some exceptions, a sign of stagnation, of commodities that aren't moving.

@art gold-ship
This is the gold standard world Marx was describing. Today currencies aren't convertible into gold, balances are settled in other ways, and the course doesn't model them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="World money as a currency at a rate@The first|The balance of trade as wealth@The second|Leaving out payments not for goods@And the third"
The first slip is treating world money as just a currency exchanged at a rate. The pound stays home. The second is taking the balance of trade for a measure of wealth. And the third is leaving out payments that aren't for goods, like interest, which ship gold as surely as trade does.
