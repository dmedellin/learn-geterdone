@chapter The pivot
@title text="The Twofold Character of Labour" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1" art=thumb
The last lesson found two things in a commodity: a use-value and a value. Marx now says the labour inside it has the same two sides.

@quote text="As this point is the pivot on which a clear comprehension of political economy turns, we must go more into detail." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §2"
He calls it his own discovery, and adds: as this point is the pivot on which a clear comprehension of political economy turns, we must go more into detail.

@words text="Smith and Ricardo" sub="labour is the measure of value"
That's a large claim. Adam Smith and David Ricardo had already said labour measures value. What Marx says they missed is that labour counts twice over, in two different ways, and that the two can move in opposite directions.

@chapter Useful labour
@art tailor-and-weaver
Take the first way. A coat is made by tailoring, a special kind of activity, set by its aim, its tools, its materials and its result. As useful labour, tailoring is counted by what it makes: so many coats.

@words text="Coats are not exchanged for coats"
And it has to differ from weaving, because coats are not exchanged for coats. Commodities only meet because a social division of labour lies behind them. But that division doesn't need commodities. A factory divides work by plan, and an old Indian village community did it with nobody selling anything.

@art two-parents
Useful labour isn't the coat's only source, either. Take the labour away and a material is left, furnished by nature. Marx borrows William Petty's line: labour is the father of material wealth, and the earth its mother. Useful things have two parents. Value, Marx insists, has only one.

@chapter Abstract labour
@art hours-only
Now the second way. Set aside what tailoring and weaving make, and each is a productive spending of human brains, nerves and muscles. That's the labour that forms value, and it's counted only in time. How much, and how long, rather than how and what.

@art skill-blocks
Marx says skilled labour counts as multiplied simple labour, and then, for simplicity, treats every hour as simple. The book never computes that multiplier, and neither does the lesson. Boehm Bawerk objected that the only evidence for it is the exchange ratios it was meant to explain.

@chapter Where they part
@quote text="Nevertheless, an increased quantity of material wealth may correspond to a simultaneous fall in the magnitude of its value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §2"
The two sides part company the moment productivity changes. Two coats clothe two people, one coat only one. Nevertheless, an increased quantity of material wealth may correspond to a simultaneous fall in the magnitude of its value.

@art more-coats-same-day
Think of anything that got cheaper as it got easier to make. There's more of it, a real gain, and each unit is worth less. Productivity belongs only to useful labour. It changes how many coats an hour yields. It can't change the hour.

@chapter One day, three ways
@math heading="A tailor's 10-hour day" items="1 coat: 10 h / 1 = 10 h a coat@one coat;;2 coats: 10 h / 2 = 5 h a coat@two coats;;½ coat: a coat needs 20 h@half a coat"
The lab runs one ten hour day three ways. At first ten hours make one coat, worth ten hours. Double productivity and they make two coats, five hours each. Halve it and they make half a coat, so a whole coat is worth twenty hours.

@math heading="The day's output" items="before: 1 · 10 = 10 h@before;;after: 2 · 5 = 10 h@after"
Now multiply back. Before, one coat at ten hours is ten hours. After, two coats at five hours is still ten. The use-values doubled and the value didn't.

@art linen-and-coat
Weaving runs alongside. On Marx's figures, twenty yards of linen are worth one coat. When tailoring doubles its productivity and weaving doesn't, a coat is worth five hours, and so only ten yards. The linen is unchanged, and what it fetches in coats has doubled.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="More useful things means more value@The first|Productivity as a property of the hour@The second|The skill multiplier as computed@And the third"
The first slip is thinking more useful things always means more value. The doubled day refutes it in one line. The second is treating productivity as a property of the hour. It belongs to the concrete labour. And the third is taking the skill multiplier as computed, when Marx simply assumes it.

@chapter Try it
@words text="10-hour day · ½ a coat" sub="what is one coat worth?"
Your turn. Tailoring's productivity halves, so a ten hour day makes half a coat. What is one coat now worth? Then ask which change would alter the value the day creates: more coats, fewer coats, or a twelve hour day.
