@chapter Where Marx starts
@title text="The Two Factors of a Commodity" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1" art=thumb
Look around the room you're in. Nearly everything in it was made by strangers, for sale.

@quote text="The wealth of those societies in which the capitalist mode of production prevails, presents itself as ‘an immense accumulation of commodities,’ its unit being a single commodity." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §1"
That's how Capital opens. The wealth of those societies in which the capitalist mode of production prevails, presents itself as an immense accumulation of commodities, its unit being a single commodity. So Marx starts with one item from the pile, and assumes nothing yet about money, wages or profit.

@chapter Use-value
@art useful-things
The first factor is the easy one. A commodity is a thing that, by its properties, satisfies human wants. That usefulness is its use-value, and it belongs to the thing's body. Iron is useful as iron, linen as linen, and they'd be useful in any society.

@words text="Use-value" sub="the material of all wealth, in any society"
Use-values are the substance of all wealth, whatever its social form. But in this society they're also something else: the bearers of exchange value.

@chapter The third thing
@art wheat-balance
Now the turn. A quarter of wheat exchanges for so much silk, so much gold, so much iron. Each of those is worth the same quarter of wheat, so they must be worth the same as one another.

@math heading="An exchange" items="1 quarter corn = x cwt iron@one quarter of corn;;equal in some third respect@some third respect"
Write any exchange as one quarter of corn equals x hundredweight of iron. The equation says two different things are equal in some third respect.

@art abstract-labour
It can't be weight, or colour, or usefulness, because exchange ignores exactly those. Take them away and one property is left. Each is a product of labour. Not weaving or mining in particular, but human labour in the abstract. Crystals of that, Marx says, are values.

@words text="A disputed step" sub="Böhm-Bawerk, 1896 · Jevons, Menger, Walras, 1870s"
This is the step most often challenged. Boehm Bawerk argued in eighteen ninety six that the elimination is rigged, since commodities also share usefulness and scarcity. The marginal utility economists of the eighteen seventies explained exchange with no labour behind it. The lesson doesn't settle that. It computes what follows from Marx's definitions.

@chapter Socially necessary time
@words text="Idle work, more value?"
If value is labour counted in hours, you might object at once. Wouldn't the idle, unskilful worker make the most valuable things, because he takes longer? Marx raises that objection himself.

@quote text="The labour-time socially necessary is that required to produce an article under the normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity prevalent at the time." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §1"
His answer. The labour time socially necessary is that required to produce an article under the normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity prevalent at the time.

@art power-loom
And prevalent at the time means the standard moves. When the power loom came into England, a hand loom weaver working exactly as before found that one hour of his labour represented only half an hour's social labour. Nothing in his work changed. The society around it did.

@chapter Three weavers
@art three-weavers
The lesson's lab turns this into a sum. It takes the average over every producer as its stand in for normal conditions. Three weavers need one, two and three hours a yard, and make forty, forty and twenty yards.

@math heading="Three weavers" items="40·1 + 40·2 + 20·3 = 180 h@a hundred and eighty hours;;40 + 40 + 20 = 100 yards@a hundred yards;;180 / 100 = 9/5 h a yard@nine fifths;;20 · 9/5 = 36 h@thirty six hours;;3 − 9/5 = 6/5 h not counted@six fifths"
That's a hundred and eighty hours for a hundred yards, so nine fifths of an hour a yard. A lot of twenty yards is worth thirty six hours. Weaver C needs three hours a yard, so six fifths of an hour on every yard isn't counted.

@art slow-weaver
Now slow weaver C down to five hours a yard.

@math heading="Slow C down to 5 h a yard" items="40 + 80 + 100 = 220 h@two hundred and twenty;;220 / 100 = 11/5 h a yard@eleven fifths;;C's 20 yards = 44 h@forty four hours;;40 extra hours: 8 in C's yards · 32 in others'@eight of them"
The hours become two hundred and twenty, and the yard rises to eleven fifths. His twenty yards are worth forty four hours, though he spent a hundred. Of his forty extra hours, eight of them show up in his own yards. The other thirty two are spread over yards the others made.

@words text="Power-loom preset: 6/5 h a yard" sub="C's hour: from 3/5 to 2/5 of a social hour"
Run the power loom on the same weavers: A and B halve their hours, and the yard falls to six fifths. Each hour C works now represents two fifths of an hour of social labour, where it used to represent three fifths.

@chapter What follows
@list heading="From the definition" items="More productive labour, less value@the less an article|Useful but no value: air, virgin soil@air|Made for yourself: not a commodity@for yourself|Useless: no value@nothing useless"
The rest follows. The more productive labour is, the less an article is worth. A thing can be useful without value, like air or virgin soil, because no labour made it. A useful product made for yourself isn't a commodity. And nothing useless has value.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Slower work makes more value@The first|Exchange value is value itself@The second|Anything useful has value@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a commodity made more slowly is worth more. The second is reading exchange value as value itself, when the ratios are only how value shows. And the third is treating anything useful as having value.

@chapter Try it
@words text="2 h × 30 yards · 4 h × 10 yards" sub="time per yard = total hours / total yards"
Your turn. One weaver works at two hours a yard and makes thirty. Another works at four hours a yard and makes ten. Total the hours and divide by the yards. The plain average of two and four won't do.
