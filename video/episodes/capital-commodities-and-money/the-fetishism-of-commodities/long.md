@chapter A very queer thing
@title text="The Fetishism of Commodities" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1" art=thumb
You know the feeling even if you've never named it. A price seems to belong to the thing. A coffee costs what it costs, the way it weighs what it weighs, and the people who grew, shipped and roasted it are nowhere in the number.

@quote text="A commodity appears, at first sight, a very trivial thing, and easily understood. Its analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very queer thing, abounding in metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §4"
Marx's claim is that the number is really a relation between all those people's labour. A commodity appears, at first sight, a very trivial thing, and easily understood. Its analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very queer thing, abounding in metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties.

@chapter The table stands on its head
@art table-on-its-head
A table made of wood is just wood, until it's sold. Then, Marx jokes, it stands on its head and evolves notions far more wonderful than table turning, the seances fashionable when he wrote. The joke is also the argument.

@words text="Not the use-value. The form." sub="relations between people → relations between things"
The mystery doesn't come from the table's usefulness, or from the plain fact that labour takes time. It comes from the form. The equality of labours shows up as equal values of products, and the relations between producers show up as a relation between things.

@chapter Behind everyone's back
@art private-producers
Why? Because producers work independently and only meet when they exchange their products. Nobody decides how much weaving society needs. Weavers find out from prices. So the proportions of social labour are set behind everyone's back.

@words text="Things rule the producers"
To each producer, those proportions arrive as the movement of things, which rule the producers instead of being ruled by them. Marx calls this the fetishism of commodities, after objects to which people ascribe powers that are really their own.

@chapter Robinson's ledger
@art robinson-ledger
To show the hours are there all along, Marx goes where they aren't hidden: to Robinson Crusoe. Robinson tames goats, fishes, makes furniture and tools. His stock book lists his things, the work each takes, and the hours they cost him on average.

@math heading="Robinson's 40-hour week" items="10 + 15 + 12 + 3 = 40 h@forty hours;;goats 10 / 2 = 5 h@five hours a goat;;fish 15 / 30 = ½ h@half an hour a fish;;furniture 12 / 2 = 6 h · tool 3 / 1 = 3 h@six hours;;one goat: 5 / ½ = 10 fish@ten fish"
The lab gives Robinson illustrative figures, since Marx gives none. Ten hours tame two goats, fifteen catch thirty fish, twelve make two pieces of furniture, and three make a tool. That's forty hours. Five hours a goat, half an hour a fish, six hours for furniture and three for the tool. So one goat equals ten fish.

@chapter The village
@art village-trade
Now give each product to a different household, thirty hours each, trading with one another. The hours per unit are identical, so the ratios are too: a goat for ten fish.

@art fish-and-goat
But in the village nobody reads the hours. They see that a goat fetches ten fish, and the ratio looks like a fact about goats. Robinson can say why. The villagers can only say what a goat fetches.

@chapter Other settings
@list heading="Hours reckoned openly" items="The medieval manor@manor|The peasant family@peasant family|An association of free individuals@association"
Marx's other settings reckon labour time openly too. On the medieval manor every serf knows what he spends in his lord's service. A peasant family divides its work by age, sex and season. And in an association of free individuals, Marx supposes, merely for a parallel, that shares are set by labour time.

@art association
The association preset is that community's whole output, a hundred and sixty hours. The lab can show its hours per unit. It can't show the shares, and it isn't a programme, only a contrast case.

@words text="Jevons, Menger · Lukács, 1923"
A marginal utility economist rejects the frame. For Jevons or Menger, a goat for ten fish reflects what one more of each is worth to the traders, with nothing behind it. Others widened Marx's idea: Lukacs made it central in nineteen twenty three. The lesson computes the hours and leaves you to judge.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Value as a physical property@The first|Fetishism as simple deception@The second|The association as a real society@And the third"
The first slip is thinking value is a physical property, like weight. No chemist, Marx says, has found exchange value in a pearl. The second is reading fetishism as deception. Nobody is fooling the villagers, and the appearance stays after it's explained. And the third is taking the association as a description of a real society.

@words text="Robinson's ledger" sub="hours per unit = hours / units made"
Your turn. Using Robinson's week, work out what one piece of furniture is worth in fish, and in goats. Then say whether, in the village, anyone could read that figure off their own work.
