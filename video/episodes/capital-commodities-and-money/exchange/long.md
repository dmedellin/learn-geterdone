@chapter Commodities can't walk
@title text="Exchange" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 2" art=thumb
Chapter one had linen expressing its value and coats acting as mirrors. Chapter two opens with a dry joke at its own expense.

@quote text="It is plain that commodities cannot go to market and make exchanges of their own account. We must, therefore, have recourse to their guardians, who are also their owners." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 2"
It is plain that commodities cannot go to market and make exchanges of their own account. We must, therefore, have recourse to their guardians, who are also their owners.

@art owners-at-market
Someone has to carry them, and that someone has a will of his own. So the owners must recognise each other as private proprietors, and their contract only reflects the economic relation underneath.

@words text="Personifications of economic relations"
One sentence here governs the rest of the book. The characters on the economic stage are personifications of the economic relations between them. When the capitalist appears later, he'll be read the same way.

@chapter The double test
@art leveller
Marx calls the commodity a born leveller and a cynic: it will exchange with anything at all. Its owner is particular. His own commodity is no use to him, which is why he brings it, and he wants something definite back.

@words text="Non-use-values for owners, use-values for non-owners"
All commodities are non use-values for their owners, and use-values for their non owners. So they must change hands, and they face a double test. They must be realised as values before they're used, and prove useful to someone before their value counts.

@chapter The owners' dilemma
@art every-owner-centre
Then the dilemma. To each owner, his own commodity is the universal equivalent of all the rest. Every owner thinks this. So in fact no commodity is the universal equivalent, and the goods can't be compared as values at all.

@quote text="But a particular commodity cannot become the universal equivalent except by a social act." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 2"
Marx answers with Goethe's Faust: in the beginning was the deed. The owners acted and transacted before they thought. But a particular commodity cannot become the universal equivalent except by a social act.

@art cattle-to-gold
Nobody sat down and chose money. Exchange began at the edges of communities, between strangers, with ratios at first a matter of chance. As it became regular, the equivalent's role attached to whatever was traded most, like cattle. Nomads developed the money form first. Over time it settled on the precious metals, whose every sample is alike and divisible at will.

@chapter Counting ratios
@art barter-web
The lab counts what one equivalent saves. If every owner trades with every other, each pair needs its own ratio. With n commodities, that's n times n minus one, over two.

@math heading="Six owners at market" items="barter: 6 · 5 / 2 = 15 ratios@fifteen ratios;;gold as equivalent: 6 − 1 = 5@five ratios;;cattle as equivalent: 6 − 1 = 5@cattle"
Six owners bring linen, coats, tea, coffee, corn and gold. Trading each with each, six times five over two is fifteen ratios. With gold as everyone's equivalent, six minus one is five ratios. And with cattle as the equivalent, it's five as well.

@art one-equivalent
Notice what the count doesn't care about: which commodity is the equivalent. The arithmetic shows why one equivalent is convenient. It can't show how one arises, or why it ended up as gold, and that's the part Marx cares about.

@chapter Not a convention
@words text="Locke · Montesquieu" sub="money as a sign, by consent"
Locke wrote that the consent of mankind gave silver an imaginary value, and Montesquieu called money a sign. Their best evidence is real: worn coins circulate, and Marx grants paper tokens can stand in for gold. His reply is that a token works only because it stands for a commodity with value.

@words text="Not its value, but its value-form"
Money is a commodity, then, and its value is fixed like any other's, by the labour time needed to produce it. Exchange gives gold not its value, but its specific value form. And once gold is money, the steps vanish. Gold seems to be money by nature. Hence the magic of money.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Money as a mere symbol@The first|The count as Marx's explanation@The second|Exchange gives gold its value@And the third"
The first slip is thinking money is a mere symbol fixed by convention. The second is reading the count as Marx's explanation. The owners never made that calculation. And the third is thinking exchange gives gold its value, when its value is set by the labour that produces it.
