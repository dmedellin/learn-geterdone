@chapter Value you can't see
@title text="The Form of Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1" art=thumb
This is the part of Chapter one that readers most often give up on, so let's say plainly what it's for. The first two sections found value behind exchange. But turn a coat over as long as you like, and you won't find its value in it.

@art coat-alone
Not an atom of matter enters into value. It can only appear in a relation between commodities. So Marx asks what that relation looks like at its simplest, and follows it until it becomes money.

@chapter Two poles
@quote text="The linen expresses its value in the coat; the coat serves as the material in which that value is expressed." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §3"
The simplest form is one equation: twenty yards of linen equals one coat. Read it as a sentence. The linen expresses its value in the coat; the coat serves as the material in which that value is expressed.

@art linen-equals-coat
So the linen is in the relative form, and the coat is in the equivalent form. They're two poles of one expression. Read it backwards, and the roles swap: now the coat's value is expressed, in linen. Which part a commodity plays depends only on where it stands. And linen equals linen expresses nothing.

@chapter The strange equivalent
@art sugar-and-iron
The equivalent is the strange one. Marx compares it with weighing a sugar loaf against iron, where the iron stands for weight alone. Then he stops the analogy. Weight is a natural property of both bodies. Value is something purely social.

@words text="Exchangeability looks natural"
Yet because it's the coat's own body that does the expressing, being exchangeable looks like a property of the coat. That appearance is the seed of the next lesson.

@chapter When values change
@list heading="Marx's four cases" items="Linen's value doubles: 20 yd = 2 coats@the linen's value doubles|Coat's value doubles: 20 yd = ½ coat@the coat's value doubles|Both change in proportion: no change@same proportion|Both change by different amounts@different amounts"
Marx takes four cases. If the linen's value doubles and the coat's doesn't, twenty yards fetch two coats. If the coat's value doubles, they fetch half a coat. If both change in the same proportion, nothing moves. And both can change by different amounts.

@math heading="The coat cheapens" items="20 yd linen (20 h) = 1 coat (20 h)@twenty hours each;;a coat now takes 10 h@ten hours;;20 h / 10 h = 2@twenty hours over ten;;20 yd linen = 2 coats@two coats"
The lab is built around one of these. Linen and a coat take twenty hours each. A new method means a coat now takes ten hours. Twenty hours over ten is two, so twenty yards of linen equals two coats.

@quote text="The relative value of a commodity may vary, although its value remains constant." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 1, §3"
The linen's value in coats has doubled, and nothing about linen changed. The relative value of a commodity may vary, although its value remains constant.

@chapter Bailey and Aristotle
@words text="Samuel Bailey, 1825" sub="value is only a ratio"
Samuel Bailey argued in eighteen twenty five that value is nothing but the ratio in which things exchange. That's a serious position, close to how economists still talk about relative prices. Marx says the equivalent form misled him. The same change in a ratio can come from either commodity, and Marx reads that as a quantity behind the ratio.

@art beds-and-house
Aristotle got remarkably far. He saw that five beds for one house is no different from five beds for so much money, and that exchange needs commensurability. Then he stopped. Marx's explanation is historical: Greek society rested on slave labour, so the idea that all human labour is equal couldn't yet take hold.

@chapter Four forms to money
@art expanded-form
Now build out from the one equation. In the expanded form, the linen is set against everything: a coat, or ten pounds of tea, or forty pounds of coffee, or two ounces of gold. Its defect is that it never ends.

@art general-form
Reverse it, and every commodity expresses its value in linen. That's the general form, with linen as the general equivalent. Put gold where linen stood, and nothing changes in the equations, only which commodity custom has fixed on. That's the money form. And twenty yards of linen equals two ounces of gold is the linen's price.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the equation as the coat's value@The first|Blaming the left side for a changed ratio@The second|Gold's power as natural@And the third"
The first slip is reading the equation as expressing the coat's value. It expresses the linen's. The second is blaming the left side when a ratio changes. Compare with a third commodity whose value didn't move. And the third is taking gold's power as natural, when it's a role fixed by custom.

@chapter Try it
@words text="20 yd linen = 1 coat" sub="linen's value doubles, the coat's doesn't"
Your turn. Start from twenty yards of linen equals one coat. Double the linen's value, and leave the coat alone. Which commodity is in the relative form, and how many coats do the twenty yards fetch now?
