@chapter Price tags
@title text="Money as Measure and Means of Circulation" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Every price you've ever read is money at work. But the shopkeeper who writes a price on a ticket uses no gold to do it.

@quote text="It is not money that renders commodities commensurable. Just the contrary." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3, §1"
Marx is clear about the order of things. It is not money that renders commodities commensurable. Just the contrary. Commodities are already commensurable, as realised human labour, and that's why one commodity can measure them all.

@art price-ticket
As a measure, money is imaginary or ideal. It doesn't take the least bit of real gold to price millions of pounds' worth of goods. Yet the price depends entirely on what money is made of. Measure a ton of iron in gold and in silver, and you get two very different prices for one value.

@chapter Measure and standard
@art gold-weights
Marx separates two jobs. As the measure of value, gold turns values into prices. As the standard of price, it's a fixed weight of metal, divided and named. The pound began as a pound weight of silver, and centuries of debasing left nothing of the original weights but the names.

@words text="Gold's value changes · the standard doesn't"
A change in gold's value leaves the standard alone, since twelve ounces are still twelve times one ounce. But it changes every price.

@words text="Wheat worth £2 may sell for £3 or £1"
A price can also part from value. A quarter of wheat worth two pounds may sell for three, or for one. Marx calls this no defect. And things with no value, like uncultivated land, can still have a price.

@chapter Selling in order to buy
@art weaver-to-bible
Now follow the weaver to market. He sells twenty yards of linen for two pounds, and spends the two pounds on a family Bible. That's C, M, C: selling in order to buy.

@words text="No one is bound to buy because he has just sold"
The first leap, from commodity to money, may fail, because the market may already have all the linen it wants. And once the halves are separate, they can come apart. Say's followers held that a general glut is impossible. Marx answers that the separation gives the possibility, and no more, of crisis.

@chapter The law of circulation
@art four-moves
Marx chains four sales. A quarter of wheat for two pounds, the two pounds for linen, then for a Bible, then for four gallons of brandy. Sold side by side, they need eight pounds. Sold one after another, the same two pounds makes four moves.

@quote text="the quantity of money functioning as the circulating medium is equal to the sum of the prices of the commodities divided by the number of moves made by coins of the same denomination." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 3, §2"
From this he draws a law. The quantity of money functioning as the circulating medium is equal to the sum of the prices of the commodities divided by the number of moves made by coins of the same denomination.

@math heading="Wheat, linen, Bible, brandy" items="4 · £2 = £8@eight pounds;;£8 / 4 = £2@velocity of four;;gold halves: 4 · £4 = £16@sixteen;;£16 / 4 = £4@four pounds"
The lab prices each at twenty hours, with a pound holding ten hours, so each is two pounds. Four times two is eight pounds of prices. At a velocity of four, the day needs two pounds. Now halve gold's value. Every price doubles to four pounds, the sum to sixteen, and the money needed to four pounds.

@chapter Hume against Marx
@art gold-and-prices
That last case is where Marx parts from David Hume. In seventeen fifty two, Hume argued that if a country's money were multiplied, prices would rise in proportion. It's a powerful argument, and it fits what followed American silver and gold into Europe.

@words text="Cheaper gold → higher prices → more gold"
Marx argues that for gold coin the causation runs the other way. Given the values and the velocity, the quantity of coin depends on gold's value. Cheaper gold raised prices, and higher prices drew more gold in. Gold that circulation doesn't need goes into hoards.

@art paper-and-gold
For paper, Marx agrees with a version of the quantity rule. Issue twice as much paper as the gold it replaces, and every price in pounds doubles. Paper has no value of its own to be withdrawn.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Coin quantity sets prices@The first|Measure confused with standard@The second|Price taken as value@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that, with gold coin, the quantity of money sets prices. The second is confusing the measure of value with the standard of price. And the third is taking a price to be the value, when it can sit above or below it.
