@chapter The question
@title text="Stable, Unstable and Semistable" sub="Equilibria, Stability and Phase Lines" art=thumb
An equilibrium is a constant solution. But will a real system stay there if it's nudged? The answer sits in the two arrows on either side of the dot.

@chapter Three cases
@art three-cases
Look at the two arrows touching an equilibrium. If both point towards it, it's stable. If both point away, it's unstable. If one points towards and one away, it's semistable. Each arrow has only two directions, so those are all the cases there are.

@list heading="The sign test" items="+ then −   →   stable@Positive then negative|− then +   →   unstable@Negative then positive|same sign both sides   →   semistable@The same sign"
In terms of f, read the sign just below the equilibrium and just above. Positive then negative gives arrows up then down, both towards it: stable. Negative then positive: unstable. The same sign on both sides: semistable. The value of f at the equilibrium is zero and says nothing.

@chapter The cubic
@math heading="f(y) = y³ − 4y² + 3y,   signs − + − +" items="y = 0:  − then +   unstable@At zero;;y = 1:  + then −   stable@At one;;y = 3:  − then +   unstable@At three"
Take the cubic from the last lesson, with signs minus, plus, minus, plus. At zero it's minus then plus, so arrows point away: unstable. At one it's plus then minus, both towards: stable. At three it's minus then plus again: unstable.

@art ball-on-hill
An unstable equilibrium is one a real system wouldn't stay at. The constant solution is exactly right, but a solution starting a hair away leaves. A stable one is where solutions from a whole interval end up, which is why it's the one a measurement is likely to find.

@chapter Where f touches zero
@math heading="f(y) = y²·(1 − y)" items="f(−1) = 1·2 = 2   +@minus one;;f(1/2) = (1/4)·(1/2) = 1/8   +@one half;;f(2) = 4·(−1) = −4   −@at two"
Now take y squared times one minus y. Test at minus one: one times two, two, positive. At one half: a quarter times a half, one eighth, positive. At two: four times minus one, minus four, negative.

@art touching-root
So zero has plus on both sides. Below it the arrow points up, towards zero. Above it the arrow points up, away. That's semistable. And one goes plus then minus, an ordinary stable equilibrium. The graph of f touches the axis at zero and turns back without crossing.

@words text="f′(0) = 0,  f′(1) = −1" sub="not the verdict"
The lab also prints the slopes, f prime of zero is zero and f prime of one is minus one. That zero tempts you to use it as the verdict. It isn't one. The signs decide, and they're always available.

@words text="harvest H = 1:  −(y − 2)²/4" sub="negative on both sides: semistable"
You met one before the word was defined. With a harvest of one, the rate was minus y minus two squared, over four, negative on both sides of two. A population above falls to two, and one below falls away.

@chapter Even and odd roots
@art multiplicity
For a polynomial, a root of even multiplicity is semistable. A root of odd multiplicity is stable or unstable, depending on which way f crosses. That's the sign argument factor by factor: an even power doesn't change sign, an odd one does. It's a shortcut. The sign test is the one to trust.

@chapter Near a semistable point
@art one-sided
Start y squared times one minus y at minus one. It rises, and approaches zero from below without reaching it. Start it at one half, and it rises to one, leaving zero behind for ever. Only the first is drawn in by zero, and only from below. That asymmetry is the whole content of the word.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Semistable as a hedge@The first|Classifying from one side only@The second|Saying a solution reaches a stable point@The third"
The first slip is treating semistable as a hedge, a bit stable. It's a third, exact case: stable from one side and unstable from the other. The second is classifying from one side only. From below, the arrow at zero points in, which looks stable. Only the arrow above shows it isn't. The third is saying a solution reaches a stable equilibrium.

@art stable-approach
It approaches it. It can't cross the equilibrium, and its speed, the size of f, goes to zero, so it doesn't arrive in finite time. The right statement is that the limit is one, and the lab's limit tile says exactly that.
