@chapter The question
@title text="One-Parameter Families" sub="Equilibria, Stability and Phase Lines" art=thumb
Put a number a into an equation, and turn it like a dial. The equilibria move. And at one special value, two of them meet, and the behaviour changes all at once.

@art dial
In y prime equals a plus y squared, the letter a is fixed for the whole solution, chosen before it begins. Each value of a gives an ordinary autonomous equation, with its own equilibria and its own phase line. So it's a whole family of equations.

@chapter Four values of a
@math heading="y′ = a + y²,   f′(y) = 2y" items="a = −4:   −2 stable,  2 unstable@minus four;;a = −1:   −1 stable,  1 unstable@minus one;;a = 0:   0 semistable@At zero;;a = 1:   none@at one"
The equilibria are where y squared equals minus a, and the slope in y is two y. At a equals minus four, they're minus two, stable, and two, unstable. At minus one, they're minus one and one. At zero, there's one, at zero, semistable. And at one, there are none.

@art closing-pair
As a rises, the pair closes up: plus or minus two, then plus or minus one, then one equilibrium at zero, then none. At a equals one, one plus y squared is positive for every y, so every solution rises.

@chapter Where they meet
@math heading="Solve both together" items="f′(y) = 2y = 0  ⟹  y = 0@two y equals zero;;f(0, a) = a = 0  ⟹  a = 0@at y equals zero"
Two roots coming together form a double root, where f and its slope in y are both zero. So solve both equations. Two y equals zero only at y equals zero. And at y equals zero the right side is a, which is zero only at a equals zero. So a equals zero is the one critical value.

@art touching-parabola
At a equals zero, the equation is y prime equals y squared, positive on both sides of zero. That's the semistable equilibrium, the last one before the pair disappears. It's one double root, and it counts once.

@chapter Another family
@math heading="y′ = y² − 2y + a" items="slope 2y − 2 = 0  at y = 1@two y minus two;;f(1, a) = a − 1 = 0  at a = 1@a minus one"
Take y squared minus two y plus a. The slope in y is two y minus two, zero at y equals one. There f is a minus one, zero when a equals one. So the critical value is one.

@art three-parabolas
Check both sides. At a equals zero, it's y times y minus two, two equilibria. At a equals one, y minus one squared, one. At a equals two, y minus one squared plus one, none.

@art harvest-family
You've run this test before. Harvesting used the family y times one minus y over four, minus H. The slope is one minus y over two, zero at two, and f of two is one minus H, zero at H equals one. That's the threshold that lesson found at the peak of the growth.

@chapter Small change, big difference
@math heading="y′ = a + y²,  y(0) = 0" items="a = −1/100:  equilibria ±1/10@minus one hundredth;;f(0) = −1/100 < 0  →  falls to −1/10@falls to"
Start at zero. At a equals minus one hundredth, the equilibria are plus or minus one tenth. The start lies between them, where f is negative, so the solution falls to minus one tenth and stays.

@words text="a = 1/100:  y′ ≥ 1/100" sub="no equilibria: rises without bound"
At a equals one hundredth there are no equilibria. y prime is at least one hundredth everywhere, so the solution rises without bound.

@art fork-in-road
Two values of a, one fiftieth apart, give a solution that settles and one that doesn't. And between minus one hundredth and zero, each equilibrium moved by a tenth while a moved by a hundredth, ten times as far. Near a critical value, a square root is steep.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Small change in a, small change in behaviour@The first|Finding the critical value from f = 0 alone@The second|Counting the double root as two, or none@The third"
The first slip is assuming a small change in a makes a small change in behaviour. The second is finding the critical value from f equals zero alone. At a equals minus four, two is an equilibrium, but it hasn't met anything, since its slope is four. The third is counting the equilibrium at the critical value as two, or none. It's one, and it's semistable.
