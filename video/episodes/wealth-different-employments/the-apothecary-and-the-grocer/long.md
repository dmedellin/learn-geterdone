@chapter The question
@title text="The Apothecary and the Grocer" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Smith says profits differ much less from trade to trade than wages do. Of the five circumstances, only two touch profit: how agreeable the business is, and how risky. So the ordinary rates of profit should be more nearly level than wages.

@art apothecary-jars
But there's an obvious objection. Apothecaries' profit was a byword for extravagance. The best employed apothecary in a market town might buy only thirty or forty pounds of drugs a year, and sell them for three or four hundred. That's a thousand per cent.

@chapter Wages in disguise
@quote text="The greater part of the apparent profit is real wages disguised in the garb of profit." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10, Part I"
Smith's answer: the greater part of the apparent profit is real wages disguised in the garb of profit. The apothecary's skill is delicate, he's the physician of the poor, and his only way to charge for it is on the price of his drugs.

@math heading="The apothecary" items="apparent:  440 − 40 = 400, or 1000%@four hundred;;his labour:  396@three hundred and ninety six;;left:  400 − 396 = 4, or 10%@four pounds;;wages' share:  396 / 400 = 99%@ninety nine"
In the lesson's figures, drugs cost forty and sell for four hundred and forty: an apparent profit of four hundred, a thousand per cent. Value his skilled labour at three hundred and ninety six a year, a figure the lesson chose. What's left is four pounds, ten per cent of the stock. Ninety nine per cent of it was wages.

@chapter The grocer
@art garb-of-profit
Smith gives the grocer's figures himself. In a small sea port, a little grocer makes forty or fifty per cent on a stock of a hundred pounds, while a wholesale merchant there scarce makes eight or ten per cent on ten thousand. But the grocer must read, write and account, and judge fifty or sixty sorts of goods.

@math heading="The village grocer" items="apparent:  150 − 100 = 50, or 50%@a hundred and fifty;;wages:  40@forty pounds;;left:  50 − 40 = 10, or 10%@ten pounds;;wages' share:  40 / 50 = 80%@eighty"
Thirty or forty pounds a year, Smith says, can't be too much for a person so accomplished. Take the upper figure. Stock a hundred, sales a hundred and fifty, an apparent fifty per cent. Take out forty pounds of wages, and ten pounds is left, ten per cent. Eighty per cent of it was wages.

@art village-and-city
In a city, it's different. Where ten thousand pounds can go into grocery, the grocer's wages are a trifling addition. With the lesson's figures, stock ten thousand and sales eleven thousand, ten per cent apparent. Take out the same forty pounds, and nine point six per cent is left.

@art share-pies
So wages are four per cent of the city grocer's apparent profit, against eighty for the village grocer. That's why groceries are generally as cheap or cheaper in the capital. And though the village grocer's rate may be high, the sum can never be great.

@chapter Three conditions
@list heading="Three conditions" items="Well known and long established@well known|In the ordinary or natural state@ordinary or natural|The sole or principal employment@sole or principal"
Smith says the circumstances level the whole advantages only under three conditions, even with perfect freedom. The trades must be well known and long established. They must be in their ordinary or natural state. And they must be the sole or principal employment of the people in them.

@art harvest-and-war
New trades pay higher wages, to entice workmen in. Hay time and harvest raise country wages, and war takes forty or fifty thousand sailors into the navy, so merchant seamen's pay rises from a guinea and twenty seven shillings to forty shillings and three pounds a month.

@art cottar-spinning
And the third: a Scottish cottar, with his house and his cow's grass, will work spare hours for less. Shetland stockings reach Leith at five to seven pence a pair, though common labour at Lerwick is tenpence a day.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading apparent profit as the profit of capital@The first|Assuming the wage figures are Smith's@The second|Taking the equality as a prediction for any market@The third"
The first slip is reading apparent profit as the profit of capital. The second is assuming the wage figures are Smith's. He gives the grocer's, not the apothecary's. The third is taking the equality as a prediction for any market, when Smith attaches three conditions.
