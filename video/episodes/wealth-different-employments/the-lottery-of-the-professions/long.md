@chapter The question
@title text="The Lottery of the Professions" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10" art=thumb
The fifth circumstance is the chance of success. In most mechanic trades, Smith says, success is almost certain. In the liberal professions, it's very uncertain.

@art shoemaker-bench
His example is a father choosing a trade for his son. Apprentice him to a shoemaker, and there's little doubt he'll learn to make a pair of shoes. But send him to study the law, and it's at least twenty to one against him ever living by it.

@chapter A fair lottery
@quote text="In a profession, where twenty fail for one that succeeds, that one ought to gain all that should have been gained by the unsuccessful twenty." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10, Part I"
That's a lottery, twenty blanks to one prize. In a profession, where twenty fail for one that succeeds, that one ought to gain all that should have been gained by the unsuccessful twenty.

@art twenty-to-one
So the counsellor who starts to earn at forty should be repaid for his own long, expensive education, and for that of more than twenty others who'll never make anything by it. Smith says that however extravagant lawyers' fees may look, their real reward never comes up to this.

@chapter Working the twenty to one
@math heading="21 enter, 1 succeeds" items="tickets:  21 · 200 = 4200@twenty one;;winner's years:  1 · 20 = 20@twenty years;;fair prize:  4200 / 20 = 210 a year@two hundred and ten;;check:  1 · 210 · 20 = 4200@check"
Here's the lesson's version. Twenty one young men enter, and one succeeds. Each education costs two hundred pounds, so the tickets cost four thousand two hundred. The winner works twenty years. To repay all the tickets, he must earn two hundred and ten a year above what common labour would give him. Check: two ten times twenty is four thousand two hundred.

@art tickets-vs-prizes
Pay him a hundred and twenty a year instead, and the prizes come to two thousand four hundred, eighteen hundred short. Spread over the twenty one, that's about a hundred and fourteen each, against two hundred each spent.

@words text="Shoemakers: 380 prizes · 360 tickets" sub="19 of 20 succeed"
Now a shoemaker's trade, also the lesson's. Nineteen of twenty apprentices succeed, at one pound a year over a labourer, for twenty years, against an education of eighteen each. The prizes, three hundred and eighty, exceed the tickets, three hundred and sixty. In the common trade, gains beat spending. In the law, they don't.

@chapter Why it's crowded
@list heading="Why the lottery is full" items="The desire of reputation@desire of reputation|Confidence in our own good fortune@natural confidence"
So why is the law full? First, the desire of reputation for excelling where few even reach mediocrity. Public admiration is part of the reward. Second, the natural confidence every man has in his own abilities, and still more in his own good fortune.

@art lottery-drum
Smith finds the same habit in the street. State lottery tickets aren't worth their price, yet sell at twenty, thirty, sometimes forty per cent above it.

@art uninsured-houses
And the chance of loss is undervalued. He reads it from the modest profits of insurers. Of houses, nineteen in twenty, or rather perhaps ninety nine in a hundred, aren't insured from fire.

@chapter Soldiers and sailors
@art sailor-ship
Young men enlist most readily at the start of a war. These romantic hopes, Smith says, make the whole price of their blood. A London sailor in peace earns a guinea to about twenty seven shillings a month, below a London labourer's forty to forty five.

@words text="Profit rises with the risk, but not in proportion"
Profit follows the same rule, a little weakened. It always rises with the risk, but not in proportion to it. The smuggler's trade, most profitable when a venture succeeds, is the infallible road to bankruptcy.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking Smith says professions should be rich@The first|Counting only the winners@The second|Calling it proof that gambling is irrational@The third"
The first slip is thinking Smith says the professions should be rich. He says what a fair reward would be, and that the real one falls short. The second is counting only the winners. Leave out the twenty failures, and the fair prize looks twenty one times too small. The third is calling it proof that gambling is irrational. Reputation is a real part of the prize.
