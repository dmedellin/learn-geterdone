@chapter The question
@title text="The Law of Settlements" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10, Part II" art=thumb
Two parishes a mile apart, and one pays half as much again as the other. If people are free to move, that gap should close. So why doesn't it? Smith's answer is the third way the policy of Europe unequalises employments: it stops labour moving.

@art two-looms
Picture a town with two manufactures, one thriving and one in decline. Plain linen and plain silk weaving are almost the same, and a weaver could become a tolerable workman at plain woollen in a very few days. Without the apprenticeship statute, wages would neither rise too high in the one nor sink too low in the other.

@words text="Easier for a wealthy merchant than for a poor artificer"
With the statute, weavers of a decaying trade can only become common labourers, and generally choose to come upon the parish. And corporation laws obstruct stock less than labour. It's much easier for a wealthy merchant to get the privilege of trading in a town than for a poor artificer to get leave to work there.

@chapter England's peculiarity
@art parish-boundary
Then the obstruction Smith calls peculiar to England, the poor laws. After the monasteries fell, a law of Elizabeth made every parish provide for its own poor. So who counted as a parish's poor became a question. Under Charles the Second, forty days' undisturbed residence gave a settlement. But within those days, two justices could remove any newcomer.

@words text="Rent a £10 tenement, or give security" sub="security: scarce less than £30"
Unless he rented a tenement of ten pounds a year, or gave security. Justices could hardly ask less than thirty pounds, which scarce any working man could give. Later statutes closed the loopholes. The forty days were counted from a notice read in church, and the other ways to a settlement were each shut to a man with nothing but his labour.

@art certificate-scroll
Certificates were meant to restore movement: a paper from the home parish, promising to take him back. But Smith's source, Dr Burn, says parishes had every reason to grant them seldom.

@chapter Two parishes
@art sea-and-mountains
Here's the claim. The very unequal price of labour in English places not far apart is probably owing to the law of settlements. In Scotland, wages may fall gradually with distance from a great town, but there are no sudden and unaccountable differences between neighbouring places.

@quote text="it is often more difficult for a poor man to pass the artificial boundary of a parish, than an arm of the sea, or a ridge of high mountains" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10, Part II"
In England, it is often more difficult for a poor man to pass the artificial boundary of a parish, than an arm of the sea, or a ridge of high mountains.

@math heading="312 days in each" items="parish A:  1 · 312 = 312@parish A;;parish B:  3/2 · 312 = 468@parish B;;spread:  468 − 312 = 156, or 1/2@a spread;;free:  5/4 · 312 = 390 in both@both at"
In the lesson's figures, in parish A, a labourer earns a shilling a day; in parish B, one and a half, both for three hundred and twelve days. Parish A gives three hundred and twelve, parish B four hundred and sixty eight. A spread of a hundred and fifty six, B above A by a half. Lift the barrier, with both at five quarters of a shilling, and the spread is nothing.

@art two-wage-bars
A man who could cross would gain a hundred and fifty six shillings a year. Smith's mechanism and the lab agree on what has to happen. What they leave open is how long it takes, and how many have to move.

@chapter Natural liberty
@words text="Most cruelly oppressed by this ill-contrived law"
There's scarce a poor man in England of forty, Smith ventures, who hasn't at some time felt most cruelly oppressed by this ill contrived law. To remove a man who's done no wrong from the parish where he chooses to live is an evident violation of natural liberty and justice.

@art tailors-cap
The chapter ends on laws that fix wages. One forbade London master tailors to pay more than two shillings and sevenpence halfpenny a day, except in general mourning. Whenever the legislature regulates masters and workmen, Smith says, its counsellors are always the masters.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading a wage gap as proof of a barrier@The first|Thinking Smith attacks poor relief itself@The second|Taking the last paragraph as a law of wage levels@The third"
The first slip is reading a wage gap as proof of a barrier. Travel costs, local demand and family ties could all keep it open. The second is thinking Smith attacks poor relief itself. The relief isn't his target; the settlement rules are. The third is taking the chapter's last paragraph as a law of wage levels. It's about proportions.
