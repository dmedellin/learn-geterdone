@chapter The question
@title text="The Five Circumstances" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Why does one trade pay twice as much as another, in the same town? Smith's answer starts with a claim that sounds like the opposite of the facts.

@quote text="and disadvantages of the different employments of labour and stock, must, in the same neighbourhood, be either perfectly equal, or continually tending to equality." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10"
The whole of the advantages and disadvantages of the different employments of labour and stock, must, in the same neighbourhood, be either perfectly equal, or continually tending to equality.

@art level-bars
Here's why. If one job were clearly better, so many people would crowd into it that its advantages would soon fall back to the level of the rest. That needs perfect liberty, everyone free to choose and change their trade. And Smith admits money wages in Europe are in fact extremely different. The equality is of the whole, not of the money.

@chapter Five circumstances
@art five-circumstances
So five circumstances make up for small money gains in some jobs and cancel large ones in others, either really, or at least in people's imagination. How agreeable the work is. How hard and costly it is to learn. How steady it is. How much trust it needs. And how likely you are to succeed.

@art dirty-work
First, how the work feels. A tailor earns less than a weaver, because his work is easier. A blacksmith seldom earns in twelve hours what a collier, only a labourer, earns in eight, because the collier's work is dirtier and more dangerous. Honour pays in a different coin. And the butcher's trade, brutal and odious, is more profitable than most.

@chapter Learning and steadiness
@art expensive-machine
Second, the cost of learning. A man trained at great expense to a skilled trade is like an expensive machine. The work he learns must replace the whole cost of his education, with at least the ordinary profit on that sum, and in a reasonable time, since a life is less certain than a machine's.

@art frost-wall
Third, steadiness. A mason can't work in hard frost or foul weather. So while he works he must earn enough to keep him while idle. Where labourers earn six shillings a week, masons often earn nine and ten. Not for skill: Smith says no skilled labour seems easier to learn. It's compensation for the inconstancy of their work.

@chapter Trust
@art trust-jewel
Fourth, trust. Goldsmiths and jewellers earn more than equally clever workmen, because of the precious materials they handle. We trust our health to the physician, our fortune and sometimes our life to the lawyer. That trust needs a rank, and the pay must give it. The fifth circumstance, success, gets the next lesson to itself.

@chapter Four trades, one advantage
@math heading="Net advantage a year, in shillings" items="labourer:  1 · 300 = 300@a labourer;;mason:  3/2 · 200 = 300@a mason;;collier:  2 · 300 − 300 = 300@a collier;;craftsman:  6/5 · 300 − 60 = 360 − 60 = 300@a craftsman"
The lab turns the list into a sum, with the lesson's figures. A labourer earns a shilling a day for three hundred days: three hundred. A mason, one and a half shillings for only two hundred days: three hundred. A collier, two shillings for three hundred days, six hundred, less three hundred for the dirt and danger. A craftsman, six fifths of a shilling: three sixty, less sixty for his learning.

@math heading="The craftsman's learning" items="400 / 20 = 20 a year@twenty working years;;10% of 400 = 40@ten per cent;;20 + 40 = 60@sixty a year"
Where does that sixty come from? He spent four hundred shillings learning, replaced over twenty working years: twenty a year. Plus ten per cent profit on the four hundred: forty. Sixty a year. Pay him only a shilling a day, and he nets two forty. Nobody would learn the trade until its wage rose.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading Smith as saying wages are equal@The first|Taking a high wage as the reward of skill@The second|Treating the equality as a law anywhere@The third"
The first slip is reading Smith as saying wages are equal. Look for the equality in the net advantage, not the wage. The second is taking a high wage as the reward of skill. The third is treating the equality as a law anywhere. It needs free movement. John Stuart Mill argued the hardest work is often the worst paid, because those who do it have no choice.
