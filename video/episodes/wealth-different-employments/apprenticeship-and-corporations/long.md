@chapter The question
@title text="Apprenticeship and Corporations" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10, Part II" art=thumb
Part one of chapter ten was about what nature does to wages. Part two is about what law does. The policy of Europe, Smith says, doesn't leave things at perfect liberty, and that causes inequalities of much greater importance.

@list heading="Three ways policy unequalises" items="Restraining competition@restraining the competition|Increasing it beyond nature@increasing it|Obstructing free movement@obstructing"
It works in three ways. By restraining the competition in some jobs to fewer than would enter. By increasing it in others beyond what it would naturally be. And by obstructing the free movement of labour and stock. This lesson takes the first two.

@chapter Fewer entrants
@art one-apprentice
The corporation restrains competition directly by limiting apprentices, and indirectly by a long term, which raises the cost of learning. In Sheffield, no master cutler could have more than one apprentice at a time. In Norfolk and Norwich, a master weaver no more than two, on pain of five pounds a month.

@art seven-years
Seven years was the old term across Europe, and the Statute of Apprenticeship made it law for all trades in England's market towns. Coachmakers couldn't make their own wheels, because wheelwrights were the older trade. Smith notes the old corporations were called universities, and the master of arts' seven years seems copied from apprenticeship.

@chapter The case against
@words text="The most sacred and inviolable" sub="the property every man has in his own labour"
Smith's argument against is about property. The property every man has in his own labour is the original foundation of all other property, and so the most sacred and inviolable. Stopping a poor man using his hands as he thinks fit is a plain violation of it. And whether he's fit to employ can be left to employers.

@words text="A few weeks · a few days"
A long apprenticeship doesn't secure good work; the sterling mark on plate does that. It doesn't teach industry either: an apprentice has no immediate interest in working. Clocks and watches, Smith says, need a few weeks' lessons, and common mechanic trades perhaps a few days.

@chapter What seven years add
@math heading="Learning over 20 years at 10%" items="few weeks, 20s:  20/20 + 2 = 3 a year → 1.01 a day@twenty shillings;;seven years:  700/20 + 70 = 105 a year@seven hundred;;wage:  300 + 105 = 405 → 405/300 = 27/20 a day@four hundred and five;;difference:  405 − 303 = 102 a year@a hundred and two"
In the lesson's figures, twenty shillings of learning, over twenty working years at ten per cent, charges three a year, so a wage of one point oh one a day matches a labourer. Seven years, with premium and keep, cost seven hundred, charging a hundred and five. The wage must be four hundred and five a year. That's a hundred and two shillings more, and the public pays it.

@art wage-gap-bars
Now cap entry instead, like Sheffield's one apprentice, and suppose the cap raises pay twenty per cent, a figure the lesson chose. The cutler's wage goes from one point oh one to about one point two one a day, and his net advantage from three hundred to three sixty point six, sixty point six above the labourer.

@chapter Town against country
@art town-and-country
Why do these rules exist? Each trade in a town is happy to let the others do the same. Within the town it's as broad as long. The gain is against the country. The town pays for its food and materials with its manufactures, and the dearer those sell, the cheaper the town buys. For one fortune made by farming, Smith says, at least a hundred are made in trade and manufactures.

@quote text="People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 10, Part II"
Then the famous sentence. People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices.

@art trade-meeting
But Smith doesn't propose banning meetings. No law could, consistent with liberty and justice. The law should just do nothing to make them easier, much less necessary.

@chapter More entrants
@art scholarship-church
The second way is the reverse: more competition, through bursaries and scholarships. Churchmen, mostly educated at public or pious expense, crowd the church, and the competition of the poor takes away the reward of the rich. Yet Smith thinks this is perhaps more good than harm, because it makes education cheap.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading Smith as saying all training is waste@The first|Taking the conspiracy line as a call to ban meetings@The second|Treating his account of guilds as the whole story@The third"
The first slip is reading Smith as saying all training is waste. He attacks a term set by law beyond what the trade needs. The second is taking the conspiracy line as a call to ban meetings. The third is treating his account of guilds as the whole story. Historians like S. R. Epstein argue guilds also trained workers and vouched for quality.
