@chapter Price's penny
@title text="Interest-Bearing Capital as a Thing" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 24" art=thumb
You've probably seen the savings advert with a curve that bends upward forever, or the calculator showing what a small sum becomes if you start young. Compound interest is real arithmetic. Marx asks what has to be true outside the arithmetic for it to be paid.

@quote text="A penny, loaned at the birth of our Savior at compound interest at 5%, would already have grown into a larger amount than would be contained in 150 million globes, all of solid gold." cite="Richard Price, quoted in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 24"
These are Richard Price's words, from a pamphlet of seventeen seventy two. A penny, loaned at the birth of our Savior at compound interest at five percent, would already have grown into a larger amount than would be contained in one hundred and fifty million globes, all of solid gold.

@art golden-globes
Price's arithmetic is correct. The question is who could ever pay it.

@chapter The pear tree
@art pear-tree
First, the appearance. Money is lent, and money returns, more of it, and nothing visible happens in between. The merchant at least bought and sold. The lender just waits. So the increase looks like a property of the money itself.

@quote text="It becomes a faculty of money to generate value and yield interest, just as it is a faculty of a pear tree to bear pears." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 24"
It becomes a faculty of money to generate value and yield interest, just as it is a faculty of a pear tree to bear pears. For Marx this is capital in its most fetish like form. Everything we've computed, industrial profit, the merchant's share, interest carved out of the average, has vanished from view.

@chapter Dazzled
@math heading="Price's formula" items="s = c · (1 + i)ⁿ@s equals"
Price took the pear tree literally. Marx says he was simply dazzled by the enormousness of figures arising from geometrical progression. He treated capital as a number increasing itself, by the formula s equals c times one plus i, to the power n.

@art sinking-fund
Pitt believed him. His sinking fund Act ordered two hundred and fifty thousand pounds to accumulate at compound interest until, with expired annuities, the fund reached four million pounds a year and paid off the national debt. Marx's comment is one line: what a pretty theoretical introduction to the national debt of England!

@chapter Thirty orders of magnitude
@math heading="A penny for 1,772 years" items="claim at 5%: 37 digits before the point@thirty seven digits;;grown with a product at 1%: 7 digits@seven digits"
Run Price's penny at five percent for seventeen hundred and seventy two years. The sum, in shillings, has thirty seven digits before the point. Grow the same penny at one percent a year, a rate an economy can sustain for a long time, and it has seven digits.

@art two-curves
The first figure is what the claim would be. The second is a generous guess at what a real product could pay.

@art penny-and-heap
The gap is thirty orders of magnitude.

@math heading="Child's £100 at 10% for 70 years" items="claim: £100 · (11/10)⁷⁰ ≈ £78,975@seventy eight thousand;;Child's figure: £100 · 2¹⁰ = £102,400@doubled ten times;;product at 2%: £100 · (51/50)⁷⁰ ≈ £400@two percent;;ratio: 78,975 / 400 ≈ 197@about two hundred"
Josiah Child said a hundred pounds at ten percent would make one hundred and two thousand four hundred in seventy years. Compounded yearly, it's about seventy eight thousand, nine hundred and seventy five. Child's figure is a hundred doubled ten times. A product growing at two percent reaches about four hundred. The claim is about two hundred times bigger.

@words text="£100 at 5% for 10 years, both ways" sub="≈ £162 17s 9½d"
On an ordinary horizon it's different. A hundred pounds at five percent for ten years, lent to a capital that also grows at five percent, gives the same sum both ways: about one hundred and sixty two pounds, seventeen shillings and nine pence halfpenny. Compound interest can be paid while the capital it's lent to grows as fast.

@chapter The limit
@quote text="By the identity of surplus-value with surplus-labor a qualitative limit is imposed upon the accumulation of capital." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 24"
That's Marx's answer. By the identity of surplus value with surplus labor, a qualitative limit is imposed upon the accumulation of capital.

@art working-day
The working day, the productivity of labour and the number of workers bound surplus value. Existing capital is also devalued as reproduction gets cheaper, and the rate of profit falls as accumulation goes on. Interest is a share of that surplus. Treated as mere interest, Marx says, it has only a limit that defies all fantasy.

@words text="Piketty, 2013: r > g" sub="a claim about shares"
The modern version is about shares, not impossibilities. Thomas Piketty argued that when the return on wealth exceeds growth for long periods, wealth's share of income rises. A share can grow only toward the whole product. How long return can outrun growth is disputed.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Money at interest grows by itself@The first|Compound interest arithmetic is wrong@The second|A sinking fund abolishes debt@And the third"
The first slip is thinking money at interest grows by itself. The second is thinking Price's arithmetic is wrong. It's right. What Marx denies is that it describes anything payable. And the third is reading Pitt's sinking fund as a way to abolish debt. The interest the fund earned was paid by the state, on its own debt.
