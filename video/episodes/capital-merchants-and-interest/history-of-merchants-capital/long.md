@chapter Older than capitalism
@title text="Historical Facts about Merchant's Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 20" art=thumb
Merchant's capital is older than capitalist production. For most of its history, its profit wasn't a share of any average at all. So where did it come from?

@quote text="The merchants' capital is at first merely the intervening movement between extremes not controlled by it and between premises not created by it." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 20"
Marx's chapter twenty starts here. The merchants' capital is at first merely the intervening movement between extremes not controlled by it and between premises not created by it.

@art between-extremes
The extremes are the producers at either end: a village that grows grain, a city that makes cloth, a court that collects tribute and wants silk. The merchant controls neither end. He only moves between them. And this, Marx says, is the oldest free existence of capital: M, C, M prime, buying in order to sell, long before anyone bought labour power to produce.

@chapter Profit upon alienation
@words text="“It is a profit upon alienation.”" sub="no average rate yet"
How does such a merchant profit? Not by sharing an average rate, because there isn't one. It is a profit upon alienation.

@art two-prices
The quantities in which different communities' goods exchange are at first arbitrary. The merchant alone sees both prices. So he buys where goods are cheap and sells where they're dear. To buy cheap in order to sell dear is the rule of trade. And by doing it again and again, he gradually pulls the prices together, and so establishes the equivalence of commodities.

@chapter Three circuits
@math heading="Same circuit, different limits" items="carrying trader: £100 − £60 = £40, rate 40/60 = 2/3@sixty;;monopoly at both ends: £100 − £25 = £75@twenty five;;capitalist merchant: £118 − £100 = £18, rate 18%@eighteen percent"
The lesson's figures are illustrative; Marx quotes none. A carrying trader buys a lot for sixty pounds and sells it for a hundred: forty pounds, two thirds of his outlay. A company with a monopoly at both ends buys for twenty five and sells for a hundred. And the capitalist merchant of Commercial Profit lays out a hundred and gets back a hundred and eighteen, eighteen percent.

@art three-loops
All three circuits have the same form, M, C, M prime. What changed isn't the form. It's what limits the increment. The old trader is limited only by the gap between prices and his power to exploit it. The capitalist merchant is held to the general rate by competition.

@chapter Robbery and an inverse ratio
@quote text="Merchants' capital in its supremacy everywhere stands for a system of robbery" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 20"
Marx doesn't romanticise the old merchant. Between undeveloped societies, profit comes largely from out bargaining and cheating. Merchants' capital in its supremacy everywhere stands for a system of robbery, tied to piracy, the slave trade and the conquest of colonies.

@quote text="The independent development of merchants' capital stands therefore in an inverse ratio to the general economic development of society." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 20"
Then the rule that organises the chapter. The independent development of merchants' capital stands therefore in an inverse ratio to the general economic development of society.

@art holland-ships
The carrying trade of the Venetians, Genoese and Dutch flourished on the backwardness of the peoples at either end, and declined as those peoples developed. For Marx, the decline of Holland as the ruling commercial nation is the history of merchant's capital being subordinated to industrial capital.

@chapter Did trade make capitalism?
@words text="Pirenne · Sweezy · Dobb · Brenner"
Historians have argued this for a century. Henri Pirenne said reviving trade rebuilt Europe's towns, and Paul Sweezy, debating Maurice Dobb, made trade the solvent of feudalism. Dobb, and later Robert Brenner, looked instead to relations inside agriculture.

@art world-market
Marx grants trade a great deal: the discoveries and the sudden world market of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries helped destroy feudal barriers. But trade doesn't decide what comes next. In antiquity it led to slave economies, in modern Europe to capitalism. And once modern industry exists, it's industry that continually revolutionises commerce.

@chapter Two roads
@art two-roads
Marx ends with two roads. The producer may become a merchant and capitalist. This, he says, is the really revolutionary way. Or the merchant may take over production, like the English clothier who sells wool to weavers and buys back their cloth, leaving the old methods in place and squeezing the weavers harder.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Every merchant's profit is a share of an average@The first|Trade by itself produced capitalism@The second|Old trade was an exchange of equivalents@And the third"
The first slip is reading an average rate back into all history. The second is thinking trade by itself produced capitalism. And the third is reading the old merchant's profit as an exchange of equivalents. Equivalence is where his trade ends up, not where it starts.
