@chapter A natural division
@title text="Interest and Profit of Enterprise" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 23" art=thumb
Think of someone who owns and runs a small business. Her accountant tells her to count a return on the money she's put in, what it would earn lent out, and to count the rest as the reward for running the firm.

@art owner-books
It seems the most natural division in the world. Interest is the fruit of owning capital, and profit of enterprise is the fruit of using it. Chapter twenty three asks where that division came from.

@chapter From quantity to quality
@art lender-and-borrower
It begins with borrowing. A capitalist working with borrowed money pays interest, and regards what's left as the product of his own activity. Interest goes to a lender who does nothing in production. The rest goes to the man who does. A split between two people becomes a split between two sources: ownership and function.

@quote text="Thus a merely quantitative division turns into a qualitative one." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 23"
Then the split spreads to everyone. Even a capitalist using his own money divides his profit into interest due to himself as owner, and profit of enterprise due to himself as active capitalist. Thus a merely quantitative division turns into a qualitative one.

@chapter A wage of superintendence?
@quote text="Under these circumstances his brain necessarily conceives the idea, that his profit of enterprise, far from being in opposition to wage-labor and representing only the unpaid labor of others, is rather itself wages of labor, wages of superintendence of labor." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 23"
Because profit of enterprise is set against interest, not against wages, the active capitalist sees it as the fruit of his work. His brain necessarily conceives the idea that it is itself wages of labor, wages of superintendence of labor.

@art one-surplus
What's forgotten, on Marx's account, is that interest and profit of enterprise are both parts of one surplus value, produced before any division.

@chapter Splitting the twenty
@math heading="£100 at 20% profit, 5% interest" items="borrower: £5 interest + £15 enterprise@the borrower;;owner: £5 to himself as owner + £15 as manager@the owner"
Take Marx's rates. On a hundred pounds at twenty percent, the profit is twenty. For the borrower, five goes in interest, and fifteen is profit of enterprise. The owner pays no interest out, but he reckons the same five to himself as owner and fifteen as manager. What differs is who gets the five, not what it is.

@chapter Hire the work
@art orchestra
Is profit of enterprise a wage, then? Marx's test is to hire the work. Coordinating combined labour is a real function, like that of a director of an orchestra, and any large business needs it. And under capitalist production, the labour of superintendence, entirely separated from ownership, walks the streets. It can be bought.

@math heading="A borrower and his manager" items="gross profit: £100 × 1/5 = £20@twenty pounds;;interest: £100 × 1/20 = £5@five pounds;;before the salary: £20 − £5 = £15@fifteen;;after the salary: £15 − £6 = £9@six pound manager"
So hire a manager. The lesson's salary is illustrative. Gross profit is twenty pounds, and interest five pounds, leaving fifteen. Now pay a six pound manager. Nine pounds are left. The managing is paid for, and nine pounds remain that, on Marx's account, pay for no work at all.

@art ex-manufacturers
Marx's evidence is from his day. After every crisis, ex manufacturers in the English factory districts superintend their own former factories, for low wages, for new owners who were often their creditors. Co-operative factories showed a profit after the manager's wages were paid.

@chapter The other side of supervision
@art overseer-tower
Supervision has a second side. Where production rests on the antagonism of owner and worker, supervision is needed to make people work, and it reaches its maximum under slavery. Marx quotes Aristotle, who thought commanding slaves best left to an overseer, and a New York lawyer of eighteen fifty nine who called slavery a just compensation to the master for ruling him.

@words text="Schumpeter, 1911 · Knight, 1921" sub="profit as innovation; profit as uncertainty"
Two later accounts didn't call profit a manager's wage. Schumpeter explained it as the reward of the innovating entrepreneur, and Knight as the return for bearing uninsurable uncertainty. The chapter sets aside a single capitalist's departures from average profit and says nothing of risk, so the lesson leaves that question open.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Profit of enterprise is a wage for managing@The first|Interest only exists when money is lent@The second|All supervision is productive coordination@And the third"
The first slip is thinking profit of enterprise is the capitalist's wage for managing. Hire the manager and something is still left. The second is thinking interest exists only when money is actually lent. And the third is treating all supervision as productive coordination.
