@chapter Two shops
@title text="The Turnover of Merchant's Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 18" art=thumb
You know two kinds of shop. One sells furniture: a few big sales a month, and stock that waits. The other sells groceries, and its stock is gone in days.

@art furniture-and-grocer
The furniture shop's margin on each item is large, and the grocer's is thin. It's easy to think the furniture dealer has simply decided to charge more. Marx's chapter eighteen says neither of them decided anything.

@words text="Faster turnover, more surplus value?" sub="for industry, yes; for the merchant, no"
For an industrial capital, turning over faster sets more labour in motion, so it produces more surplus value, and the general rate rises. A merchant's capital produces no surplus value. Its rate is the general rate, given from outside. What its turnover changes is the mark up on each sale.

@chapter Marx's figures
@quote text="If the yearly general rate of profit is 15%, and the merchant advances 100 p.st., which he turns over once a year, then he will sell his commodities at 115." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 18"
Here's how Marx puts it. If the yearly general rate of profit is fifteen percent, and the merchant advances one hundred pounds, which he turns over once a year, then he will sell his commodities at one hundred and fifteen.

@art five-loops
Now turn the same hundred over five times. Each time it buys a hundred of goods and sells them at one hundred and three. Five sales at three make fifteen. Marx's phrase: five hundred sold for five hundred and fifteen.

@math heading="100 at 15% a year" items="mark-up: 15% / 5 = 3% a turnover@fifteen percent over five;;each sale: 100 · (1 + 3/100) = 103@one hundred and three;;the year: 5 × 3 = 15 on 100 = 15%@five times three;;once a year: 100 · (1 + 15/100) = 115@turned over once"
So the mark up is fifteen percent over five, three percent a turnover. Each sale is one hundred times one plus three hundredths, one hundred and three. For the year, five times three is fifteen on a hundred, fifteen percent. Turned over once, the hundred sells at one hundred and fifteen. Same profit, same capital.

@words text="15% on every sale → 75 a year" sub="capital would rush in"
Why can't the fast merchant charge fifteen percent every time? He'd make seventy five a year on a hundred, and capital would rush into his line of trade until that stopped.

@chapter Sugar
@art sugar-sacks
Marx's sugar example comes from the price side. At one pound a pound of sugar, a hundred pounds buys a hundred pounds weight, and a merchant who turns over once adds three shillings to each pound to make his fifteen.

@math heading="Cheaper sugar, same profit" items="£1 a lb: £100 buys 100 lb, + 3s each@three shillings;;1s a lb: £100 buys 2,000 lb, + 1⅘d each@one and four fifths pence"
That's three shillings on each pound at first. If productivity cuts the price of production to one shilling, the same hundred pounds buys two thousand pounds weight, and the addition to each is one and four fifths pence. The profit for the year is unchanged.

@chapter Not the merchant's choice
@art two-dials
What fixes the mark up? The price of production, which the merchant doesn't control, and the general rate, which he doesn't control either. So Marx calls it ridiculous to think the merchant chooses between many sales at a small profit and few at a large one. His exception is the monopolist, like the Dutch East India Company.

@quote text="Small profits and quick returns appears particularly to the shopkeeper as a principle, which he follows on principle." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 18"
He enjoys the shopkeeper's motto. Small profits and quick returns appears particularly to the shopkeeper as a principle, which he follows on principle. The motto describes the result of a fast turnover, and the shopkeeper reads it as his policy.

@chapter Two qualifications
@art corner-shop
The law holds for the average turnover of a whole line of trade. A merchant who turns over faster than his line makes an extra profit, like an industrialist with a better machine. And if the advantage is something that can be bought, a good corner site, he may pay part of it away as rent.

@art wholesale-crisis
And merchants can run ahead of consumption, one selling to another on credit, until the connection is violently restored by a crisis. That's why, Marx says, crises break out first in wholesale trade and banking, not in the shops.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Faster turnover means a higher rate@The first|The merchant chooses his margin@The second|Carrying this law over to industry@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a faster turning merchant earns a higher rate of profit. Five turnovers bring three percent each, fifteen a year, just like one turnover at fifteen. The second is thinking the merchant chooses his margin. And the third is carrying this law over to industry, where a faster turnover really does raise surplus value.
