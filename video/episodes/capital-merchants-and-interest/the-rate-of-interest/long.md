@chapter The question
@title text="The Rate of Interest" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 22" art=thumb
Interest rates are news. A central bank raises or cuts its rate, mortgage payments follow, and economists argue over where the rate ought to be.

@art bread-and-rate
Behind the argument is an idea Marx rejects: that there's a right level the market rate tends toward, the way the price of bread tends toward its cost of production.

@quote text="From what has gone before it follows that there is no such thing as a ‘natural’ rate of interest." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 22"
From what has gone before it follows that there is no such thing as a natural rate of interest. The quotation marks around natural are the whole point.

@chapter A ceiling and no floor
@art ceiling-no-floor
Start with what can be said. Interest is a part of profit, so profit is its ceiling. At the ceiling, the borrower keeps nothing. Marx even allows exceptional cases where interest is larger than profit and can't be paid out of it.

@words text="“It may fall to any depth.”" sub="the minimum is wholly undefinable"
The floor is another matter. The minimum limit of interest, Marx writes, is wholly undefinable. It may fall to any depth.

@chapter Two things that vary
@math heading="Marx's figures on £100" items="20% profit, interest a quarter: 5%@a quarter is five;;16% profit, the same quarter: 4%@sixteen percent;;£20 − £8 = £12@eight percent;;£16 − £4 = £12@the same twelve"
Marx's own figures show the share and the rate moving. At twenty percent profit, interest at a quarter is five percent. At sixteen percent, the same quarter is four. But the quarter needn't hold. At twenty percent profit interest might rise to eight percent, and the borrower keeps twelve. At sixteen and four, he keeps the same twelve.

@art two-sliders
So what the borrower keeps depends on two things that vary separately: the rate of profit and the rate of interest. And nothing in the chapter says which division a given year will bring.

@chapter Three presets
@list heading="On a borrowed £100" items="Normal: 16% profit, 4% interest, £12 left@Normal|Plenty: 20% profit, 1½% interest, £18½ left@In plenty|Crisis: 5% profit, 8% interest, −£3@And in a crisis"
Normal: sixteen percent profit and four percent interest leaves twelve pounds. In plenty, twenty percent profit and one and a half percent interest, the rate Marx reports for September eighteen forty three, leaves eighteen and a half. And in a crisis, five percent profit against eight percent interest leaves the borrower three pounds down.

@art trade-cycle
That last one pairs Marx's rate for eighteen forty seven with an illustrative profit. It's not an error. Low interest goes with prosperity, a rise with the turn, and a maximum, up to a point of extreme usury, with the crisis, when money must be borrowed to meet payments at any cost.

@chapter Why no law?
@art one-pie-two-hands
For prices, Marx has a law. When supply and demand balance, a commodity sells at its price of production, a level set by production itself. For interest, a balance between lenders and borrowers reveals nothing, because both sides are dividing one profit that the same capital produced once.

@quote text="Custom, legal tradition, etc., have as much to do with the determination of the average rate of interest as competition itself" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 22"
Joseph Massie, who first saw that interest is part of profit, said the division is settled by common consent. Marx adds: custom, legal tradition, etc., have as much to do with the determination of the average rate of interest as competition itself.

@chapter Fixed-looking, lawless
@art barometer
And yet the rate of interest looks far more solid than the rate of profit. It's quoted daily, the same for every borrower of the same standing. Marx says the reports of the Bourse register it as accurately as a barometer. Beside it, the general rate of profit appears as a vanishing shape of mist. The thing without a law looks fixed.

@words text="Wicksell, 1898: a natural rate" sub="where loans demanded match savings supplied"
The opposing view has a name. Knut Wicksell, in eighteen ninety eight, defined a natural rate: where the demand for loans matches the supply of savings. Central banks estimate a neutral rate in that spirit. Marx's argument is that nothing in the division of profit fixes such a level. The lesson names that dispute and doesn't settle it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A natural rate set by productivity@The first|A breach of the ceiling is impossible@The second|Profit is the more visible rate@And the third"
The first slip is thinking there's a natural rate set by productivity. The yield fixes only the ceiling. The second is treating a breach of the ceiling as impossible. And the third is thinking the rate of profit is the more visible of the two.
