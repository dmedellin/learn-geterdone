@chapter The question
@title text="Commercial Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 16" art=thumb
Volume Three has been about capital in production: cost price, the rate of profit, and how profit averages out across industries. Part Four turns to people who never produce anything and still own capital. The first is the merchant.

@art mill-and-shop
Economists, Marx complains, file trading capital under industrial capital and miss what's peculiar to it. So what is a merchant, in a world of mills and factories? His answer fits in one sentence.

@quote text="Commercial capital is nothing else but a changed form of a portion of this capital of circulation, which exists continually on the market in the process of its metamorphoses within the sphere of circulation." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 16"
Commercial capital is nothing else but a changed form of a portion of this capital of circulation, which exists continually on the market in the process of its metamorphoses within the sphere of circulation.

@chapter A phrase at a time
@list heading="Reading the sentence" items="this capital of circulation@capital of circulation|a portion@a portion|a changed form@a changed form"
Take it a phrase at a time. The capital of circulation is the part of society's capital that's always on the market, as goods waiting to be sold or money waiting to buy. A portion, because manufacturers also trade with each other, and Marx sets that aside. And a changed form is the claim itself. The merchant's capital is the mill's selling, done by someone else.

@chapter The linen merchant
@art linen-handover
Marx's example is a merchant with three thousand pounds, who buys thirty thousand yards of linen at two shillings a yard. The manufacturer has his money and buys yarn, coal and labour. For him, the sale is over.

@words text="Nothing has happened to the linen" sub="only a change in the person of its owner"
For the linen, it isn't. It's still on the market, waiting for its final buyer. Nothing has happened to it but a change in the person of its owner. And if the merchant can't sell before the next lot is ready, he can't buy it, and the mill stalls just as if it had kept the linen itself.

@quote text="There seems then to have been no other change but that of the persons who hold this capital in their hands." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 16"
Marx's dry summary: there seems then to have been no other change but that of the persons who hold this capital in their hands.

@chapter Merchant, not clerk
@art two-circuits
So what makes the merchant a capitalist rather than a clerk? Two things. Selling becomes the exclusive business of a separate agent, and that agent advances money capital, his own or borrowed. A salesman on commission has the first and not the second.

@math heading="What changes hands twice" items="manufacturer: C – M – C@the manufacturer's;;merchant: M – C – M′@the merchant's"
Notice what changes hands twice. In the manufacturer's circuit, C, M, C, it's the money: received for linen, spent on yarn. In the merchant's circuit, M, C, M prime, it's the same linen, sold to him and sold by him. That second sale is how his money comes back.

@chapter Counting the turnover
@art four-mills
How much commodity capital does a merchant take off the manufacturers' hands in a year? It's his advance times the number of times he turns it over. Suppose he sells each lot before its maker can finish the next, and buys from four manufacturers in turn.

@math heading="One linen merchant" items="trade a year: £3,000 × 4 = £12,000@three thousand pounds times four;;turned once: £12,000 / 1 = £12,000 needed@turned over once;;turned four times: £12,000 / 4 = £3,000 needed@four times needs"
Three thousand pounds times four is twelve thousand pounds of linen a year. A merchant who turned over once would need twelve thousand pounds for that trade. Turning over four times needs only three thousand. The quicker merchant does it with a quarter of the capital.

@art smaller-reserve
That's the case for the merchant, and Marx makes it himself. His capital is smaller than the reserves the manufacturers would otherwise need, and one merchant can serve many producers, even in different trades. The cost is that a slice of social capital now sits permanently in circulation, doing nothing else.

@chapter No value created
@quote text="Merchant's capital, therefore, does not create any value or surplus-value, at least not directly." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 16"
And here's the verdict. Merchant's capital does not create any value or surplus value, at least not directly. No value is produced in circulation, only changes of form of the same values.

@art quicker-bus
But at least not directly is a real qualification. By shortening the time goods sit unsold, the merchant lets more industrial capital stay in production, and on Marx's account that raises the surplus value and the rate of profit. Think of a quicker bus to work. It makes nothing at the office, but you get more hours there.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Merchants produce value by passing goods on@The first|Selling confused with transport and storage@The second|Bigger merchant's capital means more trade@And the third"
The first slip is thinking merchants produce value by passing goods from hand to hand. The second is confusing selling with transport and storage, which Volume Two counted as production continued in circulation. And the third is thinking a bigger merchant's capital means more trade. Three thousand pounds turned four times does what twelve thousand does turned once.

@chapter Try it
@words text="£24,000 a year, 6 turnovers" sub="capital needed = trade ÷ turnovers"
