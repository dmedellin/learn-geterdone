@chapter The job
@title text="Money-Dealing Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 19" art=thumb
Every business pays out and takes in money, keeps a reserve and, if it trades abroad, changes coin. Chapter nineteen watches those technical jobs become the business of a separate capital: the money dealer's.

@words text="Money-dealing, not lending" sub="Untermann's title: “financial capital”"
A word on the name. Untermann's translation calls this financial capital. Today finance suggests lending and investment banking, which is exactly what Marx leaves out here. Lending at interest comes in the next Part. This chapter is about the purely technical movements performed by money.

@art payroll-office
Think of the payroll office of a large firm, or the payment processor behind a card. Money comes in from many people and goes out to many others. Someone must count it, keep it, balance the accounts, and hold enough in reserve for the days when more goes out than comes in.

@quote text="This labor belongs to the expenses of circulation, it does not create any values. It is abbreviated by being organised as a special department of agents, or capitalists, who perform this work for all the rest of the capitalist class." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 19"
This labor belongs to the expenses of circulation, it does not create any values. It is abbreviated by being organised as a special department of agents, or capitalists, who perform this work for all the rest of the capitalist class. Abbreviated is the right word. The dealer doesn't abolish the work. He makes it shorter.

@chapter Where it started
@art coin-exchange
Money changing came out of international trade, when merchants had to swap one country's coin for another's, or for bullion. Out of it grew the banks of exchange. Remittances between money changers in different countries go back to Greece and Rome. And in England, the goldsmiths still served as bankers for most of the seventeenth century.

@art pooled-reserve
The other root is the hoard every business keeps as a reserve. Managed for the whole class, Marx notes, the reserves need not be so large as they would have to be, did each capitalist manage his own. One cashier's fund serves many businesses whose payments partly cancel.

@chapter Where the profit comes from
@quote text="It is equally evident, that the profit of the money dealers is nothing but a deduction from the surplus-value, since they are operating merely with already realised values" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 19"
A capital put into this business must earn the general rate, or nobody would put it there. Marx is brief. The profit of the money dealers is nothing but a deduction from the surplus value, since they are operating merely with already realised values.

@chapter A cashier in the averaging
@art capitals-share
So put a money dealer into the averaging from Commercial Profit. Marx gives no figures here, so the lesson's are illustrative. Industry is nine hundred, producing one hundred and eighty of surplus value, and the merchant holds one hundred. Now a cashier adds one hundred and twenty five.

@math heading="A cashier among Marx's capitals" items="rate: 180 / (900 + 100 + 125) = 4/25 = 16%@sixteen percent;;manufacturers: 16% × 900 = 144@one hundred and forty four;;merchant: 16% × 100 = 16@the merchant;;cashier: 16% × 125 = 20@the cashier;;check: 144 + 16 + 20 = 180@add up"
One hundred and eighty over eleven hundred and twenty five is four twenty fifths, sixteen percent. The manufacturers draw one hundred and forty four, the merchant sixteen, and the cashier twenty. They add up to one hundred and eighty.

@words text="Before: 162 and 18 · After: 144, 16 and 20" sub="the goods still sell at £1,080"
Without the cashier, the manufacturers took one hundred and sixty two and the merchant eighteen. The cashier's twenty is their loss. The manufacturers now sell at one thousand and forty four, and the goods still reach their final buyers at one thousand and eighty, their value.

@words text="Add a money-changer's 75" sub="180 / 1,200 = 15%"
Add a money changer's seventy five too, and one hundred and eighty over twelve hundred is fifteen percent. The two dealers draw fifteen percent of two hundred, thirty, between them. Each new capital in circulation takes its share from what the others would have kept.

@chapter No special circuit
@art ledger-balance
Is the money dealer's circuit special? Marx says no. The merchant has M, C, M prime, with the same commodity changing hands twice. The money dealer has only the general form: money advanced and returned with an increment.

@art clearing-house
He shortens the work of payment, but doesn't decide how much is paid. The bills that cancel in a clearing house reflect trade that happened anyway.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Handling value creates value@The first|Financial capital means lending@The second|The dealer decides how much is paid@And the third"
The first slip is thinking money dealing creates value because it handles value. The second is reading financial capital as lending capital. And the third is thinking the dealer decides how much is paid. The volume of payments is set by the trade in commodities.
