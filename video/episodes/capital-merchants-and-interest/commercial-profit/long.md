@chapter The puzzle
@title text="Commercial Profit" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 17" art=thumb
Think of any shop. It buys a jacket for one price and sells it for more, and the difference pays the shop. That's the merchant's profit as everyone sees it.

@art jacket-markup
Marx agrees the profit can only come from there, the excess of the selling price over the purchase price. But the last lesson said merchants create no value. So is that excess a price raised above value? If it were, every commodity would sell above its value.

@quote text="It is only outward semblance that commercial profit is a mere addition to, a nominal raise of the prices of commodities above their value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 17"
Marx's answer: it is only outward semblance that commercial profit is a mere addition to, a nominal raise of the prices of commodities above their value.

@chapter The hidden assumption
@art rate-reworked
Why only a semblance? The mark up story assumes the manufacturer sold to the merchant at the full price of production. That price came from Part Two, where the general rate of profit was worked out before merchant's capital was in the picture. Bring the merchant in, and the rate has to be worked out again.

@chapter Marx's 900 and 100
@art industry-block
Here are Marx's figures. Society's industrial capital is seven hundred and twenty constant plus one hundred and eighty variable. With a rate of surplus value of one hundred percent, the product is worth one thousand and eighty, and one hundred and eighty of that is surplus value. On industrial capital alone, that's twenty percent.

@art merchant-joins
Now add one hundred of merchant's capital. Capital moves freely into and out of trade, so the merchant must earn the same rate as anyone else. The one hundred and eighty of surplus value is spread over a thousand.

@math heading="The general rate with the merchant" items="rate: 180 / (900 + 100) = 9/50 = 18%@eighteen percent;;manufacturer: 900 + 18% × 900 = 1,062@one thousand and sixty two;;merchant: 1,062 + 18% × 100 = 1,080@one thousand and eighty;;value: 720 + 180 + 180 = 1,080@the value"
One hundred and eighty over a thousand is eighteen percent. The manufacturers sell at their nine hundred plus eighteen percent, which is one thousand and sixty two. The merchant adds eighteen percent of his hundred and sells at one thousand and eighty. And that's exactly the value of the goods.

@words text="The purchase price stands below value"
So the merchant doesn't sell above value. He sells at value, because he bought below it. His eighteen is exactly what the manufacturers gave up: at twenty percent they'd have taken one hundred and eighty, and now they take one hundred and sixty two.

@words text="More merchant's capital → lower industrial rate"
Marx draws a corollary. The larger the merchant's capital in proportion to the industrial, the lower the rate of industrial profit, because the same surplus value is averaged over more capital.

@chapter The merchant's costs
@art office-costs
Then the merchant's own costs: offices, paper, postage and clerks. Marx adds fifty of them to the merchant's hundred. Now the surplus value is spread over nine hundred plus one hundred and fifty, which is one thousand and fifty.

@math heading="With £50 of costs" items="rate: 180 / 1,050 = 17 1/7%@seventeen and a seventh;;manufacturer: 900 + 154 2/7 = 1,054 2/7@one thousand and fifty four;;merchant: 1,080 + 50 = 1,130@one thousand one hundred and thirty"
The rate falls to seventeen and a seventh percent. The manufacturers sell at nine hundred plus one hundred and fifty four and two sevenths, which is one thousand and fifty four and two sevenths. The merchant sells at one thousand one hundred and thirty: the value plus the fifty he must recover. The price rises; the value doesn't.

@quote text="The unpaid labor of his clerks, while it does not create any surplus-value, at least appropriates surplus-value for him" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 17"
And the clerks? Their work realises value and creates none, yet the merchant profits by it, because the more they buy and sell, the more capital he can turn over. The unpaid labor of his clerks, while it does not create any surplus value, at least appropriates surplus value for him.

@chapter The other side
@art service-counter
The standard objection is that a retailer sells a service, putting goods where and when buyers want them, and the margin is the price of that service. Marx's figures also rest on Part Two's averaging, where the transformation problem enters. The lesson computes his accounting and leaves both disputes open.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Profit as a mark-up on value@The first|The rate on industrial capital alone@The second|Costs add value because they raise price@And the third"
The first slip is reading the merchant's profit as a mark up on top of value. The second is reckoning the general rate on industrial capital alone, as Part Two did. And the third is thinking the merchant's costs add value because they raise the price.
