@chapter The price of money?
@title text="Interest-Bearing Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 21" art=thumb
You've met interest from both sides: on a savings account, where it's paid to you, and on a loan or a mortgage, where you pay it. Newspapers call the rate the price of money. Marx's chapter argues that this everyday phrase hides what's being bought.

@chapter A power to make profit
@art power-of-money
Start with a man holding a hundred pounds. Where labour power and means of production can be bought, that hundred can be turned into capital, and capital makes the average profit. At twenty percent, a hundred becomes a hundred and twenty. That power is what makes the sum worth borrowing.

@words text="“capital as capital becomes a commodity.”"
So, in Marx's phrase, capital as capital becomes a commodity. The lender doesn't hand over money as money. He hands over money as capital, a sum that can make the average profit in someone else's hands.

@quote text="That part of the profit, which he pays to the owner, is called interest." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 21"
If the borrower pays five pounds at the end of the year out of the profit, he's paying for the use value of the hundred pounds as capital. That part of the profit, which he pays to the owner, is called interest.

@chapter Splitting the twenty
@art profit-split
So interest isn't a second source of income beside profit. It's a slice of the twenty pounds. Let's cut it.

@math heading="Marx's lender and borrower" items="profit: £100 × 1/5 = £20@twenty pounds;;interest: £100 × 1/20 = £5@five pounds;;kept by B: £20 − £5 = £15@fifteen;;interest's share: 5 / 20 = 1/4 of the profit@a quarter;;returned to A: £100 + £5 = £105@one hundred and five"
A hundred at one fifth makes twenty pounds. Interest at one twentieth is five pounds. The borrower keeps fifteen. Interest is a quarter of the profit, and the lender gets back one hundred and five.

@words text="At 10%: £10 and £10 · At 2%: £2 and £18"
Raise the rate of interest to ten percent, and the twenty splits ten and ten. Drop it to two percent, and it's two and eighteen. The twenty never moves. The rate of interest decides only who gets how much of it.

@chapter Follow the money
@math heading="The movement" items="M — M — C — M′ — M′@The movement"
Marx follows the money. The movement, then, is M, M, C, M prime, M prime.

@art lender-borrower
The lender hands the money to the borrower. The borrower buys means of production and labour power, sells the product, and gets back more. Then he hands the sum back, with the interest.

@art doubled-ends
The doubled letters at each end are the point. The first hand over isn't a purchase or a sale. No equivalent comes back, and the lender stays the owner. The last is a repayment. Between them, the ordinary circuit of capital runs in the borrower's business, invisible from where the lender stands.

@chapter Proudhon's hatter
@art hatter
Marx argues here with Proudhon, who thought lending unjust because it isn't selling: the lender sells the same thing over and over without giving it up. The hatter gets the value of his hats and no more, Proudhon said. Marx replies that the hatter gets more too. Hats that cost a hundred to make sell at their value of a hundred and fifteen.

@quote text="This does not alter anything in the value of the hats, but only in the distribution of the surplus-value already contained in this value between different persons." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 21"
If his capital was borrowed, he may give up five of the fifteen as interest. This does not alter anything in the value of the hats, but only in the distribution of the surplus value already contained in this value between different persons.

@quote text="The profit is doubled by the double existence of the same sum of money as a capital for two persons. It can serve as a capital for both of them only by dividing the profit." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 21"
Why can the profit be divided at all? Because the same hundred is capital twice, and makes profit only once. It can serve as a capital for both of them only by dividing the profit.

@chapter An irrational expression
@art two-figures
So is interest the price of money? A price is a thing's value expressed in money. But the hundred lent comes back as the same hundred. The interest is a second figure beside it, measuring how much profit it yields. Marx calls the price of capital an irrational expression.

@words text="Böhm-Bawerk · Fisher" sub="interest as the price of time"
An opposing account came later. Boehm Bawerk in the eighteen eighties, and Irving Fisher in nineteen thirty, explained interest as a genuine price: people value goods now more than later, so borrowers pay a premium for present money, whatever the social system. The lesson computes Marx's share and can't decide between them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Interest is the price of money@The first|The lender's money produces the interest@The second|Interest raises the value of the goods@And the third"
The first slip is calling interest the price of money. The second is thinking the lender's money produces the interest. It's its use in the borrower's business that does. And the third is Proudhon's: thinking interest raises the value of what the borrower sells.
