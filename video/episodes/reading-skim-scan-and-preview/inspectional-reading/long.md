@chapter The question
@title text="Inspectional Reading" sub="Reading Faster · Skimming, Scanning and Previewing" art=thumb
Before you read a book, there's a question worth five minutes: does it need reading, and how? You can answer it from pages most readers turn straight past.

@art four-levels
Adler and Van Doren's How to Read a Book, in its nineteen seventy two revision, names four levels of reading: elementary, inspectional, analytical and syntopical. It puts inspection before any careful reading. Its first part, which they call systematic skimming, is what this drill does.

@chapter The drill
@art book-matter
The drill is the front and back matter of a book that doesn't exist. The Patient Orchard, a grower's history of the apple: its title page, contents, preface and part of its index, five hundred and eighty six words in all.

@words text="5 questions · 300 seconds" sub="logged as an inspection, not a reading"
A prompt asks a question, like which chapter deals with the insects that attack apple trees, and you click the word that answers it. There are five questions, and the document disappears after five minutes, three hundred seconds, finished or not. You can skip a question you can't answer, and the result is logged as an inspection, not a reading.

@chapter Questions about the book
@list heading="What an inspection asks" items="Which edition is it?@which edition|How is it divided?@how it's divided|Who is it for?@who it's written for|Where does a topic sit?@where a topic sits"
The questions aren't about apples. They're about the book: which edition it is, how it's divided, who it's written for, and where a topic sits in it. Those are the questions an inspection answers.

@art index-lookup
The title and subtitle say what the book claims to be. The contents give its plan. The index says where a topic is treated, and how many pages it gets.

@chapter The preface
@art preface-signpost
And the preface often says who a book is for, and which parts to read first. It's the part a hurried reader skips, and it answers the most. This one says the book is written mainly for growers with a small garden, and that the chapter on pests was largely rewritten for this edition.

@words text="Begin with chapters 4 and 5, and the calendar"
It also says that a reader who wants only practical advice can begin with the fourth and fifth chapters and the calendar. That's the best thing an inspection can find: a reason not to start on page one.

@chapter The figures
@math heading="Seconds per target found" items="five-minutes: 300 / 4 = 75 s@three hundred over four;;thorough: 210 / 5 = 42 s@two hundred and ten over five"
The five minutes preset found four of the five answers before the limit ran out. Three hundred over four is seventy five seconds per answer found. The thorough preset read the whole contents and preface at the first prompt, and found all five in two hundred and ten seconds. Two hundred and ten over five is forty two seconds an answer.

@art timer-five
It's the same arithmetic as scanning, but it means something a little different, because these targets need a moment's thought about where a book would put a thing. The panel also prints the words passed over per second, but that's beside the point here. An inspection isn't trying to cover the text.

@chapter A decision, not a summary
@art decision-three
What an inspection produces is a decision. Read the book, read some of its chapters, or put it back. Five minutes on a book you then decide not to read is the cheapest reading you'll do. On a real book, also dip into a few pages and read the last pages of the main text, and keep the limit.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading a book means starting at page one@The first|Skipping the preface@The second|Inspecting without questions@And the third"
The first slip is taking reading a book to mean starting at page one. The second is skipping the preface. And the third is inspecting without questions. Five minutes of browsing leaves an impression. Five minutes with questions leaves a decision.

@chapter Try it
@words text="3 of 5 answers in 240 s" sub="seconds per target found?"
Your turn. An inspection finds three of five answers in two hundred and forty seconds. What's the time per target found? Two forty over three, eighty seconds, not forty eight. Then pick a real book, write three questions, and give yourself five minutes.
