@chapter The question
@title text="Scanning for a Target" sub="Reading Faster · Skimming, Scanning and Previewing" art=thumb
You need the first Sunday ferry. You don't read the timetable. You hunt. That hunt is called scanning, and it's a search for something whose form you already know: a time, a price, a name.

@art known-form
Before you look, you know what the answer will look like. A time has a colon in it. A fare comes after a pound sign. A name starts with a capital. Your eye matches shapes, and it can pass over everything that doesn't have that shape.

@chapter The drill
@art timetable-search
The drill is a ferry company's summer timetable: six hundred and thirteen words of sailing times, fares, check in rules and exceptions, with prose between the tables. A prompt asks for one thing, and you click the word that answers it. A wrong click is counted, and you keep looking. Five prompts, one clock.

@words text="Logged as a scan test" sub="never on your rate curve"
The result is logged as a scan test. It never enters your rate curve, because a scan is not a reading. And on a phone, scrolling the page is part of the search, so the clock includes it.

@chapter Two figures
@list heading="Two figures" items="Seconds per target found@seconds per target found|Words passed over per second@words passed over per second"
When the search ends, you get two figures. Seconds per target found says what each answer cost you. Words passed over per second says how much text went by while you looked. Neither says how much you understood, because you weren't trying to.

@math heading="Quick preset: 5 of 5 in 40 s" items="40 / 5 = 8 s per target@forty over five;;613 / 40 ≈ 15.3 words a second@six hundred and thirteen over forty;;613 × 60 / 40 = 919.5 words a minute@times sixty"
Take a reader who found all five in forty seconds. Forty over five is eight seconds a target. Six hundred and thirteen over forty is about fifteen point three words a second. And times sixty, that's nine hundred and nineteen point five words a minute.

@chapter Divide by what you found
@art stopwatch-targets
Notice the divisor. It's the targets you found, not the ones you were asked for. Time spent hunting for a target you never found was still spent, and it belongs to the answers you did get.

@math heading="Slow preset: 4 of 5 in 95 s" items="✗  95 / 5 = 19 s@ninety five over five;;95 / 4 = 23.75 s per target found@ninety five over four;;≈ 6.5 words a second@about six point five"
The slow preset found four in ninety five seconds, with one wrong click and one target skipped. Ninety five over five, nineteen seconds, would understate the cost. Ninety five over four, twenty three point seven five seconds, is the true figure. It passed over about six point five words a second.

@chapter Not a reading rate
@words text="919.5 words a minute ≠ a reading rate" sub="Brysbaert (2019): 238 wpm, silent reading of non-fiction"
Nine hundred and twenty words a minute is nearly four times Brysbaert's twenty nineteen estimate of two hundred and thirty eight for silent reading of non fiction. And it isn't a reading rate at all. Nobody who scanned that timetable could tell you what the other six hundred words said.

@art gears
Carver, in nineteen ninety, counted scanning as the fastest of five gears: about six hundred standard length words a minute for the college students he tested, against about three hundred for ordinary reading. What sets it apart is its goal, finding a target rather than understanding the passage.

@chapter How to search faster
@art jump-vs-line
What makes one search faster than another is mostly where you look. Read the headings first, and decide which section a target lives in. Hold the form of the answer in mind, and let your eyes jump from block to block instead of travelling along the lines.

@list heading="A better search" items="Headings first@headings first|Hold the answer's form@hold its form|Find the shape, read one sentence@find its shape"
When a target sits inside a paragraph, like the vessel out of service for inspection, don't start reading every line. Go headings first, hold its form, find its shape, then read only the sentence around it.

@chapter Only the first run counts
@art second-visit
A search of a page you've just searched is quicker, because you remember where things are. So only your first run measures scanning. That's why a second visit to the drill serves a different document: a museum's visitor guide, with five targets of its own.

@words text="Compare a scan with a scan" sub="of a similar document, never with a reading"
A scan's figures depend heavily on the layout, and on how well the targets match the headings. So compare a scan only with a scan of a similar document, never with a reading.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking scanning for fast reading@The first|Dividing by the targets asked@The second|Reading line by line in prose@And the third"
The first slip is taking scanning for fast reading. The second is dividing by the targets asked, instead of the ones found. And the third is reading line by line to find a target in prose, when the shape of the answer is still your guide.

@chapter Try it
@words text="3 of 5 found · 600 words · 60 s" sub="seconds per target found?"
Your turn. A reader finds three of five targets in a six hundred word notice in sixty seconds, and skips the other two. What's the time per target found? Sixty over three: twenty seconds, not twelve. Then try it on a document of your own, with five targets and one clock.
