@chapter The question
@title text="Skimming for Gist" sub="Reading Faster · Skimming, Scanning and Previewing" art=thumb
How much of a passage does a skim actually show you? And how much of its point does that carry? This lesson puts a number on both.

@art headings-first-lines
The drill is a passage about glaciers. For forty seconds you see only the headings and the first sentence of each paragraph. Then the display ends by itself, and four questions ask about the gist. It's logged as a skim test, and a skim never enters your rate curve.

@chapter What you saw
@art quarter-shown
Look at your score before anything else, because whatever it was, it came from under a quarter of the text. The skim showed one hundred and forty one of the passage's five hundred and ninety four words. The rest was never on the screen.

@math heading="First-sentences preset" items="141 / 594 = 47/198 ≈ 23.7% shown@one hundred and forty one over;;3 of 4 gist questions right@three of the four;;3 × 60 / 40 = 4.5 a minute@times sixty"
One hundred and forty one over five hundred and ninety four is forty seven over one ninety eight, about twenty three point seven percent. A reader who got three of the four right has a gist rate of three times sixty, over forty: four point five gist answers a minute.

@chapter More right, fewer per minute
@art two-clocks
The slower skim preset gives a reader twice as long, eighty seconds, and they get all four. Four times sixty over eighty is three a minute. More right, fewer per minute.

@words text="It depends what the skim was for"
So which is better? If you only need to know whether the passage is worth reading, the forty second skim told you nearly as much in half the time. If one wrong idea would mislead you, the extra forty seconds bought the fourth answer.

@chapter No effective rate
@math heading="What the panel won't print" items="594 × 60 / 40 = 891 words a minute@eight hundred and ninety one;;× 3/4 ≈ 668 effective@six hundred and sixty eight"
Notice what the panel doesn't print. Five hundred and ninety four words in forty seconds would be eight hundred and ninety one words a minute. Times three in four, that's an effective rate of about six hundred and sixty eight, earned on words that were never shown.

@art map-country
A skim covers a passage the way a map covers a country. So it's scored on what it was for: the gist, per minute spent.

@chapter A bet on structure
@quote text="A glacier begins wherever more snow falls in winter than melts in summer" cite="the glacier passage, its first section in a sentence"
A skim is a bet that the writer put the point of each paragraph first. Exposition written to a plan usually does, and this passage mostly obliges. A glacier begins wherever more snow falls in winter than melts in summer.

@art boulder-beach
But look at the section headed Stones far from home. Its first sentence sets a scene: a boulder the size of a small car on a beach in New England. Its point, that the ice carried the boulder there, comes at the end.

@art point-at-end
A skimmer who trusted that first sentence learned about a boulder, and not why it matters. Here it's the heading that rescues the question about it.

@chapter What the studies say
@list heading="The studies" items="Masson (1982): important points lowered less@Masson|Duggan and Payne (2009): more important ideas, no more detail@Duggan and Payne"
Masson, in nineteen eighty two, found that skimming stories lowered readers' understanding of the important points, but lowered it less than the unimportant ones. Duggan and Payne, in two thousand nine, gave readers time for half an expository text. Those who skimmed all of it remembered more of its important ideas than those who read half, though no more details.

@words text="More of the gist than the detail" sub="not all of it"
They chose where to look. A skim keeps more of the gist than of the detail. It doesn't keep all of it.

@chapter Set your limit
@math heading="A full reading at 240 wpm" items="594 × 60 / 240 = 148.5 s@times sixty over two forty;;40 s < ⅓ × 148.5 s@under a third"
Set your skim's limit from your baseline. At two hundred and forty words a minute, the passage takes five hundred and ninety four, times sixty over two forty: one hundred and forty eight point five seconds. Forty seconds is under a third of that.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Trusting first sentences always@The first|Scoring a skim as a fast reading@The second|Taking gist for understanding@And the third"
The first slip is trusting first sentences always to carry the point. The second is scoring a skim as a fast reading. And the third is taking a good gist score for understanding the text. It says what the passage is about, and nothing about its details.

@chapter Try it
@words text="120 of 600 words · 30 s · 2 of 4 right" sub="gist rate?"
Your turn. A skim shows one hundred and twenty of a passage's six hundred words for thirty seconds, and the reader gets two of four gist questions right. What's the gist rate? Two times sixty over thirty: four gist answers a minute.
