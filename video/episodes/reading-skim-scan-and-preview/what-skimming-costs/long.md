@chapter The question
@title text="What Skimming Costs" sub="Reading Faster · Skimming, Scanning and Previewing" art=thumb
A skim is often treated as a fast reading with a little less understanding. But does it lose understanding evenly? This drill splits the score in two to find out.

@chapter The drill
@art skim-vs-read
There are two readings, done in the order the lab gives. One is a passage on coral reefs, shown whole for forty seconds and then taken away. You skim it however you skim. The other is a matched passage on volcanoes, read in full at your usual pace.

@art gist-detail-split
Each passage is followed by six questions. Three are on the gist: what it's about, and what follows from it. Three are on details, a fact stated once. The panel scores the two kinds separately.

@chapter The typical preset
@math heading="Typical preset" items="skim: 410 words in 40 s = 615 wpm@four hundred and ten;;skim: gist 2/3 · detail 1/3@two of three gist;;read: 387 words in 90 s = 258 wpm@three hundred and eighty seven;;read: gist 3/3 · detail 3/3@all six"
Here's a typical reader. They skimmed the reef passage's four hundred and ten words in forty seconds, six hundred and fifteen words a minute, and got two of three gist and one of three detail. Then they read the volcano passage's three hundred and eighty seven words in ninety seconds, two fifty eight a minute, and got all six.

@words text="Lost: 1 gist · 2 details"
So the skim lost one gist question and two details. It didn't lose evenly.

@chapter The number that misleads
@math heading="Effective rates" items="skim: 615 × 3/6 = 307.5@six fifteen times three sixths;;read: 258 × 6/6 = 258@two fifty eight times"
Now look at the effective rates. Six fifteen times three sixths is three hundred and seven point five. Two fifty eight times six sixths is two fifty eight. By the single number you were taught to report, the skim won.

@art scale-tipped
That's why this lesson splits the score. The effective rate counts a gist question and a detail question as equal, and a purpose seldom does. If you needed the reef passage's facts, a skim that kept one detail in three was not a faster way to them.

@chapter Some readers skim well
@math heading="Good-skimmer preset" items="gist 3/3 · detail 2/3 in 40 s@all three gist;;615 × 5/6 = 512.5@five sixths"
The good skimmer preset kept all three gist answers and two of three details from the same forty second skim. That's six fifteen times five sixths, five hundred and twelve point five. Some readers skim well, and this drill is how you find out if you're one of them.

@chapter What the studies say
@art lose-unevenly
The typical preset is the pattern the studies report. Masson, in nineteen eighty two, found skimming stories lowered understanding of the important points and the unimportant ones, but lowered the important ones less. Duggan and Payne, in two thousand nine, found that skimming all of a text kept more important ideas than reading half of it, and no more details.

@words text="Gist suffers less than detail" sub="laboratory tasks · your passages are not theirs"
In a phrase: gist suffers less than detail. Both studies were laboratory tasks, though, and your two passages are not theirs.

@art one-pair
And if your skim kept the details as well as your reading did, you haven't refuted the studies, and they haven't refuted you. One pair of passages on one day is one measurement. The question is whether it happens again on another pair.

@chapter The purpose decides
@art purpose-fork
So choose by the purpose, not the rate. Write down what you need first: the text's point, or particular facts. If you need what a text argues, a skim that keeps the gist did its job. If you need a figure from it, a skim that lost the detail failed, however fast it was.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A skim is a fast reading, a little less understood@The first|Choosing by the effective rate@The second|Reading one question as a result@And the third"
The first slip is treating a skim as a fast reading with a little less understanding. The second is choosing by the effective rate. And the third is reading a one question difference as a result. One question is about sixteen point seven points of comprehension, and a third of its kind's score.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Skim: gist 3/3, detail 1/3 · Read: 3/3, 3/3" sub="you must quote figures at a meeting"
Your turn. A skim scores three of three gist and one of three detail. A full reading of a matched passage gets everything. You need to quote figures from texts like these at a meeting. Which do you use? The full reading, because the purpose needs detail, and detail is what the skim lost.
