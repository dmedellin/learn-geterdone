@chapter The question
@title text="Previewing Before Reading" sub="Reading Faster · Skimming, Scanning and Previewing" art=thumb
Before you read a text, you could look it over first: the headings, the first paragraph, and the last. That costs time before the reading even starts. So does it pay?

@art old-books
The advice is older than any test of it. Robinson's SQ three R, from nineteen forty six, begins with a survey of a chapter's headings. Adler and Van Doren, in nineteen seventy two, advise looking a text over before deciding how to read it. Both are advice. The drill is a way to test it on your own reading.

@chapter The drill
@art cold-vs-preview
There are two readings, in the order the lab gives. In one, you read a passage about bridges cold, at your usual pace. In the other, a passage about refrigerators first shows its headings and its first and last paragraphs for thirty seconds. Then you see the whole passage and read it as usual.

@art preview-shows
In a text written to a plan, those parts show you the question it starts from, its parts, and where it ends up. Both readings have six questions.

@art clock-includes
And here's the key. The clock starts at the preview, so those thirty seconds are in your time. A preview's cost is easy to forget, because it doesn't feel like reading.

@chapter When it pays
@math heading="Pays preset" items="cold: 451 × 60 / 110 = 246;  × 4/6 = 164@four fifty one;;previewed: 433 × 60 / (30 + 120) = 173.2@four thirty three;;× 6/6 = 173.2 → +9.2@nine point two above"
In the pays preset, four fifty one words took a hundred and ten seconds cold: two forty six a minute, four of six right, an effective rate of one sixty four. Then four thirty three words, with thirty seconds of preview and a hundred and twenty of reading, is one seventy three point two, all six right. That's nine point two above.

@words text="30 seconds → 2 more questions right"
The preview cost thirty seconds, and came with two more questions right.

@chapter When it doesn't
@math heading="Does-not preset" items="cold: 246 × 5/6 = 205@two oh five;;previewed: 173.2 × 5/6 ≈ 144.3@one forty four point three;;about 60.7 below@sixty point seven"
The does not preset has the same times, with five right on both. Two forty six times five sixths is two oh five for the cold reading. One seventy three point two times five sixths is about one forty four point three. That's about sixty point seven below. The preview bought nothing, and its thirty seconds were a fifth of the time.

@chapter Don't make it free
@art clock-late
Now leave the thirty seconds out of the pays preset. Four thirty three times sixty over a hundred and twenty is two sixteen point five words a minute: faster than the cold reading, and for the wrong reason. Starting the clock after the preview makes the preview free, and the lab refuses to do that.

@words text="216.5 vs 173.2" sub="the difference becomes 52.5 instead of 9.2"
Without those thirty seconds, the difference would be fifty two point five instead of nine point two. Counting the preview is the whole of the method.

@chapter Two ways to pay
@art skip-sections
A preview earns its time back one of two ways. You understand more of what you then read. Or you learn a part doesn't need reading, and you skip it. On a long report, the headings and last paragraph can tell you two sections don't concern you, and skipping them saves minutes, not seconds.

@words text="These passages: comprehension only"
This drill can't show that second way. The passages are short and every part is asked about, so here a preview can only pay through comprehension.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating a preview as free@The first|Starting the clock after the preview@The second|Previewing a text with no plan@And the third"
The first slip is treating a preview as free. The second is starting the clock after the preview. And the third is previewing a text with no plan. A story or a letter without headings shows you little in thirty seconds. And remember, one pair of passages is one comparison.

@chapter Try it
@words text="450 words · 30 s preview · 90 s reading · 6 of 6" sub="effective rate, preview counted?"
Your turn. A reader previews a four hundred and fifty word passage for thirty seconds, reads it in ninety, and gets six of six. What's the effective rate, with the preview counted? Four fifty times sixty, over thirty plus ninety: two twenty five. If you got three hundred, you left the preview out.
