@chapter The question
@title text="Testing a Speed Claim" sub="Reading Faster · The Inner Voice and Flashing Words" art=thumb
A claim of fifteen hundred words a minute can be checked before anyone is timed. You need three computations, and then one question: what test would settle it?

@art claims
The claims here are made at their strongest. Speed reading courses have long advertised a thousand words a minute and more, with graduates who say they read a novel in an evening. Some promise a page taken in at a glance. Photoreading claims to absorb a page without reading it, and it's tested here the same way as everything else.

@chapter What each glance would need
@math heading="225 ms + 30 ms, 6 a word, no regressions" items="glance = rate × 6 × 255 / 60,000@rate times six;;750 wpm → 19.125 characters@seven fifty;;1,500 wpm → 38.25@fifteen hundred;;10,500 wpm → 267.75@ten thousand five hundred"
First, the eye arithmetic. Characters per glance are the rate, times six, times two fifty five, over sixty thousand. Triple an ordinary two fifty is seven fifty: about nineteen point one characters. A novel an hour is fifteen hundred: thirty eight point two five. And three fifty words in a two second look is ten thousand five hundred: two hundred and sixty seven point seven five.

@art glance-overflow
Against a perceptual span of about fifteen characters, that's beyond it, two and a half times it, and several lines of text in one fixation. Shorter fixations help, but to bring fifteen hundred inside the span, a fixation and its saccade together would have to take a hundred milliseconds. That's less than half the average fixation alone.

@chapter What was understood
@math heading="At half the questions right" items="750 × 1/2 = 375@three seventy five;;1,500 × 1/2 = 750@seven fifty;;10,500 × 1/2 = 5,250@fifty two fifty"
The second computation asks what was understood. Give each claim half the questions right, and the effective rates are three seventy five, seven fifty, and fifty two fifty. Then change it to what the claimant can actually show.

@art page-turning
A rate with no comprehension attached is a page turning speed. And a claim that can't say what share of questions was answered can't fill the box at all.

@chapter The bookshelf
@art bookshelf-clock
The third computation is the bookshelf: how long a book would take. It's the words in the book divided by the rate, in minutes.

@math heading="A 90,000-word novel" items="at 238: ≈ 378 minutes@two thirty eight;;at 750: 120 minutes@seven fifty;;at 1,500: 60 minutes@fifteen hundred;;at 10,500: ≈ 8.6 minutes@eight point six"
A ninety thousand word novel at Brysbaert's two thirty eight words a minute takes about three hundred and seventy eight minutes, a little over six hours. At seven fifty, a hundred and twenty minutes. At fifteen hundred, the hour the claim promises. And at ten thousand five hundred, about eight point six minutes.

@quote text="I took a speed-reading course and read War and Peace in twenty minutes. It involves Russia." cite="Woody Allen"
Woody Allen's line puts the result of the fastest claims best. I took a speed reading course and read War and Peace in twenty minutes. It involves Russia.

@chapter What would settle it
@art three-groups
Then ask what would settle it. Just, Carpenter and Masson, in nineteen eighty two, set out the model: trained speed readers, ordinary readers, and people told to skim, on the same texts, with their eye fixations recorded and their understanding tested.

@words text="None comprehending beyond about 600 wpm" sub="Carver (1985)"
Carver, in nineteen eighty five, went looking for the world's best readers, speed reading graduates and claimants among them. He found none who showed comprehension beyond about six hundred words a minute. The fastest comprehending readers were superior readers, not graduates of the courses.

@list heading="A test that settles a claim" items="A passage the reader hasn't seen@hasn't seen|Questions someone else wrote@someone else wrote|A comparison reader or condition@comparison"
So here's the test to ask of any claim, and of your own results. A passage the reader hasn't seen. Questions someone else wrote, that can't be answered from general knowledge. And a comparison reader or condition. A testimonial has none of the three.

@art fresh-test
The drills in this Subject are built that way, and your log is the comparison.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A testimonial is a measured claim@The first|Quoting a rate without comprehension@The second|Reading the eye verdict as a law@And the third"
The first slip is treating a claim with a testimonial as a measured claim. A testimonial says how reading felt. The second is quoting a rate without the comprehension. And the third is reading the eye verdict as a law. It holds at the stated settings.

@chapter Try it
@words text="A course claims 1,000 wpm" sub="characters per glance?"
Your turn. A course claims its graduates read a thousand words a minute. At average settings, how many characters must each fixation advance? Multiply, then divide. You should get twenty five point five, beyond the perceptual span. Then ask the claim for its comprehension, and for the test behind it.
