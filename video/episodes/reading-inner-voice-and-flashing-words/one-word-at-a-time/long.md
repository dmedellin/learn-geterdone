@chapter The question
@title text="One Word at a Time" sub="Reading Faster · The Inner Voice and Flashing Words" art=thumb
Speed reading apps show one word at a time, in a fixed spot, so your eyes never move. The idea is that the eye movements were slowing you down. Does taking them away buy free speed?

@art word-box
The drill streams a story into a box, one word at a time, each for the same fraction of a second. You set it to the median of your baseline readings. That way the rate is one you read at anyway, and only the display has changed. Then six questions follow.

@words text="It can feel effortless"
Many readers find the first few lines strange, then settle into the rhythm. The stream can feel effortless. It sets the pace for you, so it rarely feels like falling behind. The questions are where to look next.

@chapter How long each word is shown
@math heading="Time per word" items="time per word = 60,000 / rate@sixty thousand;;250 wpm → 240 ms@two fifty;;500 wpm → 120 ms@five hundred;;600 wpm → 100 ms@six hundred"
A minute is sixty thousand milliseconds. So each word is shown for sixty thousand over the rate. At two fifty words a minute, that's two hundred and forty milliseconds a word. At five hundred, a hundred and twenty. At six hundred, the fastest the drill will run, a hundred milliseconds.

@art stream-flicker
Above six hundred, the drill refuses. The display becomes a flicker, rather than something to read.

@art requested-measured
The lab prints two times per word: the one you requested, and the one measured from how long the stream actually took. A busy browser can show a word a few milliseconds late. When they differ, the measured one is the rate you actually read at.

@chapter Three readers
@list heading="Three presets, one story" items="250 wpm, 5 of 6 → ≈ 208.3@two fifty|500 wpm, 3 of 6 → 250@five hundred|600 wpm, 2 of 6 → 200@six hundred"
At two fifty, a reader gets five of six, an effective rate of about two oh eight point three. At five hundred, three of six: an effective rate of two fifty. And at six hundred, two right: two hundred.

@art half-story
So the five hundred reader's effective rate is higher, because the product rewards speed. But half the questions are gone. Whether a reading that keeps half a story is good enough depends on what you're reading it for. A smooth feeling reading with three of six right lost half the story.

@chapter Why so little is saved
@math heading="The eye model" items="fixation 225 ms + saccade 30 ms = 255 ms@a fixation of;;30 / 255 ≈ 11.8%@about eleven point eight"
Why does removing eye movements save so little? Take the eye model: a fixation of two twenty five milliseconds, and a saccade of thirty. The saccades are thirty of every two hundred and fifty five milliseconds, about eleven point eight percent of the time.

@art fixation-time
Nearly all reading time is spent in the fixations, identifying words. And a word flashed in a box still needs that time. So removing every saccade would save about a tenth.

@chapter What the studies found
@words text="Sentences at about 12 words a second" sub="Potter (1984)"
That's not to say the stream is unreadable. Potter, in nineteen eighty four, showed that single sentences can be understood in a stream at about twelve words a second, around seven hundred and twenty words a minute.

@list heading="The costs come with passages" items="Lower literal comprehension with Spritz@literal comprehension|More visual fatigue, fewer blinks@blinking less|Lower comprehension at speed@at speed"
The costs come with passages, with memory, and with having no way back. When Benedetto and colleagues tested Spritz in twenty fifteen, literal comprehension was lower, and visual fatigue higher, with readers blinking less. Acklin and Papesh, in twenty seventeen, found the apps lowered comprehension at speed, compared with normal reading.

@chapter Reading your own
@art no-way-back
In the stream, your eyes don't move along a line, the next word isn't visible before it arrives, and a word that's gone can't be looked at again. The drill measures all three changes together. Compare your share right with your baselines. And remember, one question is about sixteen point seven points.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A flashed stream is free speed@The first|Judging the stream by how it felt@The second|Streaming at a guessed rate@And the third"
The first slip is believing a flashed stream is free speed. Saccades are only about a tenth of reading time. The second is judging the stream by how it felt. And the third is streaming at a guessed rate, which changes both the display and the pace, so you can't tell which one moved your score.
