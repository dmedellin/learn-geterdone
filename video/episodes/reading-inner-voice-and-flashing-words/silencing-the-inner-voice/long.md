@chapter The question
@title text="Silencing the Inner Voice" sub="Reading Faster · The Inner Voice and Flashing Words" art=thumb
The usual advice for getting rid of the inner voice is to give it something else to do. Hum, count, or repeat a word under your breath while you read, until the habit of hearing the text fades. If the voice were only a brake, that should make reading faster and cost nothing.

@art matched-pair
So the drill tests it on two matched passages, of the same length and difficulty to within a few words and a fraction of a grade. Read one normally. Read the other while counting one two three, one two three, steadily under your breath.

@chapter Keeping the voice busy
@art counting-mouth
Mouthing the numbers without sound is enough. This is called articulatory suppression: it occupies the mouth and the speech machinery while you read. Slowiaczek and Clifton used counting this way, in nineteen eighty, and this drill copies their method.

@words text="Tick it only if you kept counting"
After the counting reading, tick I kept counting the whole way, only if you did. A reading where the counting lapsed is partly a normal reading of a different passage, and the comparison is void. Both readings have six questions.

@chapter A worked pair
@math heading="Typical preset" items="normal: 250 wpm, 6 of 6 → 250@two fifty;;counting: 268 wpm, 4 of 6 → ≈ 178.7@two sixty eight;;difference ≈ −71.3@seventy one point three"
Here's a typical reader. Normally: two fifty words a minute, all six right, an effective rate of two fifty. While counting: faster, two sixty eight, but four right. That's about one seventy eight point seven. The difference is about minus seventy one point three.

@art faster-but-less
The reader went eighteen words a minute faster and lost two questions. The product says the trade was a bad one.

@math heading="Easy-text preset" items="normal: 260 wpm, 6 of 6 → 260@two sixty;;counting: 266 wpm, 5 of 6 → ≈ 221.7@two sixty six;;difference ≈ −38.3, one question@thirty eight point three"
On easier text, another reader went two sixty with six right, and two sixty six with five right while counting, about two twenty one point seven. The difference is about minus thirty eight point three, and it rests on one question. A one question difference is not a result.

@chapter What the studies found
@art muscle-feedback
The studies point the same way, and they're few and small. Hardyck and Petrinovich, in nineteen seventy, recorded the activity of the speech muscles in the throat and fed it back to readers, so they could learn to keep it quiet. When they did, comprehension of the difficult text fell, while the easy text held up.

@art heard-vs-read
And Slowiaczek and Clifton found that counting impaired comprehension of passages people read, though not of passages they heard. So counting didn't stop people understanding language in general. It hurt reading.

@words text="Counting hurt reading, not hearing" sub="Slowiaczek and Clifton (1980)"
That contrast is worth a second look. The same counting, the same people, and the cost showed up only when the language came through the eyes.

@chapter The second-task problem
@art two-tasks
The studies don't settle why. And the second task problem is always there. Counting takes attention, and a reader doing two things at once may lose comprehension whatever the inner voice does. This drill can't tell those apart. What the studies don't support is the claim that occupying the voice is free.

@chapter Reading your own
@words text="Faster is real. Did understanding keep up?"
If your counting reading was a little faster, that's the experience the advice is built on, and it's real. The question the effective rate asks is whether what you understood kept up with the speed. If you kept counting and still did as well, that's two readings, on standard text, not the difficult text where the cost was found.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Silencing the voice is the key to speed@The first|Counting a lapsed reading@The second|Reading the rate, not the effective rate@And the third"
The first slip is believing that eliminating the inner voice is the key to speed reading. None of the studies found it to be a cost free brake. The second is counting a reading where the counting lapsed. And the third is reading the rate and not the effective rate.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Normal: 250 wpm, 5 of 6 · Counting: 270 wpm, 4 of 6" sub="counting minus normal?"
Your turn. Normally a reader reads at two fifty with five of six right. While counting, two seventy with four of six. What's the difference in effective rate, counting minus normal? You should get about minus twenty eight point three.
