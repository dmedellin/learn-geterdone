@chapter The question
@title text="Reading in Phrases" sub="Reading Faster · The Inner Voice and Flashing Words" art=thumb
Speed reading courses teach reading in phrases. The advice is to read in thought units, three or four words at a time, with fewer stops along a line. Does grouping change what you understand?

@art word-vs-phrase
The drill tests a narrower version. Two streams, at one rate. The first passage comes one word at a time. The second, a matched passage, comes one marked phrase at a time. Put your baseline median into both target boxes before you start.

@chapter A phrase is a unit of meaning
@words text="into the top layer · of the soil"
A phrase here is a few words that belong together, like into the top layer, of the soil. The phrase passage is marked into groups of two to five words, and the stream shows one group at a time.

@art phrase-stream
Both passages are matched for length and difficulty, but they're different texts: one about building a campfire, one about planting a young tree. And the lab alternates which you read first between visits.

@chapter How long a phrase is shown
@math heading="At 250 words a minute" items="4 × 60,000 / 250 = 960 ms@four word phrase;;2 words → 480 ms@two word phrase;;438 words, 127 phrases: ≈ 827.7 ms mean@a hundred and twenty seven"
A phrase stays on screen as long as its words would at that rate. At two fifty words a minute, a four word phrase is shown for nine hundred and sixty milliseconds, and a two word phrase for four eighty. The tree passage has four hundred and thirty eight words in a hundred and twenty seven phrases, so the mean is about eight hundred and twenty seven point seven.

@art same-clock
So both streams run at the same words a minute, and take the same time for the same number of words. Only the grouping differs.

@chapter Two readers
@math heading="Grouping helps" items="words: 250 wpm, 3 of 6 → 125@Word by word;;phrases: 250 wpm, 5 of 6 → ≈ 208.3@five right;;difference ≈ +83.3@eighty three point three"
Here's a reader for whom it helped. Word by word, two fifty words a minute, three of six right: an effective rate of a hundred and twenty five. Phrase by phrase, also two fifty, five right: about two oh eight point three. Same rate, two more questions, a difference of about eighty three point three.

@words text="4 of 6 both times · difference 0" sub="no-difference preset"
And a reader for whom it didn't: four of six right in both streams, about one sixty six point seven each, and a difference of zero. One question either way, about sixteen point seven points, is not a result.

@chapter What the pair isolates
@art isolated
What the pair isolates is the grouping. Both streams run at one rate in the same box, and neither lets you look back. So a difference isn't about speed or regressions. It can still be about the passages, matched but not the same, and about the order.

@chapter What it can't show
@art fixate-content
What it can't show is that phrase reading speeds up ordinary reading. The usual claim is about the eye: that a trained reader sees a whole phrase in one fixation. But in normal reading, the eyes still fixate most content words. And words are identified only about seven to eight letters to the right of a fixation.

@words text="Chunking groups words by meaning" sub="it does not widen the glance"
Chunking groups words by meaning. It doesn't widen the glance.

@art unmarked-page
And on a page, nobody marks the phrases for you. So if grouping helped you, what helped may be a stream that hands you the words in meaningful pieces. That's a finding about streams, not about the page.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Several words in one fixation@The first|Comparing streams at different rates@The second|Carrying a stream result to the page@And the third"
The first slip is thinking phrase reading means seeing several words in one fixation. The second is comparing streams at different rates. If one ran faster, a difference could be the rate. And the third is carrying a stream result to the page.

@chapter Try it
@words text="300 wpm · a three-word phrase" sub="how long is it shown?"
Your turn. At three hundred words a minute, how long is a three word phrase shown? Work out one word first, then multiply. You should get six hundred milliseconds: three words at two hundred each. Then run your own pair, and compare effective rates: rate times correct over six, for each. A pattern over several pairs on different days says more than one.
