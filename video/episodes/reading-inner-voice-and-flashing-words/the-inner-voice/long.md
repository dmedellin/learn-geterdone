@chapter The question
@title text="The Inner Voice" sub="Reading Faster · The Inner Voice and Flashing Words" art=thumb
Most people hear the words as they read silently. The speed reading tradition calls that voice a brake, held at speaking pace. Is it? You can check in five minutes.

@art voice-brake
The advice is old and widely repeated. The voice in your head is a habit left over from learning to read aloud. It holds silent reading to the pace of speech, so a fast reader must learn to stop hearing the words. Readers who practise this often say the voice grows fainter as they speed up, and that experience is real enough to take seriously.

@chapter The prediction
@words text="If the voice held you to speaking pace…" sub="silent ≈ aloud"
But the claim makes a prediction. If the voice held you to speaking pace, your silent rate couldn't be much above your aloud rate.

@list heading="Brysbaert (2019)" items="Silent, nonfiction: about 238@two hundred and thirty eight|Aloud: about 183@a hundred and eighty three|Silent is about 1.3 × aloud@one point three times"
The published averages already say otherwise. Brysbaert's review puts silent reading of nonfiction at about two hundred and thirty eight words a minute, and reading aloud at about a hundred and eighty three. On average, adults read silently about one point three times as fast as they read aloud.

@chapter Your own ratio
@art two-easy-stories
So the drill has two easy stories. Read the first aloud at a natural speaking pace. A whisper is enough, and there are no questions. Read the second silently at your usual pace, then answer six questions. The lab divides the second rate by the first.

@art aloud-silent
Easy stories keep the aloud reading from turning into a struggle with pronunciation, so it measures your speaking pace. Read it as you would to someone across a table: not a performance, and not a race.

@math heading="The ratio" items="ratio = silent rate / aloud rate@divided by;;238 / 183 ≈ 1.3 ≈ 130%@one point three"
The silent to aloud ratio is the silent rate divided by the aloud rate, each in words a minute. Above one, you read silently faster than you speak. For the published averages, two thirty eight over one eighty three is about one point three, or a hundred and thirty percent.

@art ratio-bars
The lab prints it as a fraction and a percentage. Above one, your eyes and your inner voice are already going faster than your mouth.

@chapter A faster reader
@math heading="Fast-silent preset" items="aloud: 477 words in 159 s = 180 wpm@a hundred and eighty;;silent: 453 words in 84.9375 s = 320 wpm@three hundred and twenty;;320 / 180 = 16/9 ≈ 177.8%@sixteen ninths"
Here's another reader. They read four hundred and seventy seven words aloud in a hundred and fifty nine seconds: a hundred and eighty words a minute. Then four hundred and fifty three silently in about eighty five seconds: three hundred and twenty. The ratio is sixteen ninths, about a hundred and seventy seven point eight percent.

@art still-hearing
That's silent reading nearly one point eight times the speed of speech, by a reader who may well still hear every word.

@chapter What the ratio means
@words text="Ratio above 1 → the voice isn't holding you to your mouth's pace"
A ratio above one means you already read silently faster than you can speak. So whatever your inner voice is doing, it isn't holding you to the pace of your mouth. A ratio near one or below is unusual for easy stories. Check you read the silent passage at your usual pace, and not carefully.

@art inner-vs-spoken
Why can that be? Because inner speech isn't your mouth moving quietly. It's not full articulation, so it isn't tied to the pace at which lips, tongue and breath can make the sounds.

@words text="Rates, not effective rates" sub="the aloud side has no questions"
One detail. The lab compares rates, not effective rates, because the aloud reading has no questions. It's there to time your speaking pace. If you can't read aloud where you are, the lab can use Brysbaert's oral average instead, but that's a weaker comparison.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The voice limits you, so it must go@The first|Reading the averages as targets@The second|Treating one pair as a fact about you@And the third"
The first slip is believing the inner voice limits you to speaking speed, so it must go. The second is reading the published averages as targets. And the third is treating one pair as a fact about yourself. Different stories, one order, one day. A second pair will differ.

@chapter Try it
@words text="400 words aloud in 120 s · 450 silently in 90 s" sub="the ratio?"
Your turn. A reader reads four hundred words aloud in a hundred and twenty seconds, and four hundred and fifty silently in ninety. What's the ratio? Work out each rate first. You should get two hundred and three hundred, so a ratio of one point five.
