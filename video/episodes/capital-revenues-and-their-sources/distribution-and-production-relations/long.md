@chapter Arguments about fairness
@title text="Distribution Relations and Production Relations" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 51" art=thumb
Arguments about fairness are mostly arguments about distribution: the minimum wage, taxes on income and inheritance, rent controls. They assume the product could be shared out differently while it's produced as it is now. This chapter is about that assumption.

@art two-views
Marx describes two views. The ordinary one treats wages, profit and rent as natural, the way any society must share its product. The more careful one, John Stuart Mill's, grants that distribution is historical, but holds production fixed: its laws are like physical truths, while distribution is a matter of human institution that society can change.

@words text="Mill, 1848" sub="production like physics, distribution by institution"
Mill drew from it a programme of reform, taxes on inheritance and on the unearned rise in land values. The tax and transfer states of the twentieth century were built on the same distinction.

@chapter Capital before revenue
@art capital-first
Marx's reply: the product isn't divided first among wages, profit and rent. It's divided first between capital and revenue. Wages, he says, never become the worker's revenue until they've first faced him as capital. Wages exist only because the means of production belong to someone else, and labour power is bought with capital.

@quote text="The conditions of distribution are essentially identical with these conditions of production, being their reverse side, so that both conditions share the same historical and passing character." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 51"
That's a relation of production, and distribution is its other face. The conditions of distribution are essentially identical with these conditions of production, being their reverse side, so that both conditions share the same historical and passing character.

@words text="“Profit, then, appears here as the main factor, not of the distribution of products, but of their production itself”"
And profit isn't only a share. It decides where capital goes and what's produced. Profit, then, appears here as the main factor, not of the distribution of products, but of their production itself.

@chapter What every society keeps
@art common-fund
What's common to every society? Part of the labour supplies the producers' own consumption, and part makes a surplus for social needs: an insurance fund against accidents, a fund for expansion, and the keep of those too young, too old or too ill to work. Strip wages and surplus value of their capitalist character, Marx says, and you have only their foundations, common to all modes of production.

@chapter One £200, three ways
@art three-divisions
Now divide one value product three ways. The lesson's figures are illustrative: a hundred pounds of means of production to replace, and two hundred of value product.

@math heading="The same two hundred pounds, three ways" items="capitalist: 100 + 30 + 20 + 50 = £200@capitalist;;cooperative: 150 + 30 + 20 + 0 = £200@co-operative;;association: 150 consumed + 50 common fund = £200@association;;every case: c = £100 replaced@every case"
Under a capitalist firm, wages are a hundred, rent thirty, interest twenty and profit of enterprise fifty. In a workers' co-operative that rents land and borrows capital, members take a hundred and fifty, and thirty and twenty still go out. In an association, a hundred and fifty is consumed, and fifty goes to a common fund. In every case, a hundred is replaced.

@art coop-workshop
The co-operative's split would leave a capitalist owner with nothing for his trouble. It works because the workers own the firm, a change in the relations of production. Rent and interest stay, because private land and lending haven't changed.

@art association
And in the association, the lab's tiles still say wages and profit, because those are the only names it has. They're the wrong names, and that's the chapter's point. The means of production, the value product and a fund above consumption appear in all three. The forms of wages, profit and rent belong to the first.

@chapter The calculation debate
@words text="Mises, 1920 · Lange, 1936 · Hayek" sub="can an association calculate without prices?"
The standard objection is about knowledge, not fairness. Mises argued in nineteen twenty that without markets in the means of production there are no prices, so an association can't calculate. Lange replied that a planning board could find prices by trial and error. Hayek answered that the knowledge needed is dispersed, and only markets gather it.

@quote text="The shortening of the working day is its fundamental premise." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 48"
Marx says only that keeping accounts would become more essential than ever. How, Volume Three doesn't answer. Earlier, he'd looked past the realm of necessity to the true realm of freedom beyond it. The shortening of the working day is its fundamental premise.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Change distribution, keep production@The first|An association has no surplus@The second|The tile names fit every society@And the third"
The first slip is thinking distribution can be changed without changing production relations. The second is thinking an association has no surplus. It needs a fund above consumption too. And the third is reading the names wages and profit as names for every society.
