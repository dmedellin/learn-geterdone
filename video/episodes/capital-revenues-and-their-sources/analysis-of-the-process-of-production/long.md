@chapter Income and product
@title text="Concerning the Analysis of the Process of Production" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 49" art=thumb
Every year a government announces a figure for national income, and every year the shops, farms and factories turn out a product. It seems obvious the two should match. What everyone earns is what everyone can buy.

@quote text="How can the annually produced value buy a product, which has a higher value than its own?" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 49"
Marx says they can't. The year's labour adds variable capital plus surplus value, and that's all that becomes wages, profit and rent. The product also carries the value of the cotton, coal and wear it used up. So how can the annually produced value buy a product, which has a higher value than its own?

@art income-gap
Income is smaller than product, and the gap has to be made up from somewhere. Two questions follow: who pays for the constant capital, out of what, and who does the labour that replaces it? Marx says Volume Two has already solved them, and returns to it because of an error that has run through political economy since Adam Smith.

@chapter The two departments
@art two-departments
The answer is Volume Two's scheme of simple reproduction. Department One makes means of production: machines and iron. Department Two makes means of consumption. Marx copies out the numbers, and says they're chosen at random. What isn't random is the balance between them.

@math heading="Simple reproduction" items="I: 4000c + 1000v + 1000s = 6000@department one;;II: 2000c + 500v + 500s = 3000@department two;;I(v + s) = 2000 = IIc@buys"
Department One: four thousand constant, a thousand variable, a thousand surplus value, six thousand in all. Department Two: two thousand, five hundred and five hundred, three thousand. Department One's revenue, two thousand, buys Department Two's two thousand of constant capital.

@art three-flows
Read it the way the chapter does. Department Two's workers, capitalists and landlords spend their thousand on Department Two's own consumer goods. Department One's revenue can't be eaten as machines and iron, so it buys the other two thousand of consumer goods, and Department Two takes the machines and iron in return.

@math heading="Nine thousand of product, three thousand of income" items="product: 6000 + 3000 = 9000@nine thousand;;revenue: I(1000 + 1000) + II(500 + 500) = 3000@three thousand;;within Department I: Ic = 4000@four thousand"
So the product is nine thousand. The revenue of the whole society is three thousand, a third of it. And Department One's own four thousand of constant capital is replaced out of its own product,.

@art within-department
Its capitalists sell to each other, or use their own product again. That four thousand never passes through anyone's hands as income at all.

@chapter When it doesn't balance
@words text="II: 1500c + 750v + 750s" sub="I(v + s) = 2000 · IIc = 1500 · 500 left over"
Marx's starting point for accumulation in Volume Two changes Department Two to fifteen hundred, seven fifty and seven fifty. Now Department One's revenue is two thousand, and Department Two's constant capital only fifteen hundred. Five hundred is left over. In Volume Two, that's Department One's accumulation: surplus value existing as means of production.

@chapter Smith's error
@art horse-regress
Here is the error. Adam Smith argued that the price of every commodity resolves, in the end, into wages, profit and rent. The farmer's horse looks like a fourth part of the price of corn, but the horse itself, Smith said, is made of the same three parts, and so on back. Say concluded that a nation's gross product and its net income are the same thing.

@words text="“Take a whole from a whole and nothing remains.”"
Ricardo had already answered Say in one line: take a whole from a whole and nothing remains.

@quote text="The phrase that a thing is revenue for one man and capital for another saves all further thought." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 49"
Marx's diagnosis: the error comes from looking at one capital at a time. The weaver's yarn is the spinner's revenue, so constant capital seems to be just revenue seen from another side. The phrase that a thing is revenue for one man and capital for another saves all further thought.

@art one-sector
Added up over society, the riddle comes back: how can they buy a value of four with a value of three? Remove Department One altogether, as the error implies, and the balance fails at once. Department Two's constant capital has nothing to be replaced with.

@chapter Gross and net
@words text="Value added · Leontief, 1941"
Marx separates gross output, the whole product, from gross income, wages plus profit plus rent. Modern national accounts draw the same line, subtracting intermediate consumption to reach value added. Leontief, whose input output tables traced each industry's purchases, named Quesnay's Tableau as their ancestor. What's disputed is whether value added is made by labour alone.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Annual income equals the annual product@The first|Constant capital is revenue from another side@The second|The balance needs Marx's numbers@And the third"
The first slip is thinking annual income equals the annual product. The second is treating constant capital as revenue seen from another side. And the third is thinking the balance needs Marx's numbers. Any numbers work, as long as Department One's revenue equals Department Two's constant capital.
