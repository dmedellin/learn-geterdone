@chapter The last page
@title text="Classes" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 52" art=thumb
The last chapter of Capital is a page long. Marx wrote it in the manuscript of the mid eighteen sixties, which Engels published as Volume Three in eighteen ninety four, eleven years after Marx's death.

@art three-classes
It begins by naming its subject. The owners of labour power, of capital, and of land, whose revenues are wages, profit and ground rent, form the three great classes of modern society resting on the capitalist mode of production.

@chapter Blurred boundaries
@quote text="Middle and transition stages obliterate even here all definite boundaries" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 52"
He qualifies that at once. England is where modern society is most developed, and even there the classes don't appear in a pure form. Middle and transition stages obliterate even here all definite boundaries, less in the country than in the towns.

@art separating
That, he says, doesn't matter for the analysis. The tendency of capitalist production is to separate the means of production more and more from labour, turning labour into wage labour and the means of production into capital, while landed property separates from both.

@chapter The question
@quote text="The first question to be answered is this: What constitutes a class?" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 52"
Then the question. The first question to be answered is this: what constitutes a class? And what makes wage labourers, capitalists and landlords three great social classes?

@words text="Same revenue, same source?"
At first glance, Marx says, it might seem the identity of their revenues and their sources does that. It's the natural answer after the last four chapters. The trinity formula paired each class with a source: labour with wages, capital with profit, land with rent.

@chapter Testing the answer
@math heading="Group by source of revenue" items="wages, profit, rent: 3 groups@three groups;;add medical fees and salary: 3 + 2 = 5 groups@physician;;rent split five ways: wages, profit + 5 = 7 groups@seven"
The lesson tests it. Two spinners, a mill owner, a manufacturer and a landlord give three groups: wages, profit, rent. Add a physician living on fees and an official on a salary, and the same test finds five. Tell the landlords apart by what their land is, vineyards, farms, forests, mines and fisheries, and the count is seven.

@art splitting-groups
Those are Marx's own examples, and you can see why they trouble him. The test counts kinds of income, and there are as many kinds as the division of labour cares to make. It can't by itself produce three great classes, or explain why those three matter more than physicians and fishery owners.

@chapter Where the book has brought you
@art three-volumes
Step back. Volume One took a commodity and found the labour time that makes it a value, and in the working day the surplus labour that makes surplus value. Volume Two followed that value through the circuits of capital, and showed two departments can reproduce each other only in proportion.

@art surface-forms
Volume Three let surplus value appear on the surface: as profit, averaged into prices of production, shared with merchants, split into interest and profit of enterprise, and claimed by landlords as rent. Part Seven puts the pieces back on the surface, where each looks like the fruit of its own source, and asks who lives on them.

@words text="Every step is contested"
Every step of that argument is contested. The labour theory of value faces the marginal utility theory that replaced it. The passage from values to prices is the transformation problem. And the falling rate of profit is a tendency whose record is a matter of data. What the course has done is narrower: compute what follows on Marx's definitions.

@chapter Where the manuscript ends
@quote text="For instance, the landlords are divided into owners of vineyards, farms, forests, mines, fisheries." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 52"
From this point of view, Marx writes, physicians and officials would also form two classes. The same would be true of the infinite dissipation of interests and positions created by the division of labour. For instance, the landlords are divided into owners of vineyards, farms, forests, mines, fisheries.

@art torn-page
And there the manuscript ends, in the middle of the question it set itself.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A class is defined by its income source alone@The first|The three classes are pure and complete@The second|Supplying the chapter's answer@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a class is defined by its source of income alone. The chapter tests that and finds it doesn't stop at three. The second is reading the three great classes as pure and complete. And the third is supplying the chapter's answer. Lenin and Weber gave their own later. The text gives none.
