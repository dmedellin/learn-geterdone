@chapter Three trees
@title text="The Trinity Formula" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 48" art=thumb
Think about the incomes you know. A wage is paid for work. Money in a savings account earns interest, and the more money, the more interest. A flat let to a tenant earns rent.

@art three-trees
Each income seems to come from its own source, in proportion to it, and you can spend it every year without touching the source. Marx says the revenues look like the annually consumable fruits of a perennial tree, or rather of three trees. The trinity formula is that picture, written as economics.

@words text="Three fragments, one gap" sub="Engels on Chapter 48"
This is the last Part of the last volume, and the text is rough. Engels says the chapter is made of three fragments, and the text marks a missing sheet of manuscript and a place where it stops short. What survives is an argument with a clear shape.

@chapter Profit happily eliminated
@math heading="The formula" items="Capital — Profit (enterprise + interest)@first version;;Land — Ground-Rent@land;;Labor — Wages@labor;;Capital — Interest@shrinks"
In the first version, capital yields profit, made of profit of enterprise plus interest; land yields ground rent; labor yields wages. Then the formula shrinks, and capital yields interest.

@quote text="Here profit, the specific mark characterizing the form of surplus-value belonging to the capitalist mode of production, is happily eliminated." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 48"
Profit of enterprise now looks like a wage for managing. And here profit, the specific mark characterizing the form of surplus value belonging to the capitalist mode of production, is happily eliminated. Profit points back to the factory. Interest points to nothing but the sum lent.

@chapter Carrots and music
@art three-sources
Then Marx looks at the three sources, and finds they have nothing in common. They have about the same relation to each other as lawyer's fees, carrots, and music. Capital is not a thing: a spindle is capital only where one class owns it and another works it. Land is a piece of nature. And labour by itself is an abstraction.

@art one-pie
Yet the supposed fruits, interest, rent and wages, are all quantities of the same thing, value, and can be added together. Value is labor, Marx says. Therefore surplus value cannot be land. Three incomparable sources, one comparable product: that mismatch is the clue.

@chapter One value product
@math heading="Two hundred pounds in four parts" items="value product: v + s = 100 + 100 = £200@two hundred;;wages: v = £100@wages;;rent: 3/10 · 100 = £30@rent;;interest: 1/5 · 100 = £20@interest;;profit of enterprise: 100 − 30 − 20 = £50@fifty;;sum: 100 + 30 + 20 + 50 = £200@add"
The lesson's figures are illustrative. A product is worth a hundred of means of production, a hundred of wages and a hundred of surplus value. The value product the year's labour added is two hundred. Wages are a hundred. Rent takes three tenths of surplus value, thirty. Interest takes a fifth, twenty. Profit of enterprise is the remaining fifty. Add them, and you get two hundred.

@art no-landlord
Now take the landlord away: the farmer owns his land. Rent paid out is nothing, and profit of enterprise is eighty. The land is exactly as fertile as before. Marx would still count thirty of that as rent, paid by the farmer to himself.

@words text="All borrowed: interest £40 · enterprise £30"
Or let every pound of capital be borrowed. Interest doubles to forty, and profit of enterprise falls to thirty, though no machine or worker does anything different. The four parts always add up to two hundred. The shares follow titles of ownership. The sum doesn't move.

@chapter The other side
@words text="Clark, 1899: each factor earns its marginal product"
The modern view is the one most economists hold. John Bates Clark argued in eighteen ninety nine that each factor is paid its marginal product, and Wicksteed showed that, with constant returns, that uses up the whole product. On that account the trinity is no illusion: land and capital really are productive.

@art cambridge-debate
In the nineteen fifties and sixties, Joan Robinson and Piero Sraffa argued that capital can't be measured apart from the rate of profit, and Samuelson conceded the technical point. Marx's own claim is narrow: land and machines help make the use value, but add no new value.

@chapter A topsy-turvy world
@art topsy-turvy
Marx is enjoying himself. In the German the pair are Monsieur le Capital and Madame la Terre, a couple out of a fairy tale.

@quote text="It is an enchanted, perverted, topsy-turvy world, in which Mister Capital and Mistress Land carry on their goblin tricks as social characters and at the same time as mere things." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 48"
It is an enchanted, perverted, topsy turvy world, in which Mister Capital and Mistress Land carry on their goblin tricks as social characters and at the same time as mere things.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Each factor creates its own income@The first|Interest as capital's fruit, enterprise as a wage@The second|What land helps make is what rent is@And the third"
The first slip is thinking each factor creates its own income. The second is taking interest as capital's fruit, and profit of enterprise as the capitalist's wage. And the third is confusing what land helps make with what rent is.
