@chapter Two claims
@title text="Essences and Existence" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Preface" art=thumb
The Preface's first page says phenomenology is the study of essences. And then it says phenomenology puts essences back into existence. How can both be true?

@words text="From the Dasein to the Wesen" sub="from being there to what being there is"
Husserl's thesis is that every reduction is also eidetic. To scrutinise perception, you draw back from living in the world, and pass from the fact of your existence to its nature: from being there to what being there is.

@chapter A means, not an end
@quote text="the essence is here not the end, but a means" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Preface, p. xvi"
Here's the key sentence. The essence is a means. What has to be understood is our effective involvement in the world.

@art magnet-filings
And that involvement polarizes our concepts, the way a magnet lines up iron filings. Every particular concept takes its direction from it. We need the essence because our existence is held too tightly in the world to know itself while it's involved. A concept gives it distance.

@chapter The Vienna Circle
@words text="The Vienna Circle" sub="Schlick · Carnap · Neurath"
Now an opponent. The Vienna Circle held that a statement means something only if there's a way to check it. At their strongest, they warn that a word like consciousness carries old metaphysics. Looking straight at consciousness, you may only be looking at the history of a word.

@art word-and-life
The Preface calls this the antithesis of Husserl's thought. Whatever the word's history, we have direct access to what it names, because we have the experience of ourselves. That experience is what every linguistic meaning is measured against.

@chapter The fisherman's net
@art fishing-net
Then the image the section is remembered for. Husserl's essences bring back all the living relationships of experience, as the fisherman's net draws up quivering fish and seaweed from the depths. The net isn't the catch. You throw it to bring up what's alive.

@list heading="So the Preface answers" items="Wahl: Husserl separates essences from existence@Jean Wahl|The separated essences are those of language@language"
So when Jean Wahl wrote that Husserl separates essences from existence, the Preface says he was wrong. The separated essences are those of language. And even language rests on a life of consciousness before any predication. The world is not what I think, but what I live through.

@chapter Free variation
@art tone-variations
The lab runs free variation, on the lesson's own examples. Take a tone and vary it five ways, in pitch, loudness and timbre. Add a sixth with no duration, marked as no longer a sound.

@math heading="Sound preset" items="pitch: 220 and 880 both occur → varies@pitch;;loudness: soft and loud → varies@loudness;;timbre: flute and violin → varies@timbre;;some duration: in all 5 → the core@some duration;;sixth variant: lacks some duration@the sixth"
Pitch takes two values, two twenty and eight eighty, so it varies. Loudness is soft or loud, so it varies. Timbre is flute or violin, so it varies. Having some duration is in all five, so that's the core. And the sixth variant lacks exactly that.

@words text="core: some-duration"
Notice how little that says. Having some duration is true of every tone, and tells you nothing about the one you're hearing. The invariant is the net, and the tone with its pitch and grain is the catch.

@chapter A relation, not a part
@art melody-transposed
The melody matters most. In eighteen ninety, Christian von Ehrenfels asked what stays when a melody is transposed. Play C, E, G. Then D, F sharp, A. Then B flat, D, F. Every note has changed, and you hear the same tune.

@math heading="Melody preset" items="C E G · D F♯ A · B♭ D F@every note;;core: major third up, minor third up@the core;;G E C: every note of the first, no core@backwards"
Every note differs, but the core is a major third up, then a minor third up: the steps, and no note at all. And the C notes played backwards keep every note of the first and lose the core. What a melody keeps is a relation.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The inquiry ends at the invariant@ends|An essence is a word's meaning@word's meaning|The core is always a part@a part"
Don't say the inquiry ends at the invariant: the essence is a means. Don't say an essence is a word's meaning: that's the Vienna Circle's view. And don't expect the core to be a part. A transposed melody shares no note with the original, and still has one.
