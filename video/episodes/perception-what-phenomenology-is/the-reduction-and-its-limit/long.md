@chapter A step back
@title text="The Reduction and Its Limit" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Preface" art=thumb
Phenomenology asks you to describe the world as it's given. But the world you live in is usually too close to notice. So how do you see it?

@words text="Wonder" sub="Eugen Fink"
Husserl's answer was the phenomenological reduction. The Preface says its best formulation is probably Eugen Fink's, Husserl's assistant in Freiburg, who spoke of wonder in the face of the world.

@words text="A reading, not the last word"
Keep one thing in mind from the start. This is a reading of the reduction. It leans on Fink and on unpublished manuscripts most readers hadn't seen in nineteen forty five, and whether it's Husserl's own view is still disputed.

@chapter The reading set aside
@art red-book-chain
The best known version made the reduction a return to a transcendental consciousness, with the world spread out before it, completely transparent. Take a red book. A sensation shows a redness, the redness a red surface, the surface a piece of cardboard, and that, this book.

@words text="Peter's view = Paul's view" sub="seen from nowhere in particular"
Followed to the end, that road leads to an idealism where the world is just meaning. Peter's view and Paul's become the same view, seen from nowhere in particular. The Preface wants something else.

@chapter Threads and sparks
@quote text="it slackens the intentional threads which attach us to the world and thus brings them to our notice" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Preface, p. xv"
Here's its sentence. Reflection doesn't withdraw from the world. It steps back, the way you step back from a painting, to see it better, not to stop looking.

@art sparks-from-fire
What it sees are the forms of transcendence, the ways consciousness is always already out among things. They fly up like sparks from a fire. They were there all along. The step back just lets them show.

@art slack-threads
Then the famous image. A thread pulled tight is invisible from inside the pull. Let it go slack, and you see it. We're made of ties to the world, so to notice them we refuse the world our complicity for a moment. We look on without joining in.

@chapter Not Descartes's doubt
@list heading="Two different moves" items="Descartes: withhold assent, win the world back as thought@Descartes|The reduction: withdraw nothing, see the ground@The reduction"
It's easy to hear Descartes again. Descartes withholds assent from anything that can be doubted, the world included, and wins it back as thought of the world. The reduction, on this reading, withdraws nothing. The certainties of common sense stay, as the constant theme of philosophy. You just see the ground you were already standing on.

@chapter The limit
@words text="The most important lesson which the reduction teaches us is the impossibility of a complete reduction."
Then the turn. The most important lesson which the reduction teaches us is the impossibility of a complete reduction.

@art perpetual-beginner
If we were absolute mind, there'd be no problem. But we're in the world, and we reflect inside the flow of time we're trying to grasp. So there's no thought which embraces all our thought. The philosopher is a perpetual beginner, and the reduction belongs to existential philosophy, not idealism.

@chapter A cube that flips
@art wire-cube
The lab gives the step back a shape. This cube is the lesson's illustration, not the Preface's. Draw a wire cube with its back square offset by half an edge. You saw a front face at once. But which face?

@math heading="Wire cube" items="reading A: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 edges@four in the front;;reading B: front and back squares exchanged@swap;;B projected: the same 12 segments as A@the same twelve;;readings the drawing fits: 2@two readings"
Count the edges: four in the front square, four in the back, four joining them, twelve. Now swap front and back. Projected, it draws the same twelve segments. So the drawing fits two readings, and the front was never in the lines. You supplied it.

@math heading="Solid cube" items="12 − 3 = 9 edges drawn@hides three;;reading B: a different 9@a different nine;;readings: 1@just one"
A solid cube hides three edges, so it draws nine. Reading B would hide three others and draw a different nine. So the solid drawing fits just one.

@art solid-cube
Opaque things settle what wire leaves open. Which three edges are hidden depends on which face is in front, so the drawing decides.

@chapter Easy mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The reduction doubts the world@doubts the world|The reduction can be finished@can be finished|The flip proves perception deceives@deceives"
Don't say the reduction doubts the world: nothing about the drawing was doubted. Don't say it can be finished: that needs a transparent consciousness we don't have. And don't say the flip proves perception deceives. Both readings fit exactly. The flip shows a contribution habit supplied, not an error.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Wire box 2 × 1 × 1" sub="how many edges? how many readings?"
Now try the box: a wire box, two by one by one. Count its edges, form reading B, and decide how many readings the drawing fits. Then name what you'd supplied without noticing.
