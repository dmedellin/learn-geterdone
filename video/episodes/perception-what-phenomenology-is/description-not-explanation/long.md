@chapter The huge moon
@title text="Description, Not Explanation" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Preface" art=thumb
Think of the last time you watched the full moon come up behind the roofs. It was enormous, low, almost close enough to touch.

@art moon-overhead
An hour later, high in the sky, it was an ordinary coin of light. Ask why, and someone will offer you an explanation. Merleau-Ponty's Preface asks you to do something plainer first.

@chapter What is phenomenology?
@words text="What is phenomenology?" sub="Preface, p. vii"
The Preface opens with a question that sounds as if it should have been settled long ago: what is phenomenology? Half a century after Husserl's first works, Merleau-Ponty says, there's still no answer.

@list heading="What looks like contradictions" items="It studies essences, and puts them back into existence@studies essences|It suspends the natural attitude, and finds the world already there@suspends|A rigorous science, and an account of lived space and time@rigorous science"
And he shows why. Phenomenology studies essences, and puts essences back into existence. It suspends the natural attitude, and yet holds that the world is always already there. It wants to be a rigorous science, and it describes space and time as we live them.

@words text="A manner or style of thinking"
So he doesn't give a definition. Phenomenology, he says, is practised and recognised as a manner or style of thinking, reached only through its own method. And the first theme of that method is description.

@chapter Three procedures
@quote text="It is a matter of describing, not of explaining or analysing." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Preface, p. ix"
Here's the demand. Describe. Don't explain, and don't analyse.

@list heading="Three ways to answer" items="Explain: say what caused it@To explain|Analyse: rebuild it from a subject's acts@to analyse|Describe: say what is given, as it is given@To describe"
To explain is to say what caused a thing. In the Preface's sense, to analyse is to go back from the world to the acts of a subject that would have put it together. To describe is to say what is given, as it is given, before either.

@chapter The map and the countryside
@art map-and-countryside
Husserl's slogan was back to the things themselves, and the Preface even calls it a foreswearing of science. But what's refused is a standpoint, not a result: taking yourself as nothing but a bit of the world, an object for biology or sociology.

@words text="Science: a second-order expression"
Everything you know, science included, is gained from your own point of view, from an experience without which science's symbols would mean nothing. So science is a second-order expression of the world you perceive.

@art map-and-countryside zoom=in
Think of a map. It isn't false, and you can steer by it. But you can read its sign for a forest only because you've been in one. The map depends on the countryside, and the countryside doesn't depend on the map.

@chapter The second opponent
@art constructed-world
Description rules out a second procedure too. Descartes and Kant went back from the world to consciousness, as the condition of there being anything at all. At its strongest it's simple: whatever world there is, it's a world for someone.

@words text="The real has to be described, not constructed or formed."
The Preface's objection is that this rebuilds the world from a subject outside experience. It offers a reconstruction where an account was needed. The real has to be described, not constructed or formed.

@art background-field
That's why perception isn't a kind of judgement. Your field is full of flickers and noises you place in the world at once, without confusing them with daydreams. Perception is the background from which all acts stand out.

@chapter The moon by numbers
@art angle-at-eye
Now the moon, with modern astronomy's figures. The angle something subtends at your eye is its size divided by its distance, in radians.

@math heading="Size ÷ distance" items="horizon: 3474 / 384400 = 1737/192200 ≈ 0.0090 rad@at the horizon;;overhead distance: 384400 − 6371 = 378029 km@one Earth radius;;overhead: 3474 / 378029 ≈ 0.0092 rad@overhead it's;;≈ 0.52° low, ≈ 0.53° high@about half a degree"
The moon is three thousand four hundred and seventy four kilometres across. At the horizon it's three hundred and eighty four thousand four hundred away, about nought point nought nine radians. Overhead it's one Earth radius nearer, so overhead it's about nought point nought nine two. Both are about half a degree.

@art moon-over-roofs
By the angle, the overhead moon is larger, by about two per cent. And it's the one that looks small. The figures answer how wide an angle the moon subtends. They leave open how large it looks over the roofs.

@chapter Where readers slip
@list heading="Three misreadings" items="Phenomenology rejects science@reject science|Description reports private feelings@private feelings|The first person means idealism@idealism"
Three misreadings to avoid. Phenomenology doesn't reject science: the figures stay, they just aren't the first word. Description isn't a report of private feelings: the huge moon is a moon over the roofs, not a sensation in you. And the first person isn't idealism: the subject is already in the world.
