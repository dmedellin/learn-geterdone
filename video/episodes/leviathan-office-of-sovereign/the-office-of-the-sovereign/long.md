@chapter What a sovereign is for
@title text="The Office of the Sovereign Representative" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 30" art=thumb
What is a sovereign for? Hobbes says his office lies in the end he was trusted with power for: the safety of the people. He answers for it to God, and to no one else.

@art general-providence
And safety doesn't mean bare preservation. It means all the other contentments of life a man gets by lawful industry. Not by care of each person, but by a general providence of public instruction and good laws. That's a great deal more than a night watchman.

@chapter Teaching the people
@art clean-paper
Most of the chapter is about instruction. The sovereign must keep his essential rights, and see the people taught their grounds. Some say the people can't learn. Hobbes says they're like clean paper, unless tainted by the powerful or by their doctors. If whole nations believe the mysteries of the church, they can learn the plainer duties of subjects.

@words text="Impunity maketh insolence; insolence hatred"
Then equal justice. The great should have no greater hope of impunity than the meaner sort. Impunity makes insolence, and insolence hatred.

@chapter Equal taxes
@quote text="the Equality whereof dependeth not on the Equality of riches, but on the Equality of the debt, that every man oweth to the Common-wealth for his defence." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 30"
Equal justice includes equal taxes. And their equality depends not on the equality of riches, but on the equality of the debt every man owes the commonwealth for his defence.

@art wages-of-the-sword
Taxes are the wages due to those who hold the public sword. Everyone gets the same protection, so the debt is the same, except that the rich, who have the service of the poor, may owe for many more than themselves. So equal imposition means equal charge on what each consumes.

@art frugal-and-idle
Why should a man who labours much and consumes little be charged more than one who lives idly and spends all he gets, when neither has more protection than the other?

@chapter Two rates, five people
@math heading="Five people, revenue 100" items="riches: 500 + 100 + 1000 + 60 + 340 = 2000@riches;;consumption: 50 + 100 + 200 + 60 + 90 = 500@consumption;;rate on riches: 100 ÷ 2000 = 1/20@one twentieth;;rate on consumption: 100 ÷ 500 = 1/5@one fifth"
The lab takes five sample people: the frugal, the idle, a lord, the poor and a merchant. Their riches total two thousand, and their consumption five hundred. To raise a hundred, the rate on riches is one twentieth, five percent. The rate on consumption is one fifth, twenty percent.

@math heading="Who pays what" items="frugal (500, 50): 25 on riches, 10 on consumption@frugal;;idle (100, 100): 5 on riches, 20 on consumption@idle;;poor (60, 60): 3 on riches, 12 on consumption@poor man"
The frugal man pays twenty five on riches, but only ten on consumption. The idle man pays five on riches, and twenty on consumption. And the poor man pays three on riches, but twelve on consumption.

@words text="Labourer 180, 20 · Idler 20, 30 · revenue 20" sub="riches: 18 and 2 · consumption: 8 and 12"
In the pair preset, a labourer and an idler raise twenty. On riches the labourer pays eighteen and the idler two. On consumption, eight and twelve. The man who spares pays less, which is Hobbes's pair.

@chapter The objection
@art poor-mans-share
But the objection is in those figures too. The poor man spends everything he has, so a tax on consumption takes twelve of his sixty, against three on riches. Later writers made ability to pay the test. Hobbes's answer is that tax is a wage for protection, and life is equally dear to rich and poor. The lab leaves that open.

@chapter Good laws
@words text="A good law: needful and perspicuous" sub="no law can be unjust"
By a good law, Hobbes says, he doesn't mean a just law, for no law can be unjust. A good law is needful for the good of the people, and perspicuous, that is, clear.

@art hedges-on-road
Laws are like hedges, set not to stop travellers but to keep them in the way. And punishments should be severe for the crimes most dangerous to the public, and lenient to those who were led astray.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Equal tax means an equal sum@The first|Consumption tax always hits the rich@The second|The office is the sovereign's convenience@And the third"
The first slip is thinking an equal tax means the same sum from everyone. It's one rate on what each consumes. The second is thinking a tax on consumption always charges the rich more. The lord pays fifty on riches and forty on consumption. And the third is reading the office as only the sovereign's convenience.
