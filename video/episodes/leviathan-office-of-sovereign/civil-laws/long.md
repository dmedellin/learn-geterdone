@chapter The club rule
@title text="Of Civil Laws" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 26" art=thumb
A club has a rule against being late, and the committee can change it at the next meeting. Is the committee bound by the rule in the meantime?

@art bound-and-released
Hobbes says not really. A rule its makers can unmake at will is a rule they apply to others.

@chapter What law is
@words text="Law is not counsel, but command"
He starts with a definition. Civil laws are the rules the commonwealth has commanded you, by word, writing or other sign of its will, to use for telling right from wrong. Law in general is not counsel, but command. And whatever follows from the definition by necessary consequence, he says, must be accepted.

@art single-legislator
The first consequence is that the sovereign is the only legislator. The commonwealth can act only through its representative. And a law is abrogated only by another law forbidding it to be put in execution.

@chapter The argument
@quote text="For he is free, that can be free when he will: Nor is it possible for any person to be bound to himselfe; because he that can bind, can release" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 26"
The second consequence is the famous one. The sovereign isn't subject to the civil laws. Having power to make and repeal them, he can free himself when he pleases. For he is free, that can be free when he will. And no one can be bound to himself, because he that can bind can release.

@math heading="Three letters" items="p: he can repeal any civil law at will@let p;;q: he can free himself from any at will@q is;;r: he is free from the civil laws@and r"
The lab writes this with three letters. Let p be: the sovereign can repeal any civil law when he pleases. q is: he can free himself from any civil law when he pleases. And r: he is free from the civil laws.

@math heading="Hobbes preset" items="premises: p,  p → q,  q → r@the premises;;conclusion: r@conclusion;;8 rows, no counterexample: valid@eight rows"
The premises are p, if p then q, and if q then r. The conclusion is r. There are eight rows of true and false, and no row makes all the premises true and r false. So the argument is valid.

@chapter Taking premises away
@art missing-link
Now remove each premise in turn. In the Coke preset, drop p. The checker finds one counterexample, with p, q and r all false. Both conditionals are true there, and the conclusion isn't.

@words text="Drop q → r: counterexample with p, q true, r false"
In the bare preset, keep p and if p then q, but drop if q then r. Now there's a counterexample with p and q true. Each premise is needed, and the lab shows which one does which work.

@chapter The common lawyers
@art old-law-book
So who denies which premise? Hobbes had the common lawyers in view, and Sir Edward Coke above all, who died in sixteen thirty four after defending the common law against James the first and Charles the first. For them the law is older and wiser than any ruler's will. A king can't repeal what he didn't make.

@words text="An artificial perfection of reason?" sub="Coke · vs · the reason and command of the artificial man"
Coke made law an artificial perfection of reason, gotten by long study and experience. Hobbes says it's the reason of our artificial man, the commonwealth, and his command, that makes law. The lawyers deny the first premise. That's what the Coke preset shows. Without it, the other two don't deliver the conclusion.

@art endless-judges
Weigh both sides. Hobbes's reason, in Chapter twenty nine, is that a law above the sovereign sets a judge above him, and another above that, without end. The lawyers, and later Locke, answer that a ruler may be under a law he can't repeal, like the terms he was trusted on. The checker only says whether the conclusion follows.

@chapter What else follows
@art custom-and-silence
Custom is law only by the sovereign's silence. It isn't the length of time that makes the authority, but his will. While he doesn't forbid a custom, it's law, and no longer.

@list heading="From the definition" items="A law not made known is no law@not made known|The laws of nature: the only unwritten laws@unwritten|Lex and Jus: obligation and liberty@Lex and Jus"
A law not made known is no law to someone who couldn't know it, and the laws of nature are the only unwritten laws. And Lex and Jus, law and right, are as different as obligation and liberty.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Valid settles the question@The first|Free of civil law means above all law@The second|Custom is a rival source of law@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a valid argument settles the question. Valid isn't true. The second is reading free of the civil laws as above all law. He's still under the laws of nature. And the third is treating custom as a rival source of law.
