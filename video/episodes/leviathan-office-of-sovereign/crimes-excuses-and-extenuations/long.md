@chapter Sin and crime
@title text="Crimes, Excuses and Extenuations" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 27" art=thumb
Hobbes opens with a distinction that's stayed in ordinary speech. A sin is any breach of a law, and any contempt of the lawgiver, even just the purpose to transgress. A crime needs a deed or a word that a judge can be shown.

@art sin-and-crime
So every crime is a sin, but not every sin a crime. To intend to steal is a sin, even if it never shows. And where there's no civil law, there's no crime, because there's no one to accuse. There's still sin, because the law of nature is eternal.

@art imagining-mind
He's gentle about the borderline. Enjoying the thought of something you'd like, if it were real, is so natural that calling it a sin would make it a sin to be a man. You're sinful when you resolve to act, and a criminal when you do.

@chapter Where crimes come from
@list heading="The sources of crime" items="A defect of understanding@defect of the understanding|An error in reasoning@error in reasoning|A sudden force of the passions@force of the passions"
The source of every crime is some defect of the understanding, some error in reasoning, or some sudden force of the passions.

@list heading="Ignorance" items="Of the law of nature: no excuse@law of nature|Of an undeclared civil law: excuses@civil law|Of the sovereign: no excuse@the sovereign|Of the penalty: no excuse@the penalty"
Ignorance of the law of nature excuses no one with the use of reason. Ignorance of the civil law excuses only where it wasn't declared so you could know it. Ignorance of the sovereign excuses no one. And ignorance of the penalty excuses no one either. But here comes the interesting part.

@chapter The invitation
@quote text="For the punishment foreknown, if it be not great enough to deterre men from the action, is an invitement to it" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 27"
For the punishment foreknown, if it be not great enough to deter men from the action, is an invitement to it. Men compare the benefit of their injustice with the harm of their punishment, and by necessity of nature choose what appears best.

@art scales-gain-penalty
So a man is excused any penalty heavier than the law named, because it's the law that tempted him. A thief offered a fine below what he'll gain isn't choosing wickedness over virtue. He's doing a sum.

@chapter The lab's sum
@art hope-of-impunity
The lab adds one thing that's hinted at but isn't Hobbes's: the offender isn't sure to be punished. Covetousness and ambition are always pressing, Hobbes says, and whenever the hope of impunity appears, their effects proceed.

@words text="Expected penalty = chance · penalty" sub="the chance is the lab's, not Hobbes's"
The lab makes that hope a number, the chance the penalty is inflicted. Multiply the chance by the penalty, and compare it with the gain.

@math heading="A fraud gains 100, penalty 150" items="always: 1 · 150 = 150 > 100@always;;one in two: 1/2 · 150 = 75 < 100@one time in two;;must exceed: 100 ÷ 1/2 = 200@two hundred"
Take a fraud that gains a hundred, with a penalty of a hundred and fifty. If he's always punished, the expected penalty is a hundred and fifty, and the crime doesn't pay. Caught one time in two, it's seventy five, below the gain, so it pays. To deter at that chance, the penalty must exceed two hundred.

@math heading="The rich man: one in four" items="1/4 · 150 = 75/2 = 37.50@a quarter;;must exceed: 100 ÷ 1/4 = 400@four hundred"
Now a man who can corrupt the court, caught one time in four. A quarter of a hundred and fifty is thirty seven fifty. The penalty would need to exceed four hundred. Every halving of the chance doubles the penalty needed.

@art corrupted-scales
Hobbes saw this. Vain glory leads the rich to think penalties shouldn't fall on them as on the poor, so they venture on crimes, hoping to corrupt justice or buy a pardon. That's the rich preset in words. Those who hope not to be seen, he says, are like children who think all is hid when they hide their own eyes.

@chapter Degrees of crime
@art crooked-lines
Last, degrees. Crime is greater from presumption of strength or riches than from hope of escape, greater against conscience, and greater when it injures many. All crimes equally deserve the name injustice, as all deviation from a straight line is equally crooked. But they aren't equally unjust: killing is above maiming, and maiming above theft.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A harsher penalty always deters@The first|The chance is a fact about the offender@The second|A crime is a sin that also harms@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a harsher penalty always deters. The chance counts as much as the size. The second is treating the chance as a fact. It's what the offender believes. And the third is defining crime by harm. It's defined by the civil law.
