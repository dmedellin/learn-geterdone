@chapter What punishment is
@title text="Punishments and Rewards" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 28" art=thumb
When is a penalty really a punishment? Hobbes gives a definition, then draws eleven consequences that say what isn't one. Two of them are sums, and the lab tests them.

@quote text="A PUNISHMENT, is an Evill inflicted by publique Authority, on him that hath done, or omitted that which is Judged by the same Authority to be a Transgression of the Law" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 28"
A punishment is an evil inflicted by public authority, on someone that same authority has judged to have broken the law, to the end that men's wills may be better disposed to obedience.

@list heading="Piece by piece" items="An evil@An evil|By public authority@public authority|After a judgement@judged|To dispose men to obey@obey"
Take it piece by piece. An evil, so being left out of favour doesn't count. By public authority, so a neighbour's revenge doesn't. After he's been judged, so harm first and judgement later isn't punishment. And to dispose men to obey. Its aim, Hobbes says, is not revenge but terror.

@words text="Desert, or effect?" sub="Kant: never merely as a means"
A reader brought up on Kant will want to stop here. Kant held that you may be punished only because you committed a crime, never as a means to some other good. That's the main objection to Hobbes, and the chapter doesn't really answer it.

@chapter Where the right comes from
@art right-left-not-given
Where did the right to punish come from? Not from the subjects. You give away the right to defend another, but never to defend yourself, so you can't give anyone a right to hurt you. The sovereign's right is the old right of nature, which everyone else laid down. It was not given, but left to him, and to him only.

@chapter Price and hostility
@art price-tag
Now the two sums. The seventh inference: if the harm is less than the benefit that follows the crime, it isn't a punishment at all. It's rather the price, or redemption, of the crime, because it fails of its end and works the opposite way.

@art unexpected-addition
The eighth: if the law prescribes a penalty and a greater one is inflicted, the excess is not punishment, but an act of hostility. Declaring a lesser penalty took away the terror of a greater one, so the unexpected addition is no part of the punishment.

@math heading="A crime gains 100" items="price: 60 < 100, so a price@sixty;;punishment: 150 > 100, and 150 = 150@a hundred and fifty is inflicted;;excess: 400 − 150 = 250 over@four hundred;;hostility is the 250, not the 150@the two fifty"
Each crime gains a hundred, punished for certain. Sixty prescribed and sixty inflicted is below the gain, so it's a price. Then a hundred and fifty is inflicted, as prescribed: that's a punishment. Now four hundred is inflicted against a hundred and fifty prescribed. The hundred and fifty is punishment, and the two fifty over it is hostility.

@chapter Enemies and the innocent
@art declared-enemy
The list goes on. Harm for a deed done before the law is hostility. Harm to a declared enemy isn't punishment. And a subject who deliberately denies the sovereign's authority suffers as an enemy, since rebellion is but war renewed. That's the hardest point in the chapter, and Hobbes doesn't soften it.

@words text="Punishing the innocent" sub="against three laws of nature"
Punishing innocent subjects, by contrast, he calls contrary to three laws of nature: that revenge look only to a future good, that ingratitude be forbidden, and that equity be kept.

@chapter Rewards, and the name of the book
@list heading="Reward" items="Salary: by contract@salary|Grace: to encourage service@grace|Paid from fear: a sacrifice@sacrifice"
A reward is either a salary, owed by contract, or a grace, given to encourage service. A benefit given to a subject because he's feared is neither. It's a sacrifice, and it teaches others to extort.

@art nerves-and-tendons
And the chapter ends with the book's name. Punishment and reward are the nerves and tendons that move the limbs and joints of a commonwealth.

@art leviathan-king-of-proud
Having shown man's nature, whose pride compelled him to government, Hobbes turns to the great power of his governor, whom he compared to Leviathan, from Job forty one, where God calls that creature King of the Proud.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Any penalty the law names is a punishment@The first|The whole harm becomes hostility@The second|Hostility means wrong@And the third"
The first slip is thinking any penalty the law names is a punishment. Sixty against a gain of a hundred is a price. The second is thinking the whole harm becomes hostility. Only the excess does. And the third is reading hostility as wrong. It means a harm that isn't punishment.
