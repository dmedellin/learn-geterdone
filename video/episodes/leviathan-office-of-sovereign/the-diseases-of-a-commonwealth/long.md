@chapter A physician's chapter
@title text="The Dissolution of a Commonwealth" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 29" art=thumb
Nothing can be immortal which mortals make, Hobbes says. But a commonwealth could at least be spared the diseases that come from inside. And he treats them the way a physician would.

@art crazy-building
The fault, he says, is in men as makers, not as matter. Without a very able architect, they can only be put together into a crazy building. So the diseases come in a long list, each one compared to an infirmity of a natural body.

@chapter The first diseases
@art weak-crown
First, imperfect institution: taking a crown with less power than peace needs. The barons William Rufus strengthened later rebelled, backed by the French. And the Roman style, the Senate and People of Rome, let neither claim the whole power, which brought the Gracchi, Marius and Sulla, Pompey and Caesar.

@list heading="Six seditious doctrines" items="Each man judges good and evil@judge of good|Acting against conscience is sin@conscience|Faith comes by inspiration@inspiration|The sovereign is under the civil laws@civil laws|Property excludes the sovereign@property|Sovereign power may be divided@divided"
Second, six seditious doctrines. That every private man is judge of good and evil. That acting against your conscience is sin. That faith comes by inspiration. That the sovereign is subject to the civil laws. That your property excludes the sovereign. And that sovereign power may be divided. Powers divided, he says, mutually destroy each other.

@art mad-dog-bite
Then imitation, of neighbours and of the ancients. Reading the Greek and Roman writers, who make killing a tyrant look praiseworthy, he likens to a mad dog's bite, which brings a thirst for water and a horror of it at once. A monarchy bitten that way abhors the strong ruler it most needs.

@chapter Two masters
@quote text="there must needs be two Common-wealths, of one & the same Subjects; which is a Kingdome divided in it selfe, and cannot stand." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 29"
Now the worst. Where one is sovereign and another supreme, one makes laws and another canons, there must needs be two commonwealths of one and the same subjects, a kingdom divided in itself, which cannot stand.

@art two-masters
The quarrel is the clergy's claim to a supremacy that isn't the sovereign's. If the spiritual power declares what's sin, it declares what's law. And then every subject must obey two masters, which is impossible. The spiritual side never lacks a party either, because the fear of darkness and ghosts is greater than other fears.

@chapter A game of chicken
@math heading="Crown and Church: yield or insist" items="both yield: 3 each@both yield;;one insists: 5 and 1@one insists;;both insist: civil war, 0 each@civil war"
The lab writes this as a game, with payoffs the lesson invents. Crown and Church each yield or insist on the last word. If both yield, each gets three. If one insists and the other yields, it's five and one. If both insist, it's civil war, zero each. That's the game of chicken, with two pure equilibria: one insists, and the other yields.

@math heading="The mixed equilibrium" items="yield: 3 − 2t@three minus two t;;insist: 5 − 5t@five minus five t;;equal when t = 2/3@two times in three;;war: 2/3 · 2/3 = 4/9@four ninths"
There's also a mixed one. Let t be the chance the other insists. Yielding is worth three minus two t, insisting five minus five t. They're equal when each insists two times in three. Then civil war comes with chance four ninths. Nothing in the table says which outcome will happen.

@chapter Changing the table
@art sword-behind-claim
Now put a sword behind the Crown. Against an insisting Church, it gets four, and the Church loses two. Insisting dominates for the Crown: five beats three, and four beats one. The Church's best answer is to yield, one against minus two. One equilibrium. That's Hobbes's remedy, a single sovereign no rival may resist.

@words text="Cheap war: 2 each" sub="insisting dominates for both · a dilemma"
Or make war cheap, two each. Now insisting dominates for both, and the only equilibrium is war, worse for both than peace. That's a prisoner's dilemma. It's the lesson's picture, not Hobbes's.

@art worms-and-ague
The rest of the chapter is a physician's list. Want of money is an ague. Monopolies, a pleurisy. Great towns, worms in the entrails. The final dissolution comes when an enemy's victory leaves no protection, and every man may protect himself.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Hobbes predicts the table's outcome@The first|The sword preset proves one sovereign is best@The second|Divided power is the only disease@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Hobbes predicts the table's outcome. The payoffs are ours. The second is reading the sword preset as a proof. The payoffs were chosen to give one answer. And the third is thinking divided power is the only disease.
