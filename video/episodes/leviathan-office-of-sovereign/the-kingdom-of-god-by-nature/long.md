@chapter Four grounds for rule
@title text="The Kingdom of God by Nature" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 31" art=thumb
Why might a ruler have the right to rule? Because he made us, like a father. Because he's good, like a benefactor. Because we agreed. Or because nothing could stop him. For human rulers, Hobbes has argued from agreement. For God, he takes the fourth.

@quote text="The Right of Nature, whereby God reigneth over men, and punisheth those that break his Lawes, is to be derived, not from his Creating them as if he required obedience, as of Gratitude for his benefits; but from his Irresistible Power." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 31"
God's right to reign over men and punish those who break his laws is to be derived not from creating them, as if he required obedience out of gratitude, but from his irresistible power.

@chapter The argument
@art laid-down-right
Here's the case. By nature everyone had a right to everything, including the right to rule all the rest. But no one could get that by force, so for safety they laid it down and set up sovereigns by consent.

@art irresistible-power
But if any man had irresistible power, there'd be no reason he shouldn't rule by it. To whoever has that power, dominion over all men adheres naturally. God's power is irresistible. So the kingdom is his not as Creator and gracious, but as omnipotent.

@math heading="Two letters" items="p: God's power over men is irresistible@let p;;q: God has the right to reign over men@let q;;premises: p,  p → q;  conclusion: q@if p then q"
The lab writes this with two letters. Let p be: God's power over men is irresistible. Let q be: God has the right to reign over men and afflict them at his pleasure. The link, if p then q, carries the reasoning: a being with irresistible power has no reason to lay down his right, so he keeps it.

@math heading="Power and strip" items="power: p, p → q ⊢ q: 4 rows, no counterexample@four rows;;strip: p alone ⊬ q: counterexample p true, q false@p alone"
In the power preset, with p and if p then q, there are four rows and no counterexample, so it's valid. In the strip preset, remove the link. Then p alone has one counterexample, with p true and q false. So the link is doing the work.

@chapter The rival ground
@art creator-and-creature
Now the rival. Let c be: God created men. The creation preset, c and if c then q, is just as valid. If creating gives a right to rule, and God created us, he has it. Validity doesn't choose between them.

@words text="Power without right?" sub="grant p, deny p → q"
Hobbes denies the creation link. A reader who grounds God's right in creation or goodness grants p, but denies if p then q. Irresistible power by itself, they'd say, gives a fact, not a right. Their case, power without the right, is exactly the strip preset's row.

@math heading="Mixed preset: p, c → q" items="counterexample: p true, c false, q false@one counterexample"
And the mixed preset shows the other half of their view. Power joined only to the creation link doesn't yield q. Its one counterexample has c false. On that view, power does no work, and the right rests on creation.

@chapter Job
@art whirlwind
Hobbes also has the text on his side. Job's friends argued from his suffering to his sin, and Job defended his innocence. God settled it not by Job's sin, but by his own power: where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? He approved Job's innocence, and reproved his friends.

@words text="Philosophical truth · or pious intention?"
The rest works out consequences. Subjects of this kingdom are those who believe God governs, gives precepts and rewards. To dispute God's nature dishonours him, since in speaking of God we look not to philosophical truth but to pious intention.

@art one-worship
And a commonwealth, being one person, owes God one worship, which is why public worship is uniform. What the sovereign ordains as signs of honour, private men must take as such.

@chapter The end of Part Two
@art plato-republic
Part Two ends on doubt and hope. Hobbes is at the point of thinking his labour as useless as Plato's commonwealth. But he hopes it may fall to a sovereign who'll turn this truth of speculation into the utility of practice.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Valid shows the right comes from power@The first|Hobbes says God isn't good@The second|Job proves the whole claim@And the third"
The first slip is thinking validity shows God's right comes from power. The creation argument is just as valid. The second is reading Hobbes as saying God isn't good. Goodness just isn't the ground. And the third is taking Job as proof of the whole claim.
