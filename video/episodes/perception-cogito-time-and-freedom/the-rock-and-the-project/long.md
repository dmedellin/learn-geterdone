@chapter A valley path
@title text="The Rock and the Project" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Three, chapter 3" art=thumb
Picture a walk along a valley path with boulders beside it. If you mean to scramble up one, each is suddenly easy or hard, a handhold or a wall. If you're only walking past, they're scenery.

@art valley-boulders
That's a fair picture of the theory the chapter on freedom opens with. Its first pages are in that theory's voice, not Merleau-Ponty's. I can't be free in some actions and determined in others. And motives don't act on me.

@words text="my decision lends the motive its force"
The alleged motive doesn't burden my decision. On the contrary, my decision lends the motive its force.

@chapter Whose theory?
@words text="Sartre, Being and Nothingness, 1943" sub="cited in the footnotes; named once in the text"
The footnotes cite Jean-Paul Sartre's Being and Nothingness, and he's named once in the text. Sartre argued that freedom is the very being of consciousness, total in every act, and that the world's resistance appears only in the light of a chosen end.

@art fatigue-road
At its strength the view explains a lot: why the same fatigue halts one walker and not another, why deliberation often follows a decision already made, why giving up a plan can bring relief.

@chapter The rock
@art rock-faces
The rock is Sartre's own example. An unclimbable rock face, large or small, vertical or slanting, has no meaning for anyone who isn't intending to surmount it. The rocks are just forms a subject's projects carve out of the mass of being.

@math heading="Rocks of 1 m, 2 m, 3 m" items="climb, capacity 2 m: obstacle 3 m@climb preset;;walk past: none@walking past"
The lab's model makes it a count, with rocks of one, two and three metres. In the climb preset, with a capacity of two metres, only the rock strictly taller than two is an obstacle: the three metre rock. Change the project to walking past, and there's none.

@chapter No freedom without a field
@words text="no freedom without a field"
The theory runs into trouble of its own. If the slave is as free in fear as in breaking his chains, there's no free action to point to. To choose is to commit oneself, and a freedom just as free the next instant commits nothing. There's no freedom without a field.

@words text="one rock will appear as an obstacle, and another … as a means"
So the chapter grants the first half. Being unclimbable is conferred on a rock only by a project of climbing and a human presence. But one and the same project given, one rock will appear as an obstacle, and another, more negotiable, as a means.

@chapter Micromégas
@quote text="Whether or not I have decided to climb them, these mountains appear high to me, because they exceed my body's power to take them in its stride, and, even if I have just read Micromégas, I cannot so contrive it that they are small for me." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Three, chapter 3, p. 511"
Now the passage. Whether or not I've decided to climb them, these mountains appear high, because they exceed my body's power to take them in its stride. Even if I've just read Micromegas, I can't make them small for me.

@art sirius-giant
Micromegas is Voltaire's tale of a giant from Sirius who finds Earth's mountains tiny. I can take his view in thought. But beneath the thinking subject there's a natural self which doesn't budge from its terrestrial situation. Even from Sirius I borrow from the Earth: I say the Alps are molehills.

@math heading="Body preset: level 2 m, no project" items="project: none@project tile;;level: 3 m@level tile"
The body preset gives no project, and a general level of two metres, the lesson's illustrative figure. The project tile prints none, since nothing is to be climbed. The level tile prints three metres. That rock exceeds the level whatever the project.

@chapter General intentions
@art dot-pairs
These are general intentions: valuations a body makes by having hands, feet and a stride, before any decision. They're shared with everyone organised as I am. That's why a row of dots is always seen as six pairs. There is an autochthonous significance of the world, springing from the ground of our bodily dealings.

@art sediment-layers
Fatigue halts me and not my companion, because he likes the heat of the road. The chapter accepts that and adds: an attitude often confirmed acquires a favoured status for us, a sedimentation of our life. Whether the theory answered is Sartre's own, or a sharpened version, is disputed.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Obstacles exist only for a project@only for a project|The chapter brings back obstacles in themselves@in themselves|The body's level is a measured fact@measured fact"
Don't say the chapter agrees obstacles exist only for a project. Don't think it brings back obstacles in themselves; there are none. And don't take the body's level for a measured fact. Two metres is the model's simplification.
