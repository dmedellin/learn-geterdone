@chapter What survives doubt
@title text="The Cogito and the Certainty of Seeing" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Three, chapter 1" art=thumb
Part Three opens by asking what Descartes's I think, therefore I am becomes after a book about the perceiving body.

@art doubting-desk
In his Meditations of sixteen forty one, Descartes doubted everything he could: the senses, his own body, even arithmetic. One thing survives. While I doubt, I think, and while I think, I am. And even if I'm dreaming, it's certain that I seem to see light. The seeing may be false. The seeming, he holds, cannot.

@art boulevard-trees
The chapter begins somewhere else, at a desk. The author is thinking of the cogito, wanting to finish this book, feeling the coolness of the paper under his hand, and seeing the trees of the boulevard through the window.

@chapter The ash-tray
@quote text="I am not sure that there is over there an ash-tray or a pipe, but I am sure that I think I see an ash-tray or a pipe. … If I see an ash-tray, in the full sense of the word see, there must be an ash-tray there, and I cannot forego this assertion. To see is to see something." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Three, chapter 1, pp. 435–436"
Here's Descartes's distinction, in Merleau-Ponty's words. I'm not sure there's an ash tray over there, but I'm sure I think I see one. Can the two come apart? On the contrary, it is impossible. If I see an ash tray, in the full sense, there must be one there. To see is to see something.

@art ashtray-pipe
Perception can't be set apart from the thing it's aimed at. If there's nothing there, I say I didn't really see. Taken as the mere impression of seeing, the thought of seeing is certain only as a possibility. So it's certain only so long as actual sight is equally so.

@chapter The cogito as an inference
@math heading="Bare: p ∴ q" items="p: I think · q: I exist@Let p be;;4 rows, 1 counterexample: p true, q false@Of four rows;;verdict: invalid@invalid"
The lab tests reading the cogito as an inference. Let p be I think, and q be I exist. From p, conclude q. Of four rows, one has the premise true and the conclusion false: p true, q false. So it's invalid.

@math heading="Major: p, p → q ∴ q" items="0 counterexamples@no row;;modus ponens@modus ponens;;needs: whatever thinks exists@whatever thinks"
Add the premise if I think, I exist, and no row is left that refutes it. That's modus ponens. But the premise an inference would really need is general: whatever thinks exists.

@art two-readings
Descartes was asked about this. In the Second Replies he denied the cogito is deduced by a syllogism. It's recognized by a simple intuition, and the general premise is learned from one's own case. In the Principles he allows it as a presupposed notion. Readers still argue which states his view.

@chapter The chapter's reading
@words text="it is not because I think I am that I am certain of my existence"
The chapter reads it neither way. It is not because I think I am that I am certain of my existence. The certainty of my thoughts comes from their actually being performed. I'm sure I love by loving, and that I doubt by doubting.

@math heading="Seeing preset" items="s → t: if I see, I think I see@if I see;;t ∴ s: affirming the consequent@affirming;;countercase: t true, s false@that row"
Now the distinction itself. s is I see an ash tray, t is I think I see one. If I see, I think I see. Going from t to s is affirming the consequent, with one counterexample: t true, s false. Descartes needs that row to be possible.

@art table-rows
Logic finds nothing wrong with that row. The chapter's objection is that in it, the thought of seeing is just the impression of seeing, without the certainty Descartes wanted. The row is a line in a truth table, not anyone's experience.

@chapter False feelings
@words text="Besides true love, there is false or illusory love"
Then the objector: a feeling, considered in itself, is always true once it is felt. The reply: besides true love, there is false or illusory love. Not a lie I tell. I was really committed, and only after disillusionment do I find beneath it boredom, habit, a likeness to someone else.

@art isolde-book
Scheler's example is the girl who feels the feelings of Isolde or Juliet and pours them into her life, with no way to find the literary element until a feeling of her own arrives. And discovering you're in love works in reverse. The love was lived, not known, from start to finish.

@words text="existence is action"
So is the cogito impossible now? No. I can be sure of willing, loving or believing provided I actually do. Doubting is certain in the act of doubting something. The cogito is the recognition of that.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The cogito is an inference from thinking to being@an inference|The chapter gives up the cogito@gives up|The counterexample row is an experience@an experience"
Don't say the cogito is an inference from thinking to being. Don't think the chapter gives up the cogito; its sense changes. And don't take the counterexample row for an experience.
