@chapter A school proof
@title text="The Triangle" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Three, chapter 1" art=thumb
You were probably shown this proof at school. Draw a triangle, and through its top corner, draw a line parallel to the base.

@art parallel-apex
The two angles the new line makes on either side of the corner equal the two base angles, because they're alternate angles across parallel lines. And the three angles at the corner lie along one straight line, so they make two right angles.

@math heading="Angles of 60° and 70°" items="third angle: 180 − 130 = 50°@a hundred and eighty minus;;along the parallel: 60 + 50 + 70 = 180°@along the parallel"
Take angles of sixty and seventy degrees. The third angle is a hundred and eighty minus a hundred and thirty, fifty. Along the parallel, sixty plus fifty plus seventy is a hundred and eighty.

@chapter Pure thought?
@art child-drawing
Why geometry? Because feeling turned out not to be transparent, and the objection now is that pure thought, at least, must coincide with itself. The construction isn't like a child's drawing, which is a house, then a boat, then a man. From start to finish, it has a triangle in view.

@words text="one sum of angles, two groupings"
On Wertheimer's account, the proof brings one sum of angles into two groupings, and sees it as each in turn. With Gurwitsch, the first grouping survives while the second forms. So, says the objector, truth is impossible without a mind that possesses itself absolutely.

@chapter Not analytic
@words text="The necessity of the proof is not an analytic necessity"
The reply grants there's an act here. Then it asks what kind. The necessity of the proof is not an analytic necessity. There is no definition of a triangle which includes in advance the properties to be demonstrated, nor the steps that lead to them.

@art kant-concept
Kant used this very proof. A philosopher could analyse the concept of a triangle for ever and find nothing about its angles. The geometer constructs the figure in pure intuition. The comparison is the lesson's, not the chapter's. Merleau-Ponty agrees the concept isn't enough. They part on where the construction happens.

@chapter A gesture
@quote text="The construction makes explicit the possibilities of the triangle, considered not in the light of its definition and as a pure idea, but as a configuration and as the pole towards which my movements are directed. The conclusion follows of necessity from the hypothesis because, in the act of constructing, the geometer has already experienced the possibility of the transition." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Three, chapter 1, p. 449"
Words like on, through, apex and extend mean something only for a triangle in a perceptual field, oriented up and down, right and left. The construction makes explicit the triangle's possibilities as a configuration, a pole of my movements. The geometer has already experienced the possibility of the transition.

@art lines-of-force
The construction is a gesture. The lines are the outward expression of an intention. The triangle, before I drew anything, was traversed by lines of force, full of directions not traced out yet possible.

@words text="The subject of geometry is a motor subject"
So the subject of geometry is a motor subject. The body isn't an object, and its movement isn't a mere change of place. It's an original way of being directed at things.

@chapter The lab's parallel
@math heading="Classic preset, moving the parallel" items="through C: 60° and 70°@through C;;through A: 70° and 50°@through A;;through B: 60° and 50°@through B;;every time: 180°@every time"
The lab moves the parallel. Through C, the alternate angles are sixty and seventy. Through A, seventy and fifty. Through B, sixty and fifty. Every time, the three angles along the parallel make a hundred and eighty.

@art three-parallels
The construction changes, and the triangle doesn't. That's the chapter's point that the first grouping of the angles survives while the second one forms.

@math heading="Other presets" items="right: 90°, 30° → 60°@ninety and thirty;;thin: 1°, 1° → 178°@one and one;;refuse: 100°, 90° → no triangle@a hundred and ninety"
Ninety and thirty give a third angle of sixty. One and one give a hundred and seventy eight, a sliver. A hundred and ninety asks for more than two right angles, and the lab refuses. Notice it computes the third angle from the theorem itself, so it illustrates and proves nothing.

@art perceived-triangle
And essence? I believe a triangle always has this sum because I've experienced a real triangle, which presents itself as having all it will ever display. It is from the world of perception that I borrow the notion of essence.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The sum follows from the definition@the definition|The proof is the hand's movement@hand's movement|Geometry is measuring drawn triangles@measuring"
Don't say the sum follows from the definition; no definition contains the parallel. Don't say the proof is the hand's movement; it's a gesture expressing an intention. And don't think the chapter makes geometry a matter of measuring drawn triangles.
