@chapter On the bridge
@title text="Time Is Not in Things" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Three, chapter 2" art=thumb
Stand on a bridge and watch the water. It came down from the hills, it's under you now, and it'll reach the sea. Nothing seems more natural than to say time flows the same way.

@art glacier-river
The chapter on temporality begins exactly there. The water rolling by was made ready a few days ago in the mountains, with the melting of the glacier. If time is like a river, it flows from the past towards the present and the future. But this often repeated metaphor is in reality extremely confused.

@chapter A hidden witness
@art witness-bank
Why confused? In the objective world, the melting snow and what follows aren't successive events at all. To say the glacier produced the water passing now, I need a witness tied to a spot, who saw the snow melt and followed the water down.

@quote text="Change presupposes a certain position which I take up and from which I see things in procession before me: there are no events without someone to whom they happen and whose finite perspective is the basis of their individuality. Time presupposes a view of time." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Three, chapter 2, p. 477"
Events are shapes cut out by a finite observer. Change presupposes a position from which I see things in procession. There are no events without someone to whom they happen. Time presupposes a view of time.

@words text="you cannot step into the same river twice"
The image is old. Heraclitus is remembered for saying you can't step into the same river twice. The metaphor has lasted only because we slip a witness into the river.

@chapter Bank and boat
@art storm-horizon
Put the witness in openly, and the relations reverse. On the bank, the water already carried by sinks into the past, and what's to come is on the side of the source, like a storm on the horizon.

@math heading="Bank preset: observer at 4" items="water w1 … w7, source at 1@seven parcels;;past: w5, w6, w7@past;;future: w1, w2, w3@future"
The lab counts it. Seven parcels of water, at positions one to seven, the source at one, and an observer on the bank at four. The past is w five, six and seven, the water gone by. The future is w one, two and three, still on its way.

@art boat-current
Now put him in a boat carried by the current. He's moving downstream towards his future, and the course of time becomes the landscape rolling by. The chapter puts the boatman's future in the landscape, and the lab, with only positions, counts the water ahead.

@math heading="Current preset" items="past: w1, w2, w3@past is;;future: w5, w6, w7@future is"
So the past is w one, two and three, and the future is w five, six and seven. The same water, the opposite answer.

@chapter No observer
@math heading="None preset" items="past: none@past none;;future: none@future none;;each parcel at its place@its place"
Now take the observer away. Past none. Future none. And the world still lists every parcel at its place.

@words text="What is past or future for me is present in the world"
That's the claim. Within things, the water that'll flow by tomorrow is at this moment at its source. The water just passed is a little further down. What is past or future for me is present in the world.

@words text="The objective world is too much of a plenum for there to be time"
Leibniz called the objective world a momentary mind, and Augustine needed a presence of past and future besides the present. The chapter reads it its own way. The objective world is too much of a plenum for there to be time. What being lacks is not being: elsewhere, formerly, tomorrow.

@chapter Traces and idealism
@art ink-stain
Moving time into consciousness doesn't help if consciousness is a series of nows. Bergson refuted the brain theory of memory, and the chapter turns that on his unconscious memories too. The table bears my initials and an ink stain. They're present, and point to the past only for someone who already has a sense of it.

@art spread-out
The idealist's escape: consciousness constitutes time, laying out past, present and future as its objects. But then every moment is spread out before thought at once, on one level, and it's no longer time. There's time only if it's never completely laid out.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Past and future are in the world, we just observe them@just observe|The chapter makes time unreal@unreal|The two standpoints are two kinds of time@two kinds"
Don't say past and future are in the world and we just observe them; the water didn't change, only the standpoint. Don't think the chapter makes time unreal. And don't take the lab's two standpoints for two kinds of time.
