@chapter Pawnshops
@title text="Pre-Capitalist Relationships" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 36" art=thumb
Payday lenders and pawnshops still charge rates that would ruin a business, and their customers are people who need money to pay something now. Marx says the English working class of his day paid a hundred percent to the pawnshops.

@art two-borrowers
This chapter is about the time when that kind of lending was the main kind: usurer's capital. Marx names its two typical borrowers. First, extravagant persons of the higher classes, particularly landowners. Second, the small producer who owns his own means of work, the artisan, but especially the peasant.

@words text="Neither borrows to make a profit"
Neither borrows to make a profit. The lord borrows to spend. The peasant borrows because a payment is due, or a harvest has failed.

@chapter The whole surplus
@art whole-surplus
So the interest isn't a share of profit, as it is under capitalism. Under the form of interest, Marx writes, the whole of the producer's surplus over his means of subsistence may be devoured by usury.

@chapter The death of a cow
@quote text="The mere death of a cow may render the small producer unable to renew his reproduction on the former scale. Then he falls into the clutches of the usurer, and once he is in the usurer's power he never extricates himself." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 36"
The mere death of a cow may render the small producer unable to renew his reproduction on the former scale. Then he falls into the clutches of the usurer, and once he is in the usurer's power he never extricates himself.

@art dead-cow
A lost animal or a failed harvest, and the small producer needs money he doesn't have. So let's follow one debt.

@math heading="£50 at 30%, surplus £10, holding £100" items="year 1: 50 · 3/10 = 15; 50 + 15 − 10 = 55@year one;;year 2: 55 · 3/10 = 16.5; debt 61.5@year two;;years 3 to 5: 69.95, 80.94, 95.22@three to five;;year 6: about 113.78, past 100@year six"
The lesson's figures are illustrative. A peasant owes fifty pounds at thirty percent and can spare ten a year. His holding is worth a hundred. In year one the interest is fifteen. He pays ten, and the debt becomes fifty five. In year two, sixty one fifty. In years three to five, nearly seventy, then about eighty one, then ninety five. In year six it passes a hundred.

@art growing-debt
The interest was never paid in full, so every year's shortfall bore interest the next year. The usurer took the whole surplus for six years and ended with a claim bigger than the holding.

@chapter Where the line is
@math heading="An artisan: £20 at 20%, surplus £10" items="year 1: interest 4, debt 14@four;;year 2: interest 2.8, debt 6.8@six point eight;;year 3: repaid@repaid"
Compare an artisan who owes twenty at twenty percent, with the same ten to spare. The interest is four, so six pays off the debt. It falls to fourteen, then six point eight, and it's repaid in year three.

@words text="Interest > surplus: the debt can only grow"
The rate is lower, but what decides it is whether the interest is more than the surplus. Once it is, the debt can only grow. Luther saw lenders at the Leipzig fair take thirty or forty on every hundred, which, he said, is the same as devouring one peasant or burgher each year. At forty percent, the peasant is lost in year four.

@chapter Means of payment
@art payment-day
Why borrow at such rates? Because they need money to pay. The usurer's great domain, Marx says, is money as a means of payment. Rents, tithes and taxes became money payments on fixed days, and the producer with no money that day had to borrow it.

@art lord-estate
The landlord is the other form. In the lesson's example, he owes a thousand pounds at ten percent with fifty to spare, and his debt passes his two thousand pound estate in year twelve. But ruin doesn't change how the land is worked. Usury attaches itself to the mode of production as a parasite, and makes it miserable.

@chapter Credit against usury
@quote text="The development of the credit system takes place as a reaction against usury." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 36"
Then the turn. The development of the credit system takes place as a reaction against usury. Marx warns this isn't the church fathers' victory. It means interest-bearing capital subordinated to the needs of capitalist production. Credit is now given to a man as a potential capitalist, expected to profit from others' labour.

@words text="Microfinance" sub="lending to producers who are not capitalists"
A modern answer to usury is microfinance, lending small sums to poor producers on group guarantees. Supporters point to repayment rates, critics to high interest and over indebtedness. The chapter's line bears on it: does the borrower's surplus cover the interest? Which side a borrower stands on, the chapter can't say.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Usury is high interest on loans for production@The first|Old rates against modern ones show the worker's gain@The second|Usury by itself brings in capitalism@And the third"
The first slip is thinking usury is just high interest on loans for production. The second is comparing the usurer's rates with modern ones, as Henry Carey did, to show the worker's gain. Today's worker hands over his whole surplus too, as profit, interest and rent. And the third is thinking usury by itself brings in capitalist production.
