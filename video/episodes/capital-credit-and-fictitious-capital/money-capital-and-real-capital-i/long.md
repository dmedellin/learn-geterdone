@chapter Lowest when it's worst
@title text="Money Capital and Real Capital I" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 30" art=thumb
You may have noticed that interest rates are often lowest just when the news is worst. After a recession, deposits pile up in banks, and nobody wants to borrow. That's the season Marx describes.

@quote text="Not every augmentation of loanable capital indicates a real accumulation of capital or expansion of the process of reproduction." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 30"
His first question is whether a pile of money capital is a sign of real accumulation. His answer: not every augmentation of loanable capital indicates a real accumulation of capital or expansion of the process of reproduction.

@chapter Two kinds of accumulation
@art deposits-vs-spindles
Loanable capital is money in the hands of banks and lenders, offered at interest. Real accumulation is more spindles, more cotton, more workers employed. The two can move apart.

@art idle-mill
After the crisis of eighteen forty seven, production in the English industrial districts fell by a third. Yet the banks reported a great super abundance of money. The rate was low, a banker said, because of the almost complete annihilation of commerce. Loanable capital was plentiful precisely because industrial capital had been laid lame.

@chapter Hoarders and investors
@art hoard-and-buy
To see how, the lesson borrows Volume Two's scheme of expanded reproduction. Accumulating capitalists come in two groups. Some have just sold their surplus product and add the money to a hoard, until it's big enough to invest. Others spend hoards they built up before. The surplus product sells only if the second group's purchases match the first group's sales.

@math heading="Department I: 4,000c + 1,000v + 1,000s" items="to accumulate: 1000 · 1/2 = 500@five hundred;;half hoard: sales 250, purchases 250, gap 0@half;;all hoard: sales 500, purchases 0, gap +500@all of them hoard;;a quarter hoard: gap −250@a quarter"
Department One produces four thousand constant, a thousand variable and a thousand surplus value, and accumulates half: five hundred. If half the accumulators hoard, they sell two hundred and fifty and investors buy two hundred and fifty. If all of them hoard, just after a crisis, nobody buys. And if only a quarter hoard, investors want two hundred and fifty more than is offered.

@art unsold-and-fallow
Look at that middle case. Five hundred of commodity capital can't complete its round. And the money that would have bought it, the investors' old hoards, isn't spent either. With a developed credit system, it sits in the banks as deposits. Loanable capital lies fallow, while not one spindle is added.

@chapter Opposite directions
@art opposite-waves
So a pile of deposits can mean two opposite things. Over most of the cycle, Marx says, loan capital and industrial capital move in opposite directions. A low rate comes with contraction at the start, a high rate with a glut of industrial capital at the end. Only in recovery and mid prosperity do cheap loans and expanding production go together.

@art crash-gears
And in the crash? Credit stops, goods are unsold, factories close. Nothing is more erroneous, Marx says, than to blame a scarcity of productive capital for such a condition. What's scarce is credit. The real capital is in excess.

@chapter A sentence argued over
@quote text="The last cause of all real crises always remains the poverty and restricted consumption of the masses as compared to the tendency of capitalist production to develop the productive forces" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 30"
In the middle of this chapter is one of Capital's most argued over sentences. The last cause of all real crises always remains the poverty and restricted consumption of the masses as compared to the tendency of capitalist production to develop the productive forces.

@words text="Underconsumption? Or the falling rate of profit?"
Underconsumption theorists take it as Marx's verdict. Others note that Volume Two argues against explaining crises by workers' lack of purchasing power, and that Volume Three puts the falling rate of profit at the centre. The lesson takes no side.

@words text="“no manner of bank legislation can abolish a crisis”"
And bank law isn't the cause. A mistaken law like that of eighteen forty four, Marx says, may intensify a money crisis. But no manner of bank legislation can abolish a crisis.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Deposits mean plenty of real capital@The first|A low rate means good times@The second|One sentence as the settled theory of crisis@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a pile of bank deposits means plenty of real capital. The second is reading a low rate of interest as a sign of good times. And the third is taking the sentence on the masses' consumption as the chapter's settled theory of crisis.
