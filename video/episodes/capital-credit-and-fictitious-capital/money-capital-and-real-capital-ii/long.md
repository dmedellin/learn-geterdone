@chapter Nobody saved anything
@title text="Money Capital and Real Capital II" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 31" art=thumb
When a firm scales back, its overdraft shrinks and its cash piles up in a deposit account. Nobody in the firm has saved anything. It simply needs less money to keep going.

@art firm-deposit
Chapter thirty one asks where the banks' loanable money comes from. Its first answer is cases like this, where money that already exists changes hands and turns into money on offer at interest.

@quote text="an accumulation of loan capital may take place without any actual accumulation, by mere technical means, such as an expansion and concentration of the banking system" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 31"
Marx puts it this way. An accumulation of loan capital may take place without any actual accumulation, by mere technical means, such as an expansion and concentration of the banking system, or a saving in the reserves businesses keep.

@chapter Two routes
@list heading="Two routes to loan capital" items="Money converted into money capital@the mere conversion|Capital or revenue converted, then lent@the second|Only the second can go with real accumulation@Only the second"
Marx divides the ground. There's the mere conversion of money into money capital. And there's the conversion of capital or revenue into money, which is then lent. Only the second can go with an actual accumulation of industrial capital.

@chapter A business contracts
@art two-periods
The lesson takes Volume Two's numbers: a working period of nine weeks at a hundred pounds a week, and three weeks of circulation. Each week's outlay is tied up for twelve weeks before it comes back.

@math heading="Marx's 9 weeks and 3, at half the scale" items="before: (9 + 3) · £100 = £1,200@twelve hundred;;after: (9 + 3) · £50 = £600@fifty a week;;released: £1,200 − £600 = £600@released"
So the business ties up twelve hundred pounds. After a crisis it halves its scale, to an illustrative fifty a week. The advance falls to six hundred. Six hundred pounds is released.

@art released-to-bank
That six hundred lies in the business's bank account, and the bank lends it. Loan capital has grown by six hundred, while production has shrunk by half, and nobody has saved a penny. Just after a crisis, Marx says, the superfluity of loan capital is the direct opposite of actual accumulation.

@words text="Cheaper materials vs. smaller scale" sub="same £600 released, different meaning"
Compare a fall in the price of materials. That also sets capital free, but leaves the business doing as much as before. Both sums are deposited, and both influence the money market equally. The six hundred is the same either way. What it means isn't.

@chapter Banking spreads
@art country-banks
Then the spread of banking. The Bank Committee of eighteen fifty eight reported that London's stock banks grew their deposits from nearly nine million pounds in eighteen forty seven to forty three million in eighteen fifty seven. Around Ipswich, nearly all farmers, even those paying fifty pounds of rent, now had bank deposits.

@math heading="The same £100, every week" items="20 weeks · £100 = £2,000 deposited@two thousand pounds"
None of this needs more money. A dealer banks a hundred pounds every week. The bank pays it to a manufacturer, who pays his workers, who spend it at the dealer's shop, and he banks it again. After twenty weeks, he's deposited two thousand pounds by means of the same hundred.

@art same-hundred-loop
So, Marx concludes, the mass of loanable capital is different from and independent of the mass of circulating money.

@chapter Income that waits
@art waiting-revenue
Income does it too. The part of profit a capitalist means to spend is consumed a little at a time, but in the meantime it's a deposit, loan capital for the banker. Rents and salaries spent gradually do the same. Even a rise in consumption can show up as an accumulation of loan capital.

@chapter Abstinence?
@words text="Mill, 1848: capital is the result of saving"
The view in the chapter's sights is the classical one, put by John Stuart Mill: capital is the result of saving, and interest rewards abstinence. Marx doesn't deny that saved profit becomes loan capital. That's his second route. He adds the first, where loan capital grows with no saving at all. Later theories count released hoards and bank credit beside saving too.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Loan capital is only savings@The first|Rising deposits prove production is growing@The second|All released capital is a sign of health@And the third"
The first slip is thinking loan capital is only savings out of income. The second is reading a rise in deposits as proof that production is growing. And the third is treating all released capital as a sign of health.
