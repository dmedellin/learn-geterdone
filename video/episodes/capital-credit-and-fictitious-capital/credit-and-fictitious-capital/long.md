@chapter Payment due in thirty days
@title text="Credit and Fictitious Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 25" art=thumb
If you've ever sent an invoice marked payment due in thirty days, you've used the thing Marx starts from. The supplier hands over goods and becomes a creditor. The buyer takes them and becomes a debtor.

@art bill-chain
In the eighteen forties, that debt was written as a bill of exchange. The French economist Coquelin, whom Marx quotes, described the chain from the producer of raw material down to the shopkeeper: all borrow with one hand and lend with the other, sometimes money, but more frequently products.

@words text="“no finished draft, nor even an outline”" sub="Engels on Part V"
A word about this part of the book. Engels assembled Volume Three after Marx's death, and says Part Five gave him the most trouble: no finished draft, nor even an outline. Chapter twenty five is two pages of argument, then long extracts from bankers and parliamentary inquiries.

@chapter Commercial money
@quote text="Such bills of exchange in their turn circulate as means of payment until the day on which they fall due; and they form commercial money in the strict meaning of the term." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 25"
Such bills of exchange in their turn circulate as means of payment until the day on which they fall due; and they form commercial money in the strict meaning of the term.

@art endorsed-bill
A manufacturer holding a bill on his customer can endorse it over to his own supplier, who can pass it on again. And when bills balance one another, Marx says, they serve absolutely as money, because the debt is settled and no coin or note ever appears. Bank notes grow out of this, not the other way round.

@chapter A circle of bills
@art bill-circle
Take three parties. A owes B a hundred pounds for cotton. B owes C a hundred for yarn. And C owes A a hundred for cloth. All due on the same day.

@math heading="The circle" items="A: +100 − 100 = 0@A pays;;B: +100 − 100 = 0@B;;C: +100 − 100 = 0@and C;;cash needed: £0@no cash"
A pays a hundred and receives a hundred. So does B, and C. Every balance is zero. Three hundred pounds of goods have changed hands, and the settlement needs no cash at all.

@chapter Enter a bank
@art bank-discount
Banks gather the reserves of every business and lend them, and their profit is generally made by borrowing at a lower rate of interest than they lend. One way they lend is discounting bills: turning them into money before they fall due. So B sells his bill on A to a bank, takes the money now, less a deduction, and on the day A pays the bank.

@math heading="After the discount" items="A 0 · B −100 · C 0 · bank +100@B owes;;cash: £100@a hundred pounds"
Now the circle doesn't close among the merchants. B owes C a hundred with nothing coming in, and the bank is owed a hundred. The settlement needs a hundred pounds of cash. It's the bank's own money, paid to B, on to C, from C to A, and from A back home to the bank.

@quote text="even the form of an advance disappears. The transaction is then purely one of buying and selling." cite="Engels, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 26"
Was that a loan to B? Engels says no. When a bill is discounted, even the form of an advance disappears. The transaction is then purely one of buying and selling. The bankers of the day saw it the other way.

@chapter A bill for nothing
@art accommodation-bill
Now the bills that represent nothing. On top of the circle, A accepts a second hundred pound bill payable to B, with no sale behind it, so that B can have it discounted and raise money. A now owes B twice: once for cotton, and once for nothing.

@math heading="An accommodation bill" items="A −100 · B +100 · C 0@A is minus;;cash: £100@cash;;bills against nothing: £100@against nothing"
A is minus a hundred, B plus a hundred. The settlement needs a hundred pounds of cash where the circle needed none, and a hundred pounds of bills stand against nothing.

@art products-or-wind
Engels describes eighteen forty five to forty seven: goods shipped to India and China only so bills could be drawn against them. A Liverpool banker told the Lords he couldn't tell by looking at a bill whether it represents products or wind.

@chapter Real bills?
@words text="Thornton, 1802" sub="genuine bills grow with the boom"
Many bankers held the real bills doctrine: lend only against genuine trade, and credit can never be excessive. Henry Thornton objected in eighteen oh two. When prices rise, every genuine sale is a bigger bill, so genuine bills multiply with the boom they finance.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A bill is a loan of money@The first|Every bill is backed by goods@The second|The title is the theory@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a bill of exchange is a loan of money. It's a promise to pay for goods already sold. The second is treating every bill as backed by goods. And the third is reading the chapter's title as Marx's theory of fictitious capital. That comes four chapters on.
