@chapter One note, three roles
@title text="Medium of Circulation and Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 28" art=thumb
Think of a five pound note in your wallet. You were paid it as wages. You spend it at a shop. The shopkeeper banks it, and next week the bank pays it to a manufacturer to meet his wage bill.

@art five-pound-loop
For you it was income. For the shopkeeper, a return of his capital. For the manufacturer, capital advanced. Marx's own example is the same note, passing from one sphere to the other, and performing both functions in turn.

@chapter The Banking School
@art two-schools
Thomas Tooke, a Russia merchant turned historian of prices, and John Fullarton, a banker who'd worked in India, led the Banking School against Overstone's Currency School in the eighteen forties. They argued a bank can't force notes into circulation: the public holds what trade needs and returns the rest. Marx agrees.

@words text="“currency” vs “capital”" sub="Tooke's line, from the banker's desk"
But Tooke also split money into currency, the notes in the public's pockets, and capital, the funds bankers lend. Marx says those terms come from the banker's standpoint: the notes in the public's hands cost him only paper and printing, while what he lends from his own funds is his capital.

@chapter Where the line really is
@quote text="The difference, then, is in fact that between the money-form of revenue and the money-form of capital, but not that between currency and capital" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 28"
Marx keeps a real distinction, between money that realises revenue, and money that transfers capital between capitalists. The difference, then, is in fact that between the money form of revenue and the money form of capital, but not that between currency and capital.

@art shop-and-mill
Wages spent at the shop realise revenue. Cotton paid for between mill and merchant transfers capital. But both are money, circulating.

@quote text="Its mass is simply determined by its function as a medium of purchase and payment." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 28"
Whether the money represents capital or revenue, he says, is immaterial. Its mass is simply determined by its function as a medium of purchase and payment. That's Volume One's law of circulation.

@chapter Counting notes
@math heading="Notes needed = payments ÷ velocity" items="revenue: £1000 / 10 = £100@wages;;capital: £1000 / 10 = £100@between capitalists;;both: £2000 / 10 = £200@both;;capital with credit: £1000 / 25 = £40@twenty five"
The lesson's figures are illustrative. A week's wages of a thousand pounds are spent in shops, each note serving ten times: a hundred pounds of notes. A thousand paid between capitalists at ten turns: also a hundred. Both together: two hundred. And between capitalists, credit speeds the notes to twenty five turns: just forty.

@art same-stack
Calling the second thousand capital changed nothing in the notes it needed. What changed them was the velocity, and between capitalist and capitalist, Marx says, velocity is regulated directly by credit.

@chapter Through the cycle
@art cycle-two-streams
The two circulations do move differently over the cycle, and that's the grain of truth in Tooke. In prosperity, the circulation of revenue grows, with fuller employment and higher prices, while credit lets payments between capitalists grow with less money. In a crisis it's reversed: the shops' circulation shrinks, and the demand for means of payment grows as credit falls.

@chapter Fullarton's figures
@quote text="The loan remains, and the currency, if not wanted, finds its way back to the issuer" cite="Fullarton, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 28"
Fullarton held that a demand for loans and a demand for more circulation are quite distinct things. The Bank's own figures showed its loans rising while its notes often fell. Marx accepts the figures. The notes come back as deposits or as payments of bills. The loan remains, and the currency, if not wanted, finds its way back to the issuer.

@art boomerang-notes
Where Fullarton went wrong, on Marx's account, is in naming what's demanded. In a crisis, firms borrow to pay, not to buy. The notes go out and come straight back, so circulation doesn't swell. That's money as means of payment, not a demand for capital as opposed to currency.

@words text="Shares: titles of ownership · bonds: claims on loans"
And the securities a bank holds against its loans aren't capital either. Shares are mere titles of ownership. Government bonds are merely claims on loans. That's the next chapter's subject.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The notes measure the capital@The first|A bank's loans equal its notes@The second|Marx dismisses Tooke and Fullarton@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the quantity of notes measures the quantity of capital. The second is equating a bank's loans with its notes in circulation. And the third is taking Marx to dismiss Tooke and Fullarton. He accepts their evidence and corrects their terms.
