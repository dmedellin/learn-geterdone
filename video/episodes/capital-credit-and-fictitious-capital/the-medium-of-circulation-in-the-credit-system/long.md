@chapter Back in the bank by evening
@title text="The Medium of Circulation in the Credit System" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 33" art=thumb
Try to give a shopkeeper more cash than he needs, and by evening the surplus is in the bank.

@art evening-bank
A Scottish banker told the committee that's the whole story of note issue in Scotland. Everyone banks what he doesn't need that day, so that at the end of every business day all the money is in the banks, except what each carries in his pockets.

@quote text="Hence only the requirements of business itself exert an influence on the quantity of circulating money—notes and gold." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 33"
Marx's claim: hence only the requirements of business itself exert an influence on the quantity of circulating money, notes and gold.

@chapter The law of circulation
@words text="money in circulation = prices · transactions ÷ velocity" sub="Volume I, Chapter 3"
The requirements of business are the law from Volume One's chapter on money. Given the velocity and the economy of payments, the money in circulation is set by prices and the mass of transactions. The same law rules notes. Notes and gold means the whole currency, and only paper convertible into gold.

@art reflux
Bankers said so in their own trade. Tooke: the Bank has no power to expand its notes in the public's hands at its own arbitrary will. And a Nottingham banker: every surplus of the circulation will pass over into the deposits.

@chapter Need, issue and reflux
@math heading="Notes and gold" items="needed: £600 / 4 = £150@a hundred and fifty;;issued £200: £200 − £150 = £50 flows back@two hundred;;panic: £600 / 2 = £300 needed@twice"
The lesson's figures are illustrative. Cotton, yarn and cloth come to six hundred pounds, and each note serves four times: a hundred and fifty is needed. If the bank pushes out two hundred, fifty flows back, as deposits or loans repaid early. And in a panic, when each note serves only twice, the same goods need three hundred.

@art bank-and-public
Neither number was the bank's choice. It couldn't keep the extra fifty out in good times, and in the panic the public needs twice the notes for the same goods.

@chapter What the figures show
@words text="1844 → 1857: trade more than doubled, notes a little lower"
The record points the same way. The Bank of England's notes in the public's hands were a little lower in eighteen fifty seven than in eighteen forty four, although trade had more than doubled. The London Clearing House let banks settle with each other without large notes.

@chapter Credit and velocity
@art clearing-speed
Credit enters through velocity. A note paid in, lent out and paid in again does more work in a week than a coin passing only by sales. So, in a pamphlet Marx quotes, a sharp stringency in the money market generally coincides with a full circulation. When credit falters, velocity falls, and more notes are needed.

@chapter The panic
@quote text="the country needs twice as much currency as in ordinary times, because the medium of circulation is stored up by bankers and others." cite="Wright, quoted by Engels in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 33"
Engels adds what happens next. The notes, so plentiful yesterday, disappear. During the alarm the country needs twice as much currency as in ordinary times, because the medium of circulation is stored up by bankers and others.

@art locked-notes
Samuel Gurney put the notes locked away in October eighteen forty seven at four to five million pounds. His partner gave the bill brokers' view: three hundred million pounds of obligations payable in coin on demand, against about twenty three million of coin.

@words text="“the sudden change of the credit system into a monetary system”"
Marx's comment: in times of crisis we have the sudden change of the credit system into a monetary system. Bills, meanwhile, follow the rule in ordinary times, but nothing returns an excess of them. In a squeeze they multiply. In the crash, they stop.

@chapter Not powerless
@words text="“the Bank of England commands the rate of interest”"
None of this leaves the Bank powerless. In a squeeze, a London banker said, the Bank of England commands the rate of interest. It can't choose how many notes circulate. It can choose what they cost.

@art gold-and-paper
The opposing view is the Currency School's: the quantity of notes drives prices, so fix the issue by law. Friedman's monetarism later held something similar for a world without convertible notes. Engels states the chapter's limit: inconvertible bank notes are not taken into consideration at all here.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The bank decides how much circulates@The first|A large circulation means easy money@The second|The reflux holds for any paper money@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the bank decides how much money circulates. The second is reading a large note circulation as easy money. It can mean a squeeze. And the third is applying the reflux to any paper money. It depends on a holder's right to gold.
