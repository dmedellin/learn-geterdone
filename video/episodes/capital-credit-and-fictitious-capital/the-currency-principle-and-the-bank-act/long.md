@chapter A strange rule
@title text="The Currency Principle and the Bank Act of 1844" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 34" art=thumb
Picture a rule: every time five pounds of gold leaves the country, a five pound note must be taken out of use. In quiet times you'd hardly notice.

@art gold-leaves
The trouble is that gold leaves in the months before a crisis, just when everyone is about to want notes. That rule was English law from eighteen forty four, and this chapter is about what it did. Much of it is Engels's brackets around Marx's notes from the committees.

@quote text="For every five pounds in gold, then, which leave the bank treasury, a five pound note returns to the issue department and is destroyed" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 34"
For every five pounds in gold, then, which leave the bank treasury, a five pound note returns to the issue department and is destroyed.

@chapter The Currency School
@art self-correcting-gold
Put the theory at its strongest. Ricardo argued a currency of gold alone corrects itself. Too much gold raises prices, gold flows abroad, and prices fall again. Overstone and the Currency School concluded that notes must be made to behave the same way, or they'd feed speculation and drain the gold. The Act, they said, would make crises impossible.

@words text="Hubbard's tables: gold ↔ discount, not prices" sub="1838: £10,471,000 at 2.75% · 1839: £2,684,000 at 6%"
Marx's evidence comes from Hubbard, a former Governor. In March eighteen thirty eight the Bank's gold stood at ten point four seven million, with discount at two point seven five percent. By September eighteen thirty nine it was two point six eight million, at six percent. Of the prices, eight rose and five fell.

@words text="“the reduction of the quantity of gold raises only the rate of interest”"
Gold went with the rate of discount, while prices showed complete independence from the currency. Marx: the reduction of the quantity of gold raises only the rate of interest.

@chapter Computing the cap
@art two-departments
The Act cut the Bank in two. The issue department held the gold and issued notes. The banking department held deposits, with unissued notes as its reserve. The cap on notes was a fixed issue, backed by government securities, plus the gold.

@math heading="Marx's figures, in millions" items="cap: 14 + 14 = 28@twenty eight;;banking reserve: 28 − 20 = 8@reserve;;drain of 6: 14 + 8 = 22; reserve 2@six millions;;panic: 24 − 22 = 2 unmet@unmet"
The fixed issue is fourteen millions, and the gold fourteen, so the cap is twenty eight. Twenty millions are in circulation, so the banking reserve is eight. Now six millions of gold leave. The cap falls to twenty two, and the reserve to just two. Then panic adds Gurney's four millions of hoarded notes, and demand of twenty four meets a cap of twenty two. Two millions are unmet.

@art cap-and-demand
Engels says the Act directly challenged the commercial world to lay up notes on the eve of a crisis, hastening and intensifying it.

@quote text="and this sufficed in both cases to break the crisis" cite="Engels, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 34"
Twice, on the twenty fifth of October eighteen forty seven and the twelfth of November eighteen fifty seven, the government let the Bank exceed the cap, and this sufficed in both cases to break the crisis.

@art notes-return
What actually happened was smaller than the gap. In eighteen forty seven the promise alone brought hoarded notes back out, and no extra notes were issued. In eighteen fifty seven the excess didn't quite reach a million, and was out for a very short time. Once the cap went, demand fell back.

@chapter The bankers speak
@art gold-in-sea
Palmer, a director since eighteen eleven, said that in December eighteen twenty five the Bank issued five or six million notes in a week and relieved the panic. Under the Act, it couldn't. And a Liverpool witness said the gold locked in the issue department might just as well have been thrown into the sea.

@quote text="Should I answer you as a banker, I would say that it has operated splendidly" cite="Twells, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 34"
Who gained? A Birmingham banker, Twells: should I answer you as a banker, I would say that it has operated splendidly. Lending money had become very profitable.

@words text="July 1857: “Look about you”" sub="November 1857: suspended"
Engels closes with Overstone's evidence of July eighteen fifty seven, that the country's prosperity proved the Act sound. Look about you, he said. In November the Act was suspended. It was suspended a third time in eighteen sixty six, after Overend, Gurney failed.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Tying notes to gold steadies circulation@The first|The cap is what the country needed@The second|The suspension flooded the country with notes@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that tying notes to gold steadies the circulation. The second is reading the cap as the number of notes the country needed. And the third is thinking the suspension worked by flooding the country with notes. It worked by bringing hoards back out.
