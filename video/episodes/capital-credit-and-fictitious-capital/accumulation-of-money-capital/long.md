@chapter A familiar argument
@title text="Accumulation of Money Capital and the Rate of Interest" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 26" art=thumb
You know this argument in its modern form. When a central bank raises its rate, someone always says rates are high because the economy is strong.

@art overstone-bench
The man saying it here is Lord Overstone, banker, chief theorist of the Bank Act of eighteen forty four, and one of the richest men in England. Chapter twenty six is mostly his cross examination before Parliament, alongside another banker, Norman.

@quote text="The conception that the business profit is destroyed by raising the rate of interest is highly erroneous." cite="Overstone, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 26"
Put his case at its strongest. Interest is the price of capital. When trade expands, more things are wanted for production, the value of capital rises, and the rate records it. So the conception that the business profit is destroyed by raising the rate of interest is highly erroneous. A high rate, he says, means the rate of profit has risen.

@chapter Two shares of one profit
@art profit-bar
Marx's reply: the rate of profit may stay high for a long time, and yet the industrialist's profit may fall, and the rate of interest rise until it swallows most of the profit. Interest and profit of enterprise are two shares of one profit. Nothing about a high rate of profit stops the first share growing at the second's expense.

@math heading="£100 borrowed, 10% profit" items="profit: £100 · 1/10 = £10@ten pounds;;at 3%: interest £3, enterprise £7@three percent;;at 8%: interest £8, enterprise £2@eight percent;;at 10%: interest £10, enterprise £0@ten percent"
Here's the division. A manufacturer works a hundred pounds of borrowed capital at an illustrative ten percent profit: ten pounds. At three percent, where the Bank's rate began in January eighteen forty seven, three goes to the lender and seven stays. At eight percent, two is left. At ten percent, where the Bank's rate peaked in November, nothing is left.

@words text="The rate of profit never moved"
The rate of profit never moved. Overstone's value of capital rose from three to ten, and everything it gained came out of the share left to the man who employed the capital.

@chapter Not hypothetical
@art rejected-orders
Tooke told Parliament that in the Warwickshire and Staffordshire metal trades, very many orders were rejected in eighteen forty seven, because the discount the manufacturer had to pay would have swallowed his entire profit. Marx's retort to Overstone: when profit is large, businessmen pay because they're willing, and when it's low, because they must.

@chapter What did the rate measure?
@art full-warehouse
So what did the rate measure in eighteen forty seven? By October, a Liverpool banker said, goods were almost unsaleable. The warehouses were full. What firms lacked was the means to pay bills falling due.

@quote text="I do not believe that it was a lack of capital, which drove the rate of interest so high; it was the alarm, the difficulty of obtaining bank notes." cite="Samuel Gurney, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 26"
Samuel Gurney, the Quaker bill broker, said as much. I do not believe that it was a lack of capital, which drove the rate of interest so high; it was the alarm, the difficulty of obtaining bank notes.

@quote text="The value of capital in one form rose, because the value of capital in the other forms fell." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 26"
Marx puts it as a change of form. The value of capital in one form rose, because the value of capital in the other forms fell. Money capital grew dear because commodity capital couldn't be turned into money.

@chapter Interest without money?
@art seesaw-money
Norman, a director of the Bank of England, argued that the rate is set by the supply and demand for capital, and capital is all commodities used in production. So, he said, there would be interest even if no money existed.

@quote text="If there were no money at all, there would certainly be no general rate of interest." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 26"
Marx answers flatly. If there were no money at all, there would certainly be no general rate of interest. One rate for borrowers in every trade is a price of money capital, which needn't follow the market for cotton or iron.

@chapter Engels breaks in
@art collateral
Engels adds a passage on the confusion underneath. A banker thinks every payment is a loan of capital. But a customer who borrows against good securities receives money and no capital, since he's deposited more value than he gets. And one who discounts a bill has sold it. Only an unsecured advance gives capital.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A high rate shows capital is scarce@The first|A high rate means high profits for the borrower@The second|Every bank payment is a loan of capital@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a high rate shows a scarcity of capital in general. The second is treating a high rate as a sign of high profits for the borrower. And the third is reading every bank payment as a loan of capital.
