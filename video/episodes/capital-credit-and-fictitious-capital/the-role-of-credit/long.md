@chapter Mere owners
@title text="The Role of Credit in Capitalist Production" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 27" art=thumb
If you have a pension or a fund that holds shares, you're one of the people this chapter calls mere owners. You'll never meet the people who run the firms whose dividends you get, and they're paid salaries, like you.

@quote text="Transformation of the actually functioning capitalist into a mere manager, an administrator of other people's capital, and of the owners of capital into mere owners, mere money-capitalists." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 27"
In Marx's day the stock company was new outside railways, canals, banks and insurance. He saw in it the capitalist of Volume One splitting in two. Transformation of the actually functioning capitalist into a mere manager, an administrator of other people's capital, and of the owners of capital into mere owners, mere money capitalists.

@chapter Salary and dividend
@art owner-and-manager
The manager's salary is, or is supposed to be, a mere wage for a kind of skilled labour, priced in the labour market. The shareholders' dividend may contain the whole profit, but it's received only in the form of interest, as compensation for owning capital.

@words text="Profit of enterprise: £0"
Earlier, a borrowed capital's profit split into interest for the owner and profit of enterprise for the man who ran it. Here the man who runs it is an employee. Nothing is left that could be called profit of enterprise.

@chapter Three businesses
@math heading="A stock company" items="profit: £1000 · 22% = £220@twenty two percent;;salary: £20@salary;;dividend: £1000 · 20% = £200@dividend;;enterprise: £0@nothing"
The lesson's figures are illustrative. A thousand pounds of shares earn twenty two percent, two hundred and twenty pounds. The manager's salary is twenty. The dividend is two hundred, twenty percent on the shares, and nothing is left over.

@art owner-managed
Compare an owner who runs his own thousand at twenty percent. Reckoned the way chapter twenty three does it, fifty is interest on his capital at five percent, and one hundred and fifty is profit of enterprise. But both are his.

@math heading="A dealer on borrowed money" items="profit: £1000 · 20% = £200@two hundred;;interest: £800 · 5% = £40@forty;;kept: £160 on £200 of his own = 80%@eighty percent"
Now a dealer works a thousand pounds, of which eight hundred is borrowed at five percent. His profit is two hundred. The lenders get forty. He keeps one hundred and sixty, and on his own two hundred, that's eighty percent.

@chapter Other people's capital
@art others-capital
That's the chapter's fourth point. Credit gives the individual absolute command of the capital of others, within certain limits. And two old defences of profit go with it. The capitalist who saved? What he demands is precisely that others shall save for him.

@words text="“What the speculating wholesale merchant risks is social property, not his own.”"
And the merchant's risk? What the speculating wholesale merchant risks is social property, not his own.

@chapter Abolition within capitalism
@quote text="This is the abolition of the capitalist mode of production within capitalist production itself, a self-destructive contradiction, which represents on its face a mere phase of transition to a new form of production." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 27"
This is the sentence the chapter is remembered for. This is the abolition of the capitalist mode of production within capitalist production itself, a self destructive contradiction, which represents on its face a mere phase of transition to a new form of production.

@art shareholders-crowd
Read it slowly. What's abolished is capital as private property run by its owner. The means of production are already held by thousands of shareholders and run by hired staff. But the same paragraph names what comes with it: a new aristocracy of finance, of promoters, speculators and merely nominal directors.

@art cooperative-factory
Beside it Marx sets the workers' co-operative factories. There the opposition of capital and labour is overcome, but only by making the associated labourers their own capitalists.

@art trust-merger
Engels added a note thirty years later, on cartels and then trusts. His example is the United Alkali Trust: more than thirty works under one management, with about five million pounds of shares for the former owners, and a million of working capital raised from the public.

@chapter The other side
@words text="Berle and Means, 1932" sub="the principal-agent problem"
The standard objection came in nineteen thirty two. Berle and Means showed how far ownership and control had separated in American firms, and argued that managers, unchecked by scattered owners, could run firms in their own interest. On that account the manager isn't simply a wage earner. Marx takes the salary at face value.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Managers keep the profit of enterprise@The first|The abolition is the end of capitalism@The second|Borrowed capital is the borrower's risk alone@And the third"
The first slip is thinking profit of enterprise stays with the people who run a stock company. The second is reading the abolition as the end of capitalism. And the third is treating borrowed capital as the borrower's risk alone.
