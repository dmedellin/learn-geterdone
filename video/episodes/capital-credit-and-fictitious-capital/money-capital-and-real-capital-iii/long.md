@chapter Warehouses full, no cash
@title text="Money Capital and Real Capital III" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 32" art=thumb
In a financial panic, the news reports that firms can't get cash. And some of those firms have warehouses full of goods. That's the scene Marx is describing.

@art full-warehouse-cash
In a crisis, he writes, everyone borrows only for the purpose of paying, to settle obligations already contracted. In the recovery, loan capital is demanded for the purpose of buying. In a panic, the demand is for something to pay with, at any price, whatever the state of the real capital.

@chapter One chain, three ways
@art bill-triangle
Take a chain of bills, the lesson's own illustration. A owes B a hundred pounds, B owes C a hundred, and C owes A a hundred, each for goods delivered.

@math heading="Before and after a default" items="before: A 0, B 0, C 0; cash needed £0@cancel;;A fails: A +100, B −100, C 0@A cannot pay;;cash needed: £100@a hundred pounds;;goods and prices: unchanged, £300@the yarn"
Set off, the bills cancel, and no cash is needed. Now A cannot pay B. B still owes C a hundred, with nothing coming in. So the chain needs a hundred pounds of cash. And nothing about the cloth, the yarn or the cotton has changed. Their prices still add to three hundred.

@art broken-chain
Nothing real was lost between those two lines. What appeared was a demand for a hundred pounds of means of payment, which B must find before his bill to C falls due.

@words text="Credit stops: £300 in cash"
And when credit stops altogether, nobody accepts a promise to pay. Every sale must be paid in money. With each note held rather than passed on, the same three hundred pounds of goods need three hundred pounds of cash.

@chapter Both sides right and wrong
@quote text="This is the point, where both sides of the current theory are right and wrong in their opinion about crisis." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 32"
So in eighteen forty seven, did the country lack money or capital? This is the point, where both sides of the current theory are right and wrong in their opinion about crisis.

@art two-camps
Those who speak only of means of payment have in view the owners of good bills, who lack only conversion into money. Or they think banks can make bankrupt swindlers solvent with pieces of paper. Those who speak of a lack of capital are harping on words, since there's a mass of unsaleable capital from over importing and overproduction.

@chapter Overstone again
@math heading="Dearer goods, same rate" items="goods at £100: 5% · £100 = £5@instead of five;;goods at £150: 5% · £150 = £7½@seven and a half"
Overstone called the demand for loans a demand for capital. Marx says it's a demand for commodities. A merchant who now pays a hundred and fifty for goods that cost a hundred must borrow a hundred and fifty. At five percent, he pays seven and a half pounds of interest instead of five. The rate is the same. The sum is larger.

@chapter Plethora and saving
@art plethora
When profits pile up faster than they can be invested, a plethora of loanable money capital shows that capitalist production has its limits. But Marx adds at once: it doesn't necessarily mean overproduction, or a lack of places to invest.

@quote text="The last illusion of the capitalist system, to the effect that capital is the fruit of ones own labor and saving, is thereby destroyed." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 32"
And saving? Actual saving falls to those who get the smallest share, and who often lose even their savings when banks fail, while the industrialist commands the savings of others. The last illusion of the capitalist system, to the effect that capital is the fruit of ones own labor and saving, is thereby destroyed.

@chapter Why a money crisis
@art gold-drain
Why must a money crisis come at all? Credit money is money only while it can be turned into gold. When gold leaves the country, banks defend convertibility by raising the rate of interest. For the sake of a few millions of money, Marx writes, many millions of commodities must be sacrificed.

@words text="Bagehot, 1873: lend freely, at a high rate"
Walter Bagehot drew the opposite lesson in Lombard Street: in a panic, the Bank should lend freely, at a high rate, to anyone with good security. Marx agrees a bank whose credit holds eases a panic by expanding credit money. Whether that prevents crises or only shortens them, the chapter doesn't settle.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="In a crisis, capital is scarce@The first|Dearer goods must raise the rate@The second|A plethora proves overproduction@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that in a crisis capital is scarce. Goods fill the warehouses. The second is thinking dearer goods must raise the rate of interest. They raise the sum borrowed. And the third is taking a plethora of loan capital as proof of overproduction.
