@chapter The pound has fallen
@title text="Precious Metal and the Rate of Exchange" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 35" art=thumb
When the news says the pound has fallen, it means the price of other currencies in pounds has risen. In Marx's day, that price could rise only so far. Past a point, it was cheaper to ship gold than to buy a bill on Hamburg.

@quote text="The barometer for the international movement of the money metals is the rate of exchange." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 35"
The barometer for the international movement of the money metals is the rate of exchange. If England owes more to Germany than Germany owes England, bills on Germany are scarce in London, and their price rises.

@art bills-vs-gold
If nothing evens the balance, the price rises until it pays to send gold coin or bullion instead of bills. The gold that goes is the balance that bills couldn't settle.

@chapter The balance as a sum
@math heading="A bad harvest" items="trade balance: 9 − 6 = 3 million@nine million;;less receipts: 3 − 2 = 1 million@interest and dividends;;shipped in gold: 1 million@shipped"
The lesson's figures are illustrative. A country imports nine million pounds, including a million of extra corn after a bad harvest, and exports six million. That's three to pay. It receives two million in interest and dividends. So one million is shipped in gold.

@art failed-harvest
A drain caused by a crop failure, Marx says, needs no further explanation. He adds only that its effects are greater the harder production is running when it comes.

@chapter The pivot
@art three-jobs
Where does the gold come from? The Bank of England's reserve, which does three jobs: world money for payments abroad, a fund for gold circulating at home, and a fund for paying deposits and changing notes. The first job drains the third.

@quote text="The central bank is the pivot of the credit system. And the metal reserve in its turn is the pivot of the bank." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 35"
The central bank is the pivot of the credit system. And the metal reserve in its turn is the pivot of the bank.

@chapter Small sums, big effects
@art feather-scale
That's why small sums matter. In eighteen forty seven, England's exports of precious metals came to about eight point six million pounds. Marx calls such a sum a vanishing magnitude beside English production. It tells because of when it comes. It works like a feather added to scales already swaying, and tips them.

@quote text="In 1847 the drain of gold ceased in April and the crash came in October." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 35"
The order of events matters too. In eighteen forty seven the drain of gold ceased in April and the crash came in October. A drain is a symptom of a change in foreign trade, a warning of the crisis, not its cause.

@art pivot
While credit holds, money looks like book keeping. But once credit is shaken, all real wealth must suddenly be turned into gold and silver, a crazy demand that grows out of the system itself. Marx's joke: the monetary system is essentially Catholic, the credit system essentially Protestant.

@chapter India, 1855
@math heading="Newmarch's figures for 1855" items="imports from India: £12,670,000@twelve million;;exports to India: £10,350,000@ten million;;Company's drafts on India: £3,700,000@drafts;;to ship: £0 · owed to England: £1,380,000@owed to England"
William Newmarch's figures for eighteen fifty five. England imported twelve million, six hundred and seventy thousand pounds from India, and exported ten million, three hundred and fifty thousand. The East India Company drew three million seven hundred thousand of drafts on India. Count those, and nothing is shipped, with one million three hundred and eighty thousand owed to England.

@art tribute-ship
Marx's comment: the drafts resolve themselves into a tribute levied on India. Newmarch said Britain exported good government for them.

@chapter Is gold wealth?
@words text="Hume, 1752: price-specie flow"
The oldest view is the mercantilist one: a country's gold is its wealth, and an outflow makes it poorer. David Hume answered it in seventeen fifty two: gold that leaves lowers prices at home and comes back by itself. Marx adds that the outflow is small beside production, and matters through the reserve, the rate of interest and the phase of the cycle.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A gold outflow makes the country poorer@The first|The crash comes while gold flows out@The second|Every export of capital moves the exchanges@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a gold outflow means the country is getting poorer. The second is thinking the crash comes while the gold is flowing out. And the third is thinking every export of capital moves the exchanges. Rails bought in England for India are English capital invested in England.
