@chapter Twenty years' rent
@title text="The Component Parts of Bank Capital" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 29" art=thumb
You've met this sum whenever you've heard that bond prices fall when interest rates rise, or that a house is worth twenty years' rent. Marx's chapter twenty nine gives the rule behind both.

@quote text="The forming of a fictitious capital is called capitalising." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 29"
The forming of a fictitious capital is called capitalising. Every regular income is treated as interest on a capital, at the going rate of interest.

@art income-to-capital
So a claim to a hundred pounds a year is worth whatever sum would earn a hundred pounds a year if it were lent at the going rate.

@chapter Change only the rate
@math heading="£100 a year" items="at 5%: £100 / (1/20) = £2000@five percent;;at 10%: £100 / (1/10) = £1000@ten percent;;at 2½%: £100 / (1/40) = £4000@two and a half"
At five percent, a hundred over one twentieth is two thousand pounds. That's Marx's own example. Now change only the rate. At ten percent, the same income is priced at one thousand. At two and a half percent, at four thousand.

@art seesaw-rate
The income is the same in all three. Nothing that pays it has grown or shrunk. And yet the capital has halved and doubled. Marx makes the same move himself: when the rate rises from five to ten percent, a security paying five pounds represents a capital of only fifty.

@chapter A spent loan
@art state-bond
His first example is a government bond. The state borrowed a hundred pounds and spent it, on a war perhaps, and pays the lender five a year out of taxes. The lender can't ask for the hundred back, but he can sell his claim. To buyer and seller alike, it looks like a capital of a hundred pounds.

@quote text="Not only does the amount loaned to the state exist no longer, but it was never intended at all to be invested as capital" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 29"
But not only does the amount loaned to the state exist no longer, but it was never intended at all to be invested as capital. That's fictitious capital in its plainest form. If the certificates became unsaleable, the fiction would disappear.

@words text="£50 a year at 5% = a “capital” of £1,000" sub="“The insanity of the capitalist mode of conception reaches its climax here.”"
The habit goes further. A worker earning fifty pounds a year, at five percent, can be called a capital of a thousand pounds, and statisticians did so. The insanity of the capitalist mode of conception reaches its climax here. He has to work to get this interest, and he can't sell the capital.

@chapter Shares
@art rails-and-paper
Shares are different. A railway share is a title to real capital, the rails and engines the shareholders paid for. But this capital does not exist twofold: once as rails, and again as the price of the paper.

@math heading="Marx's share" items="£100 paid in, 10% dividend: £10 a year@ten pounds a year;;at 5%: £10 / (1/20) = £200@two hundred"
The paper's price is fixed by capitalising its dividend. A share issued at a hundred pounds, whose company pays ten percent, brings ten pounds a year. At five percent interest, that's priced at two hundred. The rails are the same rails at either price.

@chapter A bubble bursts
@art crash-chart
This is why a crash can destroy so much wealth and so little capital. The Governor of the Bank told the committee that on the twenty third of October eighteen forty seven, public funds, canal and railway stocks had lost over one hundred and fourteen million pounds.

@words text="“the nation did not grow poorer by one cent”"
Marx's comment: unless the fall stopped trains and canal boats, the nation did not grow poorer by one cent through the bursting of this bubble. The holders who had to sell grew poorer, and the buyers richer. The fall centralises money.

@chapter Inside the bank
@art bank-vault
Then the bank itself. Its capital is cash, bills of exchange and securities, mostly its depositors' money, not its own. Bonds represent spent capital, shares are claims on future yields, and deposits are lent out again. So, Marx says, with credit all capital seems to double, or even treble, itself.

@chapter Fiction or information?
@words text="price = present value of expected income"
The rule is now the first lesson of finance, and Irving Fisher built a theory of capital on it in nineteen oh six. What modern economists dispute is the word fictitious. On the standard view, a share's price is the market's best estimate of what the real capital will earn. Marx's strongest case is the state bond, a claim on future taxes for capital long spent.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A security's price measures real capital@The first|A share's price is a second capital@The second|A price fall destroys national wealth@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a security's price measures real capital invested. The second is counting a share's price as a second capital beside the company's. And the third is believing a fall in security prices destroys national wealth.
