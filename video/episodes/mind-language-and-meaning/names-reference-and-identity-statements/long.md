@chapter The puzzle
@title text="Names, Reference and Identity Statements" sub="Mind, Language and Meaning" art=thumb
Hesperus is Hesperus. Anyone knows that. Hesperus is Phosphorus. That had to be discovered. But if both names pick out the same planet, how can the second sentence tell you anything the first doesn't?

@art evening-morning
People saw a bright light in the evening sky, and named it Hesperus. They saw a bright light in the morning sky, and named it Phosphorus. It was later established these were one planet, Venus.

@words text="Reference and sense" sub="the individual picked out · the way it is presented"
Frege drew a distinction. The reference of a name is the individual it picks out. Its sense is the way that individual is presented, the information that goes with using the name. Two names can share a reference, and differ in sense.

@chapter A model
@math heading="Model 1:  h → a,  p → a" items="h = p:  a = a,  True@Evaluate h;;∀x (x = h → x = p):  True@whatever is Hesperus"
Build a model with two individuals: a for the planet, and b for something else. Let h be the name Hesperus, and p the name Phosphorus. Both pick out a. Evaluate h equals p. Both terms pick out a, so it's true. And whatever is Hesperus is Phosphorus is true too.

@art pointer-names
In a model, a name is just a pointer to an individual. Since a sentence's value is built from the referents of its names, you can put p where h stood, anywhere, and the value can't move. Shines h and shines p are true together, or false together.

@chapter The other model
@math heading="Model 2:  h → a,  p → b" items="h = p:  a = b,  False@give p the referent b;;∀x (x = h → x = p):  fails at a@fails at a"
Now give p the referent b. The identity is false, and the uniqueness sentence fails at a, which is Hesperus but not Phosphorus. This model is just as easy to write down. It's a perfectly good description of how things might have been.

@art two-skies
For the early astronomers, it was a live candidate for how things were. So learning that Hesperus is Phosphorus was learning which of the two models described the sky. The model contains the discovery, as the difference between two ways of assigning referents.

@chapter What the model misses
@art two-windows
But the model also misses something. An astronomer could sincerely say Hesperus is visible in the evening, and wonder whether Phosphorus is. No sentence the lab evaluates can express that, once h and p share an individual. Frege's account is that the two names have different senses, and belief goes by sense. The lab has no senses to hold.

@chapter Twin Earth
@art twin-earth
Putnam's Twin Earth shows the same gap from the other side. Take, water is H two O. In the first model, a is water and H two O, and b is a different liquid, X Y Z. The sentence is true. In the second, the word water applies to the stuff around the speakers there, which is X Y Z. The sentence is false, and fails first at a.

@art head-and-world
It's the same string. What the speakers have in their heads can be the same on both planets. Putnam's claim is that the meaning of the word isn't in the head alone. The lab shows only that extension is fixed by the model, not by the sentence.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking if a = b is true, the names mean the same@The first|Reading the lab as a theory of belief@The second|Treating Twin Earth as a proof about meaning@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that if a equals b is true, the two names mean the same. Sameness of reference is a fact about the pointer. The second is reading the lab as a theory of belief. It can't say what anyone doubts. And the third is treating Twin Earth as a proof about meaning. The lab only illustrates that extension varies.

@chapter Try it
@words text="h → a,  p → a" sub="then move p to b"
Evaluate h equals p in the first model, then substitute p for h in a sentence and check the value holds. Then move p to b, and evaluate again. Finally, say what a speaker could still doubt in the first model, and why the table has no place for it.
