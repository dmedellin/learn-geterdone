@chapter The puzzle
@title text="Definite Descriptions and the Present King of France" sub="Mind, Language and Meaning" art=thumb
The present King of France is bald. It looks like a sentence about a king. But there is no king. A sentence can't be about a thing that isn't there. Yet it's plainly meaningful. So what is it saying?

@words text="Definite description" sub="the F: the author of Waverley, the present King of France"
A definite description is a phrase of the form, the F. The author of Waverley. The present King of France. It's used to speak of one thing, by saying what that thing is like. On the surface, it behaves like a name. The King of France is bald has a subject and a predicate, and seems to be about the king. So either there is some such thing, or the surface form misleads.

@chapter Russell's analysis
@math heading="The F is G" items="∃x ( F(x)@there is an x;;∧ ∀y ( F(y) → y = x )@any y;;∧ G(x) )@and that x is G"
Russell holds that the surface misleads. The F is G is really a quantified sentence with three clauses. There is an x that is an F. Any y that is an F is that x. And that x is G. Something is an F, no more than one is, and the one is G.

@art three-clauses
All three are part of what's said, and each can fail. So three models cover the cases: one F, no F, or too many.

@chapter One, none, too many
@art one-author
In the first, the domain has a, b and c. Author holds of b alone, and Scot holds of b and c. The description picks out b, the one and only author. b is a Scot, so the sentence is true.

@art empty-throne
In the second, King holds of nobody, and Bald holds of a. The first clause fails. No x is a king. So whatever the third clause says, the conjunction can't hold, and the sentence is false. The lab also reports that the description denotes nothing.

@art two-crowns
In the third, King holds of a and of b, and both are bald. The first clause is met. The second isn't, since b is a king and isn't a. So the sentence is false, and the description isn't unique. That both kings are bald doesn't help, because the sentence claims there's just one.

@chapter Strawson's objection
@art presupposition-gap
Now the dispute. Strawson's observation is about what people say. Asked whether the present King of France is bald, almost no one answers no. They say there's no such king. The sentence, he held, presupposes a king, and when that fails, it's neither true nor false.

@list heading="Same model, two names for the result" items="Russell: false, the existence clause fails@Russell's reply|Strawson: the presupposition fails@Strawson's reading"
Russell's reply is that the sentence does say there's a king, so it's false, just as, there is a king who is bald, is false. Strawson's reading is that the presupposition fails. They agree about the model, and differ on what to call the result. That's a philosophical choice the arithmetic doesn't make.

@chapter What it buys
@art bundle
What the expansion buys is a way to say the sentence is meaningful, though nothing is denoted. The description was never a name, only a bundle of claims. Its price is a complication for the grammar, since the King of France no longer behaves as a single phrase. The next lesson turns that into a second reading.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Saying Russell gives it no truth value@The first|Thinking two Fs are fine if every F is G@The second|Treating a description as a failed name@And the third"
The first slip is saying the sentence has no truth value on Russell's account. That's Strawson's position. Russell says false. The second is thinking a doubled description is true if every F is G. The word the puts uniqueness in. And the third is treating a description as a name that happens to fail. A description is evaluated by looking at its restrictor, which may be empty. An empty one makes the sentence false, not unparseable.
