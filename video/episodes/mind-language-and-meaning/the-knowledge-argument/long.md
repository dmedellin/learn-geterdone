@chapter Mary's room
@title text="The Knowledge Argument" sub="Mind, Language and Meaning" art=thumb
Mary is a scientist who knows every physical fact there is about colour, and the vision of colour. She's lived her whole life in a room where everything is black, white or grey.

@art grey-room
One day she's let out, and sees a ripe tomato. The knowledge argument says she learns what red looks like, that this is something she didn't already know, and that therefore not every fact is a physical fact.

@chapter Standard form
@math heading="a: physicalism is true,  n: Mary learns a new fact" items="a → ¬n@If physicalism;;n@Mary does learn;;∴ ¬a@So physicalism is false"
Let a say physicalism is true, every fact is a physical fact. Let n say that on release, Mary learns a new fact. If physicalism is true, Mary, who already knew every physical fact, learns no new fact. Mary does learn a new fact. So physicalism is false.

@art four-rows
That's modus tollens. The lab builds a row for every assignment of a and n, four in all, and finds none with both premises true and the conclusion false. So the argument is valid. That settles the logic, and puts all the weight on the premises.

@chapter The ability reply
@art ripe-tomato
A physicalist has two ways out. Neither says the form is invalid. The first is the ability reply. What Mary gains isn't a fact, but an ability: to recognise red, imagine it, remember it. Knowing how isn't knowing that. So this denies n.

@math heading="Restated with an ability" items="k:  Mary gains a new ability@let k say;;a → ¬n,  k  ⊬  ¬a@do not lead;;row:  a true,  k true,  n false@a counterexample row"
Restate the argument with the ability in place of the fact. Let k say Mary gains a new ability. Then if a then not n, and k, do not lead to not a. The lab finds a counterexample row, with a and k true and n false. A new ability doesn't conflict with physicalism.

@art ability-tools
So the reply isn't that the argument is invalid. It's that the fact premise is false, and the ability premise, which is true, doesn't carry the conclusion.

@chapter The equivocation charge
@art two-keys
The second way out is the charge of equivocation. Knowing a physical fact is knowing that something is so. Knowing what red looks like seems to be knowing it by acquaintance. If those are different senses, the premise linking them fails.

@math heading="Two senses, two atoms" items="f:  Mary knows all the physical facts@f for;;l:  lacks what red looks like, same sense@l for;;m:  lacks it by acquaintance@m for;;a → ¬(f ∧ l),  f,  m:  invalid@The premises"
Give the senses separate atoms. f for Mary knowing all the physical facts. l for her lacking what red looks like in that same sense. And m for lacking it by acquaintance. The premises are, if a then not both f and l, then f, and m. The lab finds a counterexample, with a, f and m true, and l false.

@words text="Make l and m one atom" sub="and the argument is valid again"
The dualist's answer is visible too. Make l and m one atom, so what Mary lacks is the same in both premises, and the argument is valid again. Whether they're one atom is the dispute. The dualist holds acquaintance with red is knowledge of a fact. The physicalist holds it isn't.

@chapter Each side's cost
@art two-costs
Each side pays. To deny Mary learns a new fact is to say her coming to know what red looks like adds nothing to what is so, which many find hard to say with a straight face. To accept the new fact is to accept something is left out of the physical story, which is what the physicalist can't.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the argument is invalid@The first|Counting a change of atom as a refutation@The second|Treating the equivocation charge as settled@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the knowledge argument is invalid. It's modus tollens. The second is counting a change of atom as a refutation. The ability version is a different argument. And the third is treating the equivocation charge as settled. Whether l and m are one is the question between the sides.

@chapter Try it
@words text="a → ¬n,  n  ∴  ¬a" sub="four rows, no counterexample"
Build the four row table yourself, and check only one row makes both premises true. Then, in the equivocation version, put l in place of m, and see the verdict return to valid.
