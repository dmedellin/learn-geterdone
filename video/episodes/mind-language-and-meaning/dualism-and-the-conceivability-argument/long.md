@chapter The argument
@title text="Dualism and the Conceivability Argument" sub="Mind, Language and Meaning" art=thumb
Can you imagine being in pain with nothing at all happening in your brain? Many people say yes. And from that, the dualist argues that pain isn't a brain state. Here's exactly where that argument has to commit itself.

@art nerve-fibres
Dualism about the mind is the claim that mental states aren't identical to physical states. The version here is about one pair: pain, and the firing of C fibres in the brain. The claim isn't what pain is. It's that pain is not that.

@list heading="Four steps" items="Pain without C-fibre firing is conceivable@It's conceivable|Whatever is conceivable is possible@Whatever is conceivable|So it is possible@So it's possible|So pain is not C-fibre firing@So pain is not"
The argument has four steps. It's conceivable that there's pain with no C fibre firing. Whatever is conceivable in this way is possible. So it's possible. And if pain were identical to C fibre firing, the two would go together in every possible case. So pain is not identical to C fibre firing.

@chapter The identity as a necessity
@math heading="p: someone is in pain,  c: C-fibres are firing" items="□(p ↔ c)@necessarily"
Let p say someone is in pain, and c that C fibres are firing. The identity, taken as a necessity, is necessarily, p if and only if c. A box is true at a world when its contents are true at every world it sees. So the claim is only as strong as the list of worlds in view.

@chapter The dualist's model
@art two-worlds
Here's the model the dualist needs. Two worlds. World one is actual, and it sees itself and world two. At world one, p and c are both true. At world two, p is true and c is false. Pain, without the fibres.

@math heading="At w1" items="p ↔ c at w1:  true@true at world one;;p ↔ c at w2:  false@false at world two;;□(p ↔ c) at w1:  false@Since world one sees"
The biconditional is true at world one, where both are true, and false at world two. Since world one sees world two, the necessity is false at world one.

@chapter Remove one world
@art one-world
Now remove world two. The atoms at world one are what they were, and the formula is what it was. But the verdict reverses. With only world one in view, the necessity is true. No logic changed. A world was added or taken away.

@art thought-bubble
So the work of the argument is done by the second step, the licence to add the world. The rest is arithmetic.

@chapter The dispute over the licence
@art heat-motion
The dualist can say why the licence is good. We only know pain by how it feels, and how it feels seems to leave the brain open. The physicalist answers with a precedent. Before the chemistry, heat without molecular motion was just as easy to imagine. Yet heat is molecular motion. What was imagined was something that felt like heat.

@words text="For pain, the feeling is the thing" sub="no gap between seeming and being"
The dualist replies that for pain, the way it feels is the thing itself, so there's no such gap between seeming and being. The lab can't settle that.

@art lightning
The physicalist has a second place to stand. The first identity theorists held that pain is C fibre firing as lightning is an electrical discharge, a fact found out, not a necessity. Kripke's answer is that an identity between two things, each named for what it is, can't hold here and fail elsewhere. So the physicalist must fight at the second step instead.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating what can be conceived as what is possible@The first|Reading the lab's false as refuting physicalism@The second|Reading □A as A at the starting world@And the third"
The first slip is treating what can be conceived as what's possible. Heat was once imagined without molecular motion. The second is reading the false necessity as a refutation of physicalism. The model already contains the world the dualist wants. And the third is reading box A as A at the world you start from. A coincidence here isn't a necessity.
