@chapter The question
@title text="Plateaus and Transfer" sub="Reading Faster · Your Training Plan" art=thumb
Your tests have gone flat. Is that your limit? And did the gains you made on the practice passages carry over to other kinds of text at all? One reading can tell you where to look.

@chapter The plateau
@art plateau
Suppose your recent test median is two seventy effective words a minute, against a baseline range of two thirty eight to two fifty one. That's a change by the Subject's rule. And suppose the tests have stayed near two seventy for some weeks. That's a plateau.

@art practice-loop
The natural reading, that this is your limit, is the least informative one. A plateau often means the practice has stopped asking for more: the same target, the same kind of passage, comfortably held. Or it can mean that what improved was reading the practice material.

@chapter The drill
@art argument-page
An unpractised kind of text is how you tell those apart. The drill is In Praise of Maintenance, an argument of three hundred and eighty nine words at the hard band. Long sentences, an objection answered, a claim built across paragraphs. The Daily Drill's eighteen pool passages hold just one argument.

@words text="Logged as other, not as a test"
Read it at natural pace with no pacer, and answer six questions. It's logged as other, not as a test. It belongs to no series yet, and as a test, it would pull down the median of your tests on standard passages.

@chapter Two readings
@math heading="Transfers preset" items="389 × 60 / 97.25 = 240 wpm@ninety seven point two five;;240 × 6/6 = 240 effective@all six;;inside 238 to 251 · 30 below 270@thirty below"
The transfers preset read it in ninety seven point two five seconds, two forty words a minute, with all six right: an effective rate of two forty. That's inside the old baseline range, and thirty below the recent median. Slower than the practised kind, as harder text often is, and comprehension held.

@words text="Consistent with some carrying over, or none"
It's consistent with some of the gain carrying over, and also with none. Without a reading of this kind from before the practice, one reading can't say which.

@math heading="Does-not preset" items="389 × 60 / 77.8 = 300 wpm@three hundred words;;300 × 4/6 = 200 effective@four questions;;238 − 200 = 38 below the range@thirty eight below"
The does not preset read the same passage in seventy seven point eight seconds, three hundred words a minute, the practised pace kept up. But with four questions right, the effective rate is two hundred, thirty eight below the bottom of the baseline range.

@art rate-kept
The rate carried over, and the understanding didn't. The faster reading is the worse one. And notice the trap: three hundred looks like a gain over two seventy, but two seventy is an effective rate. Compare effective rate with effective rate.

@chapter What it can't separate
@words text="Harder, and a different kind" sub="one passage can't split the gap"
Two things this comparison can't separate. The argument is harder than your baseline passages, and it's a different kind of text. Both lower a first reading. What it can tell you is whether the practised pace survived a text that asked more of you.

@words text="Practice improves the practised task most" sub="Rayner et al. (2016)"
The wider evidence points the same way. Practice generally improves the practised task more than tasks that only resemble it. Rayner and colleagues, in twenty sixteen, found the evidence weak that eye exercises carry over to reading, and concluded a large gain in rate with comprehension held is unlikely.

@chapter What to do next
@art transfer-bridge
So if it went like does not, practise on that kind of text. Paste arguments you haven't read into the Daily Drill's own text box, and read them with the pacer below your hold target. Raise it once you can state each one's claim and its answer to an objection.

@art restart-target
If it went like transfers, the plateau is more likely the practice. Has the target been raised after three holds, or has it sat where it was comfortable for weeks? If the routine has quietly stopped asking for more, restart it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A plateau means I've hit my limit@The first|A first reading is a failed test@The second|Comparing a rate with an effective rate@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a plateau means you've reached your limit. The second is treating a first reading as a failed test. It starts a series rather than ending one. And the third is comparing a rate with an effective rate.

@chapter Try it

Your turn. Three hundred and eighty nine words in ninety seven point two five seconds, with five of six right. What's the effective rate? The rate is two forty words a minute, and two forty times five sixths is two hundred.
