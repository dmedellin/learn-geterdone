@chapter The question
@title text="A Practice Week" sub="Reading Faster · Your Training Plan" art=thumb
You've kept a routine since the Daily Drill. Now it's time to review it and plan next week. What makes a week of practice worth doing? Not just how many minutes it has.

@chapter The routine so far
@list heading="The routine" items="10 to 15 minutes a day@ten to fifteen minutes|Pacer at your hold target@hold target|A test every fifth session@every fifth session|±5% after 3 holds or 2 misses@about five percent"
Here's the routine. Ten to fifteen minutes a day. One pool passage with the pacer at your hold target. Every fifth session, a test at natural pace. The target raised by about five percent after three holds in a row, and lowered by the same after two misses. And at most two timed readings in a sitting.

@art practice-vs-test
Practice and test are different jobs. A practice reading with the pacer at your hold target trains. A test reading at natural pace, with no pacer, measures. Only tests go into the verdict.

@chapter Four rules
@list heading="A week worth doing" items="Sessions of 10 to 15 minutes@ten to fifteen minutes|Five or more days@five or more|A test at least every fifth session@at least every fifth|Inside the time you actually have@actually have"
A week worth doing meets four rules. Sessions of ten to fifteen minutes. A session on five or more days. A test at least every fifth session. And a total inside the time you actually have. The lab checks the arithmetic and that last rule only. The other three are yours to check.

@art session-clock
The ten to fifteen minutes isn't a minimum to beat. Past the point where attention holds, a reading practises drifting. So when your attention leaves the text, the answer is a shorter session, not a slower pacer.

@chapter Fifteen a day
@math heading="Fifteen-a-day preset" items="5 × 15 + 2 × 15 = 105 min@five times fifteen;;120 − 105 = 15 to spare@fifteen to spare;;30 / 105 = 2/7 ≈ 28.6% test@two sevenths"
The fifteen a day preset keeps all four. Five fifteen minute sessions of pacer practice and two test sessions: five times fifteen plus two times fifteen is a hundred and five minutes, against a budget of a hundred and twenty. Fifteen to spare. The tests are thirty of the hundred and five minutes: two sevenths, about twenty eight point six percent.

@art week-rows
Every session is within ten to fifteen minutes, there are seven days with a session, and two of the seven are tests. That's more often than one in five, which costs nothing but a little practice.

@chapter Weeks that look fine
@art weekend-only
The weekend only preset is two sixty minute sittings, one opening with a test. A hundred and twenty minutes, exactly the budget, and half of it the test session. Every tile looks generous. But an hour is four to six times the routine's session, so most of each sitting comes after attention has started to leave the text.

@words text="Most days, not one day"
And five days of the seven are empty. Readings in one sitting share that sitting's conditions: the same tiredness, the same hour, the same mood. One long Saturday gives the log one day, measured several times.

@art all-sprints
The all sprints preset is seven fifteen minute sessions of sprints above comfort. A hundred and five minutes, the same as fifteen a day, and the test share tile reads no test in the week. At the end, you'll know how the sprints felt, and nothing about whether your reading changed.

@chapter Deliberate practice
@art edge
The idea under the routine has a name. Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch Romer, in nineteen ninety three, in a study of musicians, called it deliberate practice: designed to improve one thing, at the edge of what you can do, with knowledge of the result after each attempt. The pacer is the edge, and the six questions are the result.

@words text="Its shape, not a promise" sub="music, not reading · Rayner et al. (2016): doubling a rate is unlikely"
How much of expert performance such practice explains is contested, and their study was of music, not reading. The routine borrows its shape, not a promise. Rayner and colleagues, in twenty sixteen, concluded that doubling or tripling a rate with comprehension held is unlikely.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="More drills means more progress@The first|Treating the budget as a target@The second|Taking a test with the pacer on@And the third"
The first slip is thinking more drills means more progress. Progress is what the tests show. The second is treating the budget as a target. Spare minutes aren't a failure. And the third is taking a test with the pacer on. Then it measures how well you followed the pacer.

@chapter Try it
@math heading="4 × 12 practice · 1 × 12 test · 2 × 15 own text" items="48 + 12 + 30 = 90 min@ninety minutes;;12 / 90 = 2/15 ≈ 13.3%@two fifteenths"
Your turn. Pacer practice, four sessions of twelve minutes. One twelve minute test. And two fifteen minute sessions on your own text. The week is forty eight plus twelve plus thirty, ninety minutes. The test is twelve of them: two fifteenths, about thirteen point three percent. The lab counts minutes, not sessions.
