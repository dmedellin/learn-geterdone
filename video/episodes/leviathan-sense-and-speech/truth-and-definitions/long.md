@chapter Truth lives in speech
@title text="True and False, and Definitions" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 4" art=thumb
Where does truth live? Most people would say in the match between a sentence and the world. Hobbes puts it somewhere else.

@quote text="For True and False are attributes of Speech, not of Things. And where Speech is not, there is neither Truth nor Falshood." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 4"
Join two names, a man is a living creature, and if the second signifies all the first does, it's true. For true and false are attributes of speech, not of things. And where speech is not, there is neither truth nor falsehood.

@words text="Error, but not untruth"
So an animal, or a man who simply expects something that doesn't happen, makes an error. But nobody can charge him with untruth, because he made no affirmation.

@chapter Definitions first
@art bird-in-lime
If truth is an ordering of names, you'd better remember what each name stands for. Otherwise you get tangled in words like a bird in lime twigs: the more you struggle, the more stuck you get.

@art geometry-first
Geometry, Hobbes says, is the only science God has so far given mankind, and it begins by settling what its words mean. Those are definitions, and they go at the start of the reckoning, because the errors of definitions multiply as the reckoning goes on.

@quote text="words are wise mens counters, they do but reckon by them: but they are the mony of fooles," cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 4"
Men who trust books add little sums into bigger ones without checking the little ones. Words are wise men's counters, they do but reckon by them: but they are the money of fools, who value them by the authority of an Aristotle or a Cicero.

@art counters-and-coins
That was an attack on a way of teaching. In the universities of sixteen fifty one, philosophy meant, to a great extent, reading Aristotle and his commentators.

@chapter One error, three verdicts
@math heading="Bad definition" items="man = body + living + sensible@forgets rational;;man is living-creature: still true@still true;;man is rational: true → false@man is rational;;Peter is rational: true → false@Peter is rational;;just is rational: true → false@just is rational"
The lab makes the point with two presets. Suppose the definition of man forgets rational. Man is a living creature: still true. But man is rational flips from true to false. Peter is rational flips too, because Peter is defined from man. And just is rational flips as well.

@art error-spreading
One omitted part changes three verdicts, all about names defined from man. That's what multiplying looks like. And Hobbes adds that ignorance sits in the middle: a man taught wrongly by books is as far below the ignorant as true science is above them.

@chapter Names that signify nothing
@list heading="Insignificant sounds" items="New names never defined: quiddity, entity@new names|Names made of contradictory names: round quadrangle@contradict"
All other names, Hobbes says, are insignificant sounds, of two sorts. New names whose meaning is never defined, like the schools' entity and quiddity. And names made from two names whose meanings contradict, like a round quadrangle, a mere sound.

@art round-square
The lab gives those two sorts separate labels, absurd and insignificant, and that sorting is the lab's, not Hobbes's. For him, if it's false that a quadrangle is round, then round quadrangle signifies nothing. Round quadrangle is man comes out absurd. Quiddity is man, with no definition at all, comes out insignificant.

@words text="incorporeal substance" sub="a contradiction in terms?"
The stakes weren't only academic. Calling incorporeal substance a contradiction denies immaterial spirits, and Bishop Bramhall read it as a step toward atheism. Defenders reply the names were never contradictory. That's a dispute about definitions, and the lab can't settle it.

@chapter Names of the passions
@art two-scales
Last, the names of virtues and vices. One man calls wisdom what another calls fear, one cruelty what another justice. These names carry the speaker's nature and interest, so they can never be true grounds of reasoning.

@math heading="Peter is just?" items="just-by-law: just = man + observes-laws → true@by law;;just-by-might: just = man + yields-to-the-strongest → false@by might"
Two presets show it on one name. Define just by law, as a man who observes the laws, and Peter, who does, is just. Define it by might, as one who yields to the strongest, and the same Peter isn't. Same words, same man, opposite verdict.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The lab's three verdicts are Hobbes's three kinds@The first|One bad definition is one error@The second|The lab proves there are no incorporeal substances@And the third"
The first slip is taking the lab's labels for Hobbes's own kinds. The second is treating one bad definition as one error, when it spreads to everything defined from it. And the third is thinking the lab shows there are no incorporeal substances. It only shows what follows once the names are declared opposed.
