@chapter Where thoughts begin
@title text="Sense and Imagination" sub="Leviathan, Chapters 1 and 2" art=thumb
Hobbes starts the book with the thoughts of one man, taken singly. Every one of them, he says, began in sense. There's nothing in the mind that wasn't first begotten, whole or in parts, on the organs of sense.

@art pressure-inward
So what is sense? A body outside presses on an organ, directly in taste and touch, at a distance in seeing, hearing and smelling. The pressure is carried inward to the brain and heart, and the heart pushes back outward. Because that push is outward, it seems to be something out there.

@chapter Colour is motion
@quote text="(for motion, produceth nothing but motion.)" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 1"
Heat, colour, sound, hardness: in the object they're only motions of its matter, and in us they're only motions too, for motion produceth nothing but motion. What we have is the appearance, the fancy, and it's the same waking or dreaming.

@words text="The object is real" sub="what isn't in it: redness as you see it"
Notice what he doesn't say. The object is real, and it's the cause. What he denies is that redness, as you see it, is a property of the thing.

@art pressed-eye
His first argument is from the body. Press, rub or strike the eye, and you fancy a light. Press the ear, and there's a din. So when you see a body, the same fancy comes from a strong action you just don't notice.

@chapter The echo argument
@art mirror-echo
The second argument is the one the lab tests. If colours and sounds were in the objects, they couldn't be severed from them. But a mirror and an echo show them apart. We know the thing is in one place, and the appearance in another.

@math heading="Modus tollens" items="a: colours are in the objects@let a;;b: they cannot be severed@and b;;a → b,  ¬b  ∴  ¬a@if a then b"
Let a be: colours are in the objects. And b: they cannot be severed from them. The premises are if a then b, and not b. The conclusion is not a.

@math heading="Four rows" items="a true, b true: not b fails@a true, b true;;a true, b false: if a then b fails@a true, b false;;a false, b true: not b fails@a false, b true;;a false, b false: all true@a false, b false"
Two letters, four rows. A true, b true: the second premise fails. A true, b false: the conditional fails. A false, b true: the second premise fails again. A false, b false: both premises true, and so is the conclusion. No counterexample, so the argument is valid.

@chapter Deny a premise
@art two-cliffs
If something's wrong, it isn't the logic. A critic has to deny a premise. Drop the conditional, and one row appears: the colour is in the object and is still shown apart. That's the schools' view, a quality in a thing that is also carried from it.

@words text="Colour = a disposition to reflect light?" sub="then nothing was severed"
Or deny the second premise. A modern physicist might say a body's colour is its disposition to reflect some light, which stays in the body while the mirror shows only the light. Then nothing was severed. It all turns on what counts as the colour.

@chapter Decaying sense
@art fading-image
Chapter two starts with motion. A body in motion moves forever, unless something stops it. And after the object's gone, you keep an image of it, more obscure. Imagination is nothing but decaying sense, dimmed the way the sun hides the stars by day.

@quote text="So that Imagination and Memory, are but one thing, which for divers considerations hath divers names." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 2"
Call it imagination when you mean the image, and memory when you mean it's fading and past. So that imagination and memory are but one thing, which for divers considerations hath divers names. And much memory is called experience.

@chapter Dreams and ghosts
@art sleeper-and-ghost
Dreams are imagination in sleep, set going by the body's inner motions. How do you know you're awake? Hobbes's answer is modest. Awake, he knows he's not dreaming, because dreams don't hold long coherent trains, and he never dreams of the absurdities of his waking thoughts.

@words text="Fear of ghosts → fitter for obedience?" sub="Hobbes: take the fear away"
Why is this in a book about the state? Because fear of apparitions is a way to govern people. Take that superstitious fear away, Hobbes says, and men would be much fitter for civil obedience.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The world is only appearance@The first|Valid means the conclusion is shown@The second|Imagination and memory are two faculties@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Hobbes says the world is only appearance. The object is real; the colour as you have it isn't in it. The second is thinking a valid argument proves its conclusion. And the third is treating imagination and memory as two faculties.
