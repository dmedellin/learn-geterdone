@chapter A watch has a life
@title text="The Artificial Man" sub="Leviathan, Introduction" art=thumb
Hobbes opens Leviathan with one picture and one rule. The picture is a commonwealth built like a giant man. The rule is that you learn other people by reading yourself.

@art watch-springs
Start with the step that's easy to skip. Hobbes asks why we shouldn't say that a watch, an engine that moves itself by springs and wheels, has an artificial life. If life is just motion of the limbs, begun somewhere inside, then the heart is a spring and the nerves are strings.

@words text="Life is motion" sub="not an ornament, a claim"
That isn't decoration. It's a claim about what life is. And the commonwealth is the next step down the same road: art imitating nature's best work, which is man.

@quote text="For by Art is created that great LEVIATHAN called a COMMON-WEALTH, or STATE, (in latine Civitas) which is but an Artificiall Man;" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Introduction"
Here's the sentence. For by art is created that great Leviathan called a commonwealth, or state, which is but an artificial man.

@chapter Part by part
@list heading="The body of the state" items="Sovereignty: the soul@the soul|Magistrates: the joints@joints|Reward and punishment: the nerves@the nerves|Wealth: the strength@strength|Counsellors: the memory@memory|Equity and laws: reason and will@reason and will"
Go through the list slowly. The sovereignty is the soul. Magistrates and officers are the joints, and reward and punishment are the nerves that move each joint to its duty. The members' wealth is the strength, counsellors are the memory, and equity and laws are an artificial reason and will.

@quote text="Concord, Health; Sedition, Sicknesse; and Civill war, Death." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Introduction"
Health and sickness have their counterparts too. Concord, health; sedition, sickness; and civil war, death.

@art giant-of-many
The word that matters most is artificial. This man was made, by pacts and covenants, which Hobbes compares to God's let us make man. A commonwealth isn't an organism that grew. It's a device people built, so you can ask how it should be built.

@chapter A dangerous book
@words text="Paris, 1651" sub="between too great liberty and too much authority"
The book is dated from Paris in sixteen fifty one. Hobbes left England in sixteen forty as the quarrel between king and Parliament became war, and King Charles was executed in sixteen forty nine. He knew readers on both sides were looking for offence, and said it was hard to pass between them unwounded.

@art the-plan-of-four
Then the plan. First the matter and maker of the artificial man, both of which are man. Second, how and by what covenants it is made. Then a Christian commonwealth, and lastly, the kingdom of darkness.

@chapter Read thyself
@art mirror-and-others
How could anyone know what a man is made of? Hobbes answers with an old saying, read thyself. Whoever looks into himself and considers what he does when he thinks, hopes and fears, can read the thoughts and passions of all other men on like occasions.

@art key-not-message
The limit matters as much as the rule. It's the passions that are alike, desire, fear, hope, not the objects they're aimed at. What a man wants depends on his temper and upbringing, and he can hide it. Reading yourself gives you the key, not the message.

@chapter Set it out as an argument
@math heading="Three letters" items="p: the passions are the same in all men@p;;q: a man looks into himself@q;;r: he reads the passions of others@r;;p, q ∴ r ?@the conclusion"
Now test the rule as logic. Let p be: the passions are the same in all men. Let q be: a man looks into himself. And let r be: he reads the passions of others. The premises are p and q, and the conclusion is r.

@math heading="The bare argument" items="rows: 2 · 2 · 2 = 8@eight rows;;p true, q true: 2 rows@two rows;;p true, q true, r false: 1 row@one of them"
Three letters give two times two times two, eight rows. Only two rows make both premises true, and they differ only in r. One of them, alike passions, a man who looked, and reads nothing, refutes it. So as stated, the argument is invalid.

@art bridge-premise
What closes the gap is a bridge: if the passions are alike and a man looks into himself, then he reads others. Add it, and there are zero counterexample rows. Drop q but keep the bridge, and one row fails again: nobody looked, so nobody reads. The rule works only for the man who does the looking.

@chapter Valid isn't true
@quote text="For this kind of Doctrine, admitteth no other Demonstration." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Introduction"
Whether the premises are true is another matter, and the table can't say. Hobbes's test is your own introspection: consider whether you find the same in yourself. For this kind of doctrine admitteth no other demonstration.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading yourself tells you what others want@The first|The artificial man is just a metaphor@The second|A valid argument is a true one@And the third"
The first slip is thinking reading yourself tells you what others want. It gives the passions, not their objects. The second is treating the artificial man as a mere figure of speech. And the third is taking a valid argument for a true one.
