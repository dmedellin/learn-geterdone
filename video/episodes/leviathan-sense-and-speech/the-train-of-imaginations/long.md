@chapter A strange question
@title text="The Train of Imaginations" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 3" art=thumb
In a conversation about the civil war, someone asked what a Roman penny was worth. It sounds like nonsense. Hobbes says the connection was plain.

@art chain-of-thoughts
The war brought up the thought of the King delivered to his enemies. That brought up Christ delivered up, and that, the thirty pence, the price of the betrayal. And out came the question, all in a moment, because thought is quick. Hobbes calls it malicious.

@quote text="Not every Thought to every Thought succeeds indifferently." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 3"
That's the general point. Not every thought to every thought succeeds indifferently. Thoughts that followed one another in sense keep together afterward, like water on a table drawn wherever a finger leads it.

@chapter Two kinds of train
@art wandering-and-guided
Some trains wander, with no desire to steer them, as in a dream. Others are regulated by desire and design. From the end you want, you think of means, then means to those means, until you reach something in your own power.

@list heading="A regulated train seeks" items="Causes of an effect: man and beast@causes|Effects of a cause: man only@possible effects"
That kind comes in two forms. Seeing an effect, you look for its causes, and beasts do that too. Imagining a thing, you look for all its possible effects, what you could do with it. Hobbes says he never saw a sign of that except in man.

@chapter Foresight
@art crime-to-gallows
Now the case. A man wants to know what will happen to an offender. He recalls what he's seen follow such crimes before: the crime, the officer, the prison, the judge, the gallows. That's foresight, or prudence.

@quote text="And though it be called Prudence, when the Event answereth our Expectation; yet in its own nature, it is but Presumption." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 3"
Only the present exists in nature. The future is a fiction of the mind, laying past sequels onto present actions. And though it be called prudence when the event answereth our expectation, yet in its own nature it is but presumption.

@words text="The oftener observed, the less uncertain the sign"
What makes the presumption is a sign: an event that came before a consequence, when the like has been seen before. And the oftener they've been observed, the less uncertain the sign. Hobbes doesn't say how much less.

@chapter Watching the court
@art two-benches
To see the shape of that, the lesson builds a model Hobbes didn't have. There are two possible benches. A severe one hangs four in five offenders. A lenient one hangs one in five. The man starts with no opinion, half and half.

@math heading="One hanging" items="severe: 1/2 · 4/5 = 2/5@a half times;;lenient: 1/2 · 1/5 = 1/10@one tenth;;severe: (2/5) / (1/2) = 4/5@four fifths;;next hanged: 4/5 · 4/5 + 1/5 · 1/5 = 17/25@seventeen"
He sees one hanging. The severe bench gets a half times four fifths, two fifths. The lenient gets one tenth. Together that's a half, so the severe bench is now at four fifths. The chance the next one hangs is seventeen twenty fifths.

@math heading="More observations" items="three hangings: 64/65 severe@sixty four over sixty five;;next: 257/325@two hundred;;two hangings, one pardon: 4/5@a pardon"
Three hangings in a row give the severe bench sixty four over sixty five, and the next hangs with chance two hundred fifty seven over three hundred twenty five. But two hangings and a pardon leave it back at four fifths. The pardon cancels one hanging. It's how the observations fall, not how many.

@chapter How far experience carries
@math heading="Six hangings" items="severe: 4096/4097@four thousand;;next hanged: 3277/4097@three thousand;;never above 4/5@four in five"
After six hangings, the severe bench is at four thousand ninety six over four thousand ninety seven. As good as certain. Yet the next offender hangs with chance three thousand two hundred seventy seven over four thousand ninety seven, just under four in five. Knowing the court perfectly can't beat the court's own habit.

@art guesser-signs
That's why Hobbes calls such conjecture fallacious, even in the best informed man. The best prophet, naturally, is the best guesser, the one with the most signs to guess by. And prudence isn't what sets man apart: some beasts at a year old are more prudent than a child of ten.

@art endless-path
The chapter ends on infinity. Whatever we imagine is finite, since every image began in sense. So when we call a thing infinite, we only mean we can't conceive its ends. We have no conception of the thing, only of our own inability.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="More experience makes it certain@The first|Counting observations instead of reading them@The second|Prudence separates man from beast@And the third"
The first slip is thinking more experience makes a prediction certain. It makes the sign less uncertain, never sure. The second is counting observations instead of reading them. And the third is thinking prudence is what separates man from beast.
