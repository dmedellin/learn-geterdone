@chapter One and more than one
@title text="One Noun, Two Nouns" sub="Nouns and Articles" art=thumb
Every noun you can count has two forms: one for one thing, and one for more than one. The second is called the plural.

@art one-to-many
You've already met the main rule. It's the same s rule that goes on a verb after he, she and it. Let's run it on nouns, and count how often it's right.

@chapter Three parts
@math heading="The plural rule" items="cat → cats  ·  day → days@add s;;box → boxes  ·  church → churches@after a hiss;;city → cities  ·  baby → babies@consonant and y"
The rule has three parts, and they're all about the end of the word. Add s: cat, cats. Day, days. Add e-s after a hiss: box, boxes, church, churches. And after a consonant and y, change the y to i-e-s: city, cities.

@words text="day → days · boy → boys" sub="a vowel before y keeps it"
A vowel before the y keeps it, so you just add s. Day, days. Boy, boys. Key, keys.

@chapter How often it's right
@math heading="1,887 nouns, checked against a dictionary" items="right for 1,866  ·  98.9%@one thousand eight hundred and sixty-six;;wrong for 21@twenty-one"
The rule was run on one thousand eight hundred and eighty-seven nouns from a list of common words, and each answer was checked against a dictionary. It's right for one thousand eight hundred and sixty-six. That's ninety-eight point nine percent. It's wrong for twenty-one.

@art big-meter
That's a rule you can use without stopping to think. And the twenty-one it misses aren't random. Each one has a reason.

@chapter Reading the 21
@list heading="Five reasons" items="9 old plurals: men, feet, children@Nine are old plural|5 f words: half, life, self, shelf, wife@Five end in f|5 from Greek: crisis → crises@Five come from Greek|potato@Potato|stomach@stomach"
Nine are old plural forms that no ending makes, like man, men, foot, feet, and child, children. Five end in f or f-e, and take v-e-s: half, life, self, shelf and wife. Five come from Greek, like crisis. And two are single cases: potato, and stomach.

@art old-forms
Seven of the nine old plurals change the vowel in the middle: man, woman, foot, tooth, mouse, chairman and gentleman. Child adds an ending of its own, children. And die has dice.

@art five-short-lists
Stomach ends in c h, but those letters say k, so the hiss part gives the wrong form. That's five reasons, and the longest list has nine words. The rule plus five short lists is enough.

@chapter Three ways to improve it
@math heading="Add: consonant + o → -oes" items="potato → potatoes  ✓@It gains potato;;photo, piano, pro  ✗@It loses photo;;1,866 → 1,864@falls by two"
A natural fix is to add a part: after a consonant and o, add e-s. It gains potato. But it loses photo, piano and pro. So the score falls by two, to one thousand eight hundred and sixty-four.

@art gain-one-lose-three
The spelling can't tell potato from photo. Both end in a consonant and o, and nothing in the letters says that one takes e-s.

@math heading="Add: f or fe → ves" items="fixes half, life, self, shelf, wife@It fixes;;breaks belief, brief, chief, golf, proof, relief, roof, safe@breaks;;1,864 → 1,861@falls to"
Now add the idea that f or f-e becomes v-e-s. It fixes half, life, self, shelf and wife. But it breaks belief, brief, chief, golf, proof, relief, roof and safe. Five fixed, eight broken. The score falls to one thousand eight hundred and sixty-one.

@chapter The version that helps
@words text="-lf · -ife · -eaf · -olf → ves" sub="1,868 of 1,887 · 99.0%"
The third change names its endings exactly: use v-e-s only after l f, i f e, e a f, or o l f. That raises the score to one thousand eight hundred and sixty-eight, ninety-nine percent. It breaks just one, golf.

@art narrow-rule
But the gain is only two nouns, and the cost is a rule with a list inside it. For most learners, the better choice is the plain rule and the short lists.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Writing f becomes ves as the rule@The first|Adding -es to every noun ending in o@The second|Treating men and feet as mistakes in the rule@the third"
The first slip is writing f becomes v-e-s as the rule. It breaks more than it fixes. The second is adding e-s to every noun that ends in o. And the third is treating an old plural as a mistake in the rule. Men and feet came before the s rule.

Your turn. Which of these does the rule get wrong: box, city, day, or woman? Box, city and day are all right by the rule. Woman becomes women, an old plural.
