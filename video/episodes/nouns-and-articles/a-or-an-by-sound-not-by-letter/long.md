@chapter The rule you were taught
@title text="A or An: by Sound, Not by Letter" sub="Nouns and Articles" art=thumb
You were probably taught that an goes before a vowel letter: a, e, i, o or u. That rule is about letters. And letters are the wrong thing to look at.

@art letter-vs-sound
Here's a test. Take four hundred and ninety-three printed lines, each with a or an and the word that follows. A dictionary says how each next word begins. Then score the letter rule on every line.

@math heading="The letter rule" items="right on 486 of 493  ·  98.6%@four hundred and eighty-six;;a University  ·  a utilitarian  ·  an hour ×5@a University"
It's right on four hundred and eighty-six of them. That's ninety-eight point six percent. Seven lines break it: a University, a utilitarian, and an hour, which one play uses five times.

@chapter The rule about sounds
@math heading="The sound rule" items="an before a vowel sound@an before a vowel sound;;right on 493 of 493@all four hundred and ninety-three"
Now change the question. Use an before a vowel sound, not a vowel letter. The rule is right on all four hundred and ninety-three lines. Nothing about the lines changed. Only the question changed.

@art two-vowels-meet
Why sounds? A and an are one word in two shapes. The shape that ends in n is there so that two vowel sounds don't meet: an egg, not a egg. The reason is spoken, so the rule is spoken.

@words text="letter rule = a short way of writing the sound rule"
So why was the rule ever about letters? Most words begin with the sound their first letter suggests, and letters are easier to teach. It's a good short way. It's just not the reason.

@chapter Step by step
@list heading="Four steps" items="Say the next word, listen to how it begins@Say the next word|Vowel sound? → an@Is that sound a vowel|Consonant sound? → a@consonant sound|Check: u like you, silent h, one@Check the three kinds"
Say the next word, and listen only to how it begins. Is that sound a vowel? Then use an: an hour, an egg, an answer. If it's a consonant sound, use a: a University, a house. Then check the three kinds that fool the letter.

@chapter Three words that fool the letter
@math heading="Letter and sound disagree" items="a University  ·  u said like you  ·  /juː/@a University;;a utilitarian  ·  u said like you@a utilitarian;;an hour  ·  h not said  ·  /aʊ/@an hour"
In a University, the u is said like the word you, which starts with a consonant sound. A utilitarian is the same. And in an hour, the h isn't said at all. Hour begins with the vowel sound in our.

@art silent-h
The first two begin with a vowel letter said as a consonant. The third begins with a consonant letter that isn't said. Five of the seven misses are an hour.

@art you-sound
So you and one begin with a consonant sound, whatever the letter says. Remember three kinds: a u said like you, a silent h, and one. For everything else, the letter and the sound agree.

@words text="a one" sub="one begins with the sound of w"
There's a third kind: one. It begins with the sound of w, so it takes a, as in a one. It isn't among the seven here, but it shows up in a whole novel.

@chapter Three sources, one result
@math heading="Both rules, three sources" items="the play  ·  letter 448 of 455  ·  sound 455 of 455@The play has;;two modern documents  ·  both 29 of 29@modern documents;;passage from the novel  ·  both 9 of 9@passage"
The play has four hundred and fifty-five lines. The letter rule is right on four hundred and forty-eight, and the sound rule on all of them. On two modern documents, both rules are right on twenty-nine of twenty-nine. On the passage from the novel, both are right on nine of nine.

@art three-sources
The modern documents show that the two rules agree for most words. The play is where they part, and all seven lines that separate them are in it.

@chapter Austen's habit
@quote text="an union · an uniform" cite="Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, 1813"
Over the whole of Pride and Prejudice, counted once, the sound rule is right on two thousand two hundred and sixty-three of two thousand two hundred and sixty-six lines. The three it misses are an union, twice, and an uniform.

@art old-habit
That's Austen's habit in eighteen thirteen, and the habit has gone. Union begins with the sound of you, so today it's a union. And one more limit: the dictionary uses American sounds, and another kind of English may say a word a little differently.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Choosing by the vowel letter@The first|Copying an union from an old book@The second|Reading unknown words as errors@the third"
The first slip is choosing by the vowel letter. It's a good guide, but a bad reason. The second is treating an union as correct because an old book has it. And the third is reading the seventeen lines the dictionary can't score as errors. They're just not scored.

@words text="a hour · an hour · an university · an one" sub="which one is right?"
Your turn. Which is right: a hour, an hour, an university, or an one? Say each word. Hour begins with a vowel sound, so it's an hour.
