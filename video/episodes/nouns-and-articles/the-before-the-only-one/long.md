@chapter Only one
@title text="The Before the Only One" sub="Nouns and Articles" art=thumb
Take a word that names the one that's more than all the others: tallest, youngest, greatest. Only one thing can be the tallest. So the small word before it is the.

@art one-of-group
The same is true of same, which names the one that's no different. The tallest girl is one girl. The same thing is one thing. When only one can fit, you point to it with the.

@words text="her youngest sister" sub="a possessive points to the one too"
A possessive, like her or our, points to it as well: her youngest sister. But not both. A possessive takes the place of the. It doesn't join it.

@art possessive-swap
So either one points to the one: the youngest sister, or her youngest sister. In the count you'll see next, ninety-three lines have the, and thirty-four have a possessive.

@chapter Counting it
@math heading="405 lines from Pride and Prejudice" items="-est word  ·  127 of 142  ·  89.4%@one hundred and twenty-seven;;same  ·  69 of 69  ·  100%@sixty-nine;;next  ·  49 of 72  ·  68.1%@forty-nine"
Four hundred and five lines from Pride and Prejudice were checked, looking at the word just before. With an e-s-t word, the rule holds on one hundred and twenty-seven of one hundred and forty-two lines. With same, it holds on sixty-nine of sixty-nine, every one. With next, forty-nine of seventy-two.

@art same-one
Same takes the on every single line. That's the strongest rule in this whole course. Next is the weak one of the three.

@art next-alone
Next is weaker for a simple reason. It's often a time word that stands alone: next week, next year. Next to names a place. And in when they next met, it isn't before a noun at all.

@chapter What the 15 are
@math heading="Fifteen that look like misses" items="the two youngest  ·  the is a word earlier@the two youngest;;the best and safest  ·  one the for two words@the best and safest;;it would be wisest  ·  no noun follows@it would be wisest;;dearest Lizzy  ·  a way of calling her@dearest Lizzy"
The fifteen lines that don't fit are four kinds. In the two youngest, the is there, just one word earlier. In the best and safest, one the covers two words. In it would be wisest, there's no noun to point to. And dearest Lizzy is a way of calling someone.

@art scan-one-word
None of these breaks the rule. The scan reads just one word before, so it can't see them. Each is a correct sentence that the scan can't judge.

@chapter The word that is two words
@math heading="most + adjective  ·  122 lines" items="the most  ·  37@the small word before most is the;;a most  ·  45@a or an forty-five;;rule holds  ·  40 of 122  ·  32.8%@forty of"
Now choose most. The small word before most is the thirty-seven times, and a or an forty-five times. So the rule holds on only forty of one hundred and twenty-two lines. Thirty-two point eight percent.

@words text="a most delightful evening" sub="most = very"
The forty-five are the lesson. A most delightful evening, a most excellent ball. These don't name the best evening, or the best ball. Most means very, and a is the small word you'd use before any adjective and noun.

@words text="she was most kind" sub="most = very, no small word"
Fifteen more follow was, been, am, is, be or seemed. She was most kind. There too, most means very.

@art two-jobs-most
So most has two jobs. The most beautiful names the one. A most agreeable man is just a very agreeable man. This meaning is old and formal, and less common today.

@chapter What to do
@list heading="Ask what the word is doing" items="Does it pick one from a group? → the@pick the one|-est or same? → the, or a possessive@ends in e-s-t|most = the highest → the most@the highest|most = very → a most, or no small word@means very|next week, next year → stands alone@time word"
Does the word pick the one from a group? If it ends in e-s-t, or it's same, put the before it, or a possessive. If most means the highest, use the most: the most beautiful of the sisters. If it means very, use a most, or no small word. And as a time word, next stands alone.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking most always makes a superlative@The first|her the youngest ✗@The second|Reading a miss as a mistake in the writing@the third"
The first slip is thinking most always makes a superlative. A most agreeable man isn't the most agreeable of all. The second is using the and a possessive together. And the third is reading a miss in the count as a mistake in the writing.
