@chapter Who gains?
@title text="Differential Rent II at a Constant Price of Production" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 41" art=thumb
When a farmer adds capital to his field and the price of corn doesn't move, who gains? Marx's answer is the landowner, at the next lease. It takes him four cases to show how much.

@art same-as-a
If the added capital on a better soil yields only what the same capital yields on A, it earns the average profit and nothing more. Everything remains as it is. It's as if more acres of A had been ploughed. The other three cases are the interesting ones. Soil A still sets the price at sixty shillings a quarter.

@chapter Proportional doses
@art doubled-doses
First, Marx's Table Two. Every soil takes a second capital exactly as productive as its first. A yields one and one, B two and two, C three and three, D four and four.

@math heading="Table II: every dose repeated" items="B 120s · C 240s · D 360s@a hundred and twenty;;total: 720s@seven hundred and twenty;;rate of rent: 720/400 = 360/200 = 180%@a hundred and eighty percent"
The rents double. B pays a hundred and twenty, C two hundred and forty, D three hundred and sixty, and the total is seven hundred and twenty shillings. The rent per acre has doubled, and so has the price of land. But the rate of rent hasn't moved: on the capital, it's a hundred and eighty percent either way.

@chapter Slower doses
@art smaller-second
Second, Table Three. The second doses are less productive than the first, but still beat A. B's second dose yields one and a half quarters, C's two, and D's three and a half.

@math heading="Table III: smaller second doses" items="B: 60s → 90s@ninety;;C: 120s → 180s@a hundred and eighty;;D: 180s → 330s@three hundred and thirty;;total: 600s@six hundred"
At sixty shillings, B's rent goes from sixty to ninety, C's from a hundred and twenty to a hundred and eighty, and D's from a hundred and eighty to three hundred and thirty. The total is six hundred shillings.

@math heading="Soil B, two doses" items="first dose: 2 · 60 − 60 = 60s@first dose;;second dose: (3/2) · 60 − 60 = 30s@second dose;;rent per acre: 60 → 90s@per acre;;rate of rent: 60/50 = 120% → 90/100 = 90%@rate of rent"
Look at B. The first dose makes sixty shillings of surplus, the second dose thirty. So the rent per acre rises from sixty to ninety. But the rate of rent on the capital falls from a hundred and twenty percent to ninety.

@art rate-vs-acre
So the two measures part ways. Rent per acre is rent divided by the land. The rate of rent is rent divided by the capital. The first rises while the second falls.

@quote text="But this is the law: That the rent increases absolutely upon all these classes of soil, although not in proportion to the additional capital invested." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 41"
But this is the law: that the rent increases absolutely upon all these classes of soil, although not in proportion to the additional capital invested.

@chapter Faster doses
@words text="Second doses yield 3, 4 and 5" sub="B 180s · C 300s · D 420s · total 900s"
Third, the second doses yield more than the first. Marx gives no figures here, so the lesson's are illustrative: three on B, four on C, five on D. Rents are a hundred and eighty, three hundred and four hundred and twenty, nine hundred in all. Rent per acre rises faster than the capital.

@chapter Two countries
@art two-countries
Marx's consequence is about countries. Take two with the same prices, the same soils, the same farm capital, but one concentrates it on a smaller area. Total rent can be the same, but rent per acre, and the price of land, is higher in the first. That comes solely from how the capital is invested. As capitalist farming concentrates, land gets dearer.

@chapter Where does it stop?
@art stopping-point
A reader who knows diminishing returns will push back. Each added dose on B yields less. Surely the farmer stops? He does, and the chapter agrees where: when a dose would yield no more than the same capital on A, because beyond that it doesn't pay the average profit. Up to that point, every dose raises the rent per acre.

@words text="Table III's D: 3½ quarters" sub="Chapter 40's sketch disagrees"
A note on D. Chapter Forty's first sketch of this case gives D three different yields that don't agree with each other. The lesson follows Table Three, the table this chapter computes with, where D's second dose yields three and a half quarters.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="More output always lowers rent per acre@The first|Rent per acre confused with the rate of rent@The second|Chapter 40's figure for D at face value@And the third"
The first slip is thinking more output always lowers rent per acre. The second is confusing rent per acre with the rate of rent. And the third is taking Chapter Forty's figure for D at face value.
