@chapter More land, or the same land harder?
@title text="Differential Rent II" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 40" art=thumb
Think of the difference between farming more land and farming the same land harder. A farmer with more money can rent the next field. Or he can put more manure, labour and machinery on the field he has.

@art spread-vs-stack
The first is how the last lesson's rent arose. The second is this chapter's, and Marx says it's the one that grows as capitalist agriculture develops. Economically, intensive cultivation is the concentration of capital on the same piece of land, instead of spreading it over neighbouring pieces.

@chapter Four doses on one acre
@art four-doses
His test is direct. Take Table One's capital, in four equal portions of fifty shillings. Instead of spreading them over one acre each of A, B, C and D, lay them one after another on a single acre of D. The first yields four quarters, the second three, the third two, and the fourth one.

@math heading="One acre of D, four doses" items="cost and profit: 60 shillings a dose@sixty shillings;;price: 60 / 1 = 60s (dose 4)@the fourth dose;;doses 1 to 3: (4 + 3 + 2) · 60 − 3 · 60 = 360@the first three;;dose 4: 1 · 60 − 60 = 0@itself;;rent of the acre: 360s@three hundred and sixty"
Each dose costs sixty shillings with profit. The fourth dose, yielding one quarter, regulates the price at sixty, as A did. The first three earn surplus profits that add to three hundred and sixty. The fourth earns nothing itself. So the acre's rent is three hundred and sixty shillings, the very total of the four soils side by side.

@quote text="It is evident, in that case, that differential rent No. II is but a different expression of differential rent No. I, but that it coincides with it in substance." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 40"
It is evident, in that case, that differential rent number two is but a different expression of differential rent number one, but that it coincides with it in substance.

@chapter A case in between
@math heading="A second dose on D" items="D's first dose: 180s@a hundred and eighty;;D's second dose, 3 quarters: 120s@a hundred and twenty;;total rent: 360s → 480s@four hundred and eighty"
Now a case in between, with an illustrative yield. All four soils are farmed, and D takes a second fifty shillings that yields three quarters. A still sets the price at sixty. D's first dose pays a hundred and eighty, its second a hundred and twenty, and total rent rises to four hundred and eighty, with no change in price.

@art rentless-dose
And if D's second dose yields only one quarter, like A? Then it's a rentless investment. It earns the average profit and no more, and nothing changes for anyone.

@chapter What's different: the lease
@art lease-clock
If the sums are the same, what's different? The conversion into rent. On separate fields, a landowner can see what each acre yields and set the rent. On one field, capital added during a lease earns its surplus for the tenant. The rent is fixed when the lease is made out, and only at renewal can the landlord claim it.

@art tenant-and-landlord
Hence, Marx says, English tenants resisted farm statistics and fought for long leases, while landlords preferred tenancies that could end every year. And the valuer who assesses rents becomes a very important, complicated and difficult profession.

@chapter Ricardo's one case
@art diminishing-doses
The quarrel is again with Ricardo, who made this form one case only: extra capital on land already farmed, with diminishing returns, so the price of corn rises, wages rise, profits fall, and rents climb. Marx keeps that case, but says Ricardo stamped it as the only case, the normal case.

@words text="Diminishing doses, constant price"
Every later textbook calls it diminishing returns: add more of one input to fixed land, and each addition yields less. But most of Marx's own doses diminish, and the price doesn't move. It's set by the least productive investment the market needs, not by the last one made on the best field.

@words text="Marx's slip: “12 pounds sterling”" sub="dose by dose at 30s: 300s − 240s = 60s"
One caution. In a reversed case, where B sets the price at thirty shillings, Marx reckons D's rent at twelve pounds by setting four doses against one. Valued dose by dose, it's sixty shillings, three pounds. His point, that price can fall while rent rises, survives, and the next lessons compute it properly.

@list heading="Common slips" items="A different source of rent from the first form@The first|Diminishing returns must raise the price@The second|Marx's 12 pounds as a correct rent@And the third"
