@chapter Dear bread, rich landlords
@title text="Differential Rent II at a Rising Price of Production" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 43" art=thumb
Bread gets dearer and landlords get richer. Anyone in England in eighteen fifteen could see the two go together, and the obvious conclusion was that rents made bread dear.

@art bread-and-manor
Ricardo's reply was that this had cause and effect backwards. Corn is dear because the last land the country needs is poor and costly to farm. Rent is only what the better land earns at that price. Abolish every rent and corn wouldn't fall a penny. Marx agrees with the first half, and adds a qualification to the second.

@words text="Chapter 43 opens with Engels" sub="the manuscript had “only its title”"
A note on authorship. The chapter's opening is Engels's, and he says so: the manuscript had only its title. His tables mostly price each soil's whole product at its average, so the lesson takes its cases from Marx's own pages instead.

@chapter A weaker dose on B
@art weak-dose
Marx's soil B yields two quarters on its first fifty shillings and one and a half on its second. A yields one, and the price is sixty. Now the market needs one and a half quarters more, and the only way to get them is a further hundred shillings on B, at eighty shillings a quarter.

@math heading="The extra corn costs 80s" items="price: 60s → 80s@eighty;;A: 0 → 20s@a rent would be formed upon A;;B: 90s → 160s@a hundred and sixty"
If the market demands them, the regulating price must rise to eighty. The rent on B rises for its first and second doses, and a rent would be formed upon A. A goes from nothing to twenty shillings, and B from ninety to a hundred and sixty.

@chapter Soils worse than A
@art worse-than-a
Or the extra quarters come from soils worse than A, which Marx calls A minus one and A minus two. One and a half quarters on A minus one, at eighty shillings a quarter. And the last quarter on A minus two, at a hundred and twenty.

@math heading="Marx's soils A−1 and A−2" items="A−2: 120 / 1 = 120 shillings a quarter@a hundred and twenty;;A: 120 − 60 = 60 shillings@A's quarter;;B: (7/2) · 120 − 120 = 300 shillings@three and a half;;A−1: (3/2) · 120 − 120 = 60 shillings@A minus one;;total: 60 + 300 + 60 + 0 = 420 shillings@four hundred and twenty"
Now a hundred and twenty regulates. A's quarter leaves a rent of sixty. B's three and a half quarters leave three hundred. A minus one leaves sixty, and A minus two nothing. The total is four hundred and twenty shillings.

@chapter Ricardo's one case
@art weak-on-d
Third, the case Marx says Ricardo made the only one. All four soils are farmed, and the extra corn comes from a second dose on the best soil, D, yielding only three quarters of a quarter, an illustrative figure. The price rises to eighty, and every rent rises with it: A twenty, B a hundred, C a hundred and eighty, D two hundred and sixty.

@words text="“Differential rent would then rise in connection with a falling rate of profit.”"
In Chapter Forty, Marx describes this case: rent rising on all soils, as though something worse than A regulated, and wages rising with the price of bread. Differential rent would then rise in connection with a falling rate of profit.

@chapter The lease hastens the rise
@art lease-squeeze
Now Marx's qualification. A tenant selling his whole acre could let strong doses cover weak ones, and the rent would be absorbed. But he's signed a lease. Once he pays the rent, he must keep paying it, so every weak dose is a straight loss to him. He stops adding capital sooner, and the extra corn must come from somewhere dearer, sooner.

@quote text="the existence of differential rent as rent is at the same time a reason for the earlier and more rapid rise of the general price of production" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 43"
So rent doesn't set the price, but the existence of differential rent as rent is at the same time a reason for the earlier and more rapid rise of the general price of production.

@chapter After repeal
@art drainage-rents
Engels closes with history. When the corn duties were repealed in eighteen forty six, manufacturers expected the landlords' ruin. Instead tenants invested more per acre, landlords voted themselves drainage subsidies, and rents rose. Ruin came later, from the prairies and the pampas.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Rent causes high food prices@The first|Diminishing returns always raise the price@The second|Engels's opening taken for Marx's@And the third"
The first slip is thinking rent is the cause of high food prices. The second is thinking diminishing returns always raise the price. The last lesson showed they needn't. And the third is taking Engels's opening for Marx's.
