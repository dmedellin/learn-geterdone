@chapter Three ways to one more quarter
@title text="Differential Rent Also on the Worst Cultivated Soil" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 44" art=thumb
Picture three ways a country can grow one more quarter of wheat. Plough some worse land. Put a second capital on its worst land. Or put another capital on a better field.

@art three-routes
Whichever is cheapest per quarter will be used, and whichever is used sets the price. Marx prices all three. A yields one quarter at sixty shillings. B yields three and a half from two doses, with C and D better still.

@math heading="The extra quarter" items="on worse soil A−1: 80s@eighty;;a second dose on A: 75s@seventy five;;another dose on B: 70s@seventy"
The extra quarter would cost eighty shillings on a worse soil, seventy five from a second dose on A, and seventy from another dose on B.

@chapter B's dose regulates
@art cheapest-wins
B's is cheapest, so B's extra dose regulates the price at seventy shillings. If the quarter cost more on B than on A, Marx says, it would naturally be produced on A instead. The cheapest source that fills the market is the one used.

@math heading="Marx's extra quarter on B" items="cheapest extra source: B, at 70 shillings@B, at seventy;;A: 70 − 60 = 10 shillings@ten shillings;;B, first two doses: (7/2) · 70 − 120 = 125@a hundred and twenty five;;B, extra dose: no surplus at its own price@no surplus;;C 265s · D 405s · total 805s@eight hundred and five"
So B, at seventy, regulates. A's single quarter costs sixty and sells for seventy, so A earns ten shillings. B's first two doses earn a hundred and twenty five. B's extra dose has no surplus at its own price. C and D pay two hundred and sixty five and four hundred and five, eight hundred and five in all.

@quote text="As soon as differential rent No. II comes into action by successive investments of capital, the limits of the rising price of production may be regulated by better soil, and the worst soil, the basis of differential rent No. I, may also carry a rent." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 44"
That's the chapter's point. As soon as differential rent number two comes into action by successive investments of capital, the limits of the rising price of production may be regulated by better soil, and the worst soil, the basis of differential rent number one, may also carry a rent.

@chapter A's own second dose
@art second-dose-on-a
Now the second route: A's own second dose, at seventy five. If A's two quarters are averaged, a hundred and thirty five shillings for two, A pays no rent. But if the second quarter sells at its own seventy five, A's first capital at sixty earns fifteen.

@words text="A 15s · B 142s 6d · C 292s 6d · D 442s 6d"
Then B's rent rises to a hundred and forty two shillings and six pence, C's to two hundred and ninety two and six, and D's to four hundred and forty two and six. That lasts only while no fresh land of A's quality is on offer, unless the landlord has fixed the surplus in a lease.

@art worse-soil-instead
And worse soil, A minus one at eighty, would be a different case. A would pay twenty shillings, but as the better of two soils, under differential rent one.

@chapter Engels objects
@art engels-average
Engels, editing the chapter, objected in a bracketed note. B's tenant, he argues, has to meet his costs, profit and the rent already fixed in his lease, and that average per quarter becomes the regulating price. On his rule the price is about sixty two shillings, A pays only one ninth of a pound, and B keeps its old rent.

@words text="Marx: dose by dose · Engels: the acre's average"
Engels's rule is the acre's average price, which the last lesson met. Marx's figures value the extra dose by itself, and the lesson follows Marx. Both men reach the chapter's point: a better soil can set the price, and the worst soil can pay rent.

@chapter The marginal view
@art four-rents
A later economist would put the chapter in a sentence: the price is set by the marginal source of supply, whichever field it lies on, and every unit inside the margin earns a rent. Nothing is special about the worst soil. What the chapter adds is the lease.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The worst soil can never pay differential rent@The first|The dearest source regulates@The second|Marx's figure for A as settled@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the worst soil can never pay differential rent. The second is assuming the dearest available source regulates. It's the cheapest that fills the market. And the third is treating Marx's figure for A as settled, when Engels disputed it.
