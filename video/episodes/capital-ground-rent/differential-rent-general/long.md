@chapter Steam and water
@title text="Differential Rent: General Remarks" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 38" art=thumb
Marx starts the theory of rent away from the farm, on purpose. Most of a country's factories run on steam. A few run on natural waterfalls.

@art steam-and-water
On steam, a capital of a hundred makes goods that sell at a hundred and fifteen: the cost plus the average profit of fifteen percent. That's the regulating price for the whole branch. The factory on the waterfall costs only ninety, because the water wheel need not be heated. And it still sells at a hundred and fifteen.

@chapter Two measures
@math heading="Steam and water" items="price: 100 + 15% of 100 = 115@a hundred and fifteen;;water user's profit: 115 − 90 = 25@twenty five;;against the steam user: 25 − 15 = 10@ten;;water user's own price: 90 + 15% of 90 = 103½@a hundred and three;;against its own price: 115 − 103½ = 11½@eleven and a half"
The price is a hundred and fifteen. The water user's profit is twenty five, against the steam user's fifteen: a surplus profit of ten. That's the measure Marx carries forward. He also defines it a second way. The water user's own price of production is ninety plus fifteen percent, a hundred and three and a half. Against that, the surplus is eleven and a half.

@words text="10 or 11½?" sub="15 in money, or 15% on his own cost"
Why two? The first treats the water user as entitled to fifteen pounds, the steam user's profit. The second gives him fifteen percent on his own smaller cost. The lessons that follow use the first.

@chapter What's special
@art better-machine
So far, this is ordinary. Any capitalist with a better machine makes a surplus profit, and competition wears it away as the method spreads. What's different here? The waterfall's advantage is a natural power capital can't make. Where it doesn't exist, no investment can create it. It's a monopolised natural power, tied to particular pieces of the globe.

@art owner-collects
Now let someone own those pieces. The manufacturer pays the owner of the waterfall ten pounds a year, keeps fifteen on his hundred like everyone else, and the surplus profit has become ground rent. If he owned the fall himself, he'd collect the ten as a landowner.

@quote text="Hence private property in land does not create that portion of value, which is transformed into surplus profit, but it merely enables the landowner, who has possession of the waterfall, to coax this surplus profit out of the pocket of the industrial capitalist into his own." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 38"
Hence private property in land does not create that portion of value, which is transformed into surplus profit, but it merely enables the landowner, who has possession of the waterfall, to coax this surplus profit out of the pocket of the industrial capitalist into his own.

@chapter Basis, not source
@quote text="The natural power is not the source of the surplus profit, but only its natural basis, because this natural basis permits an increase in the productive power of labor." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 38"
Here's the claim the whole Part leans on. The natural power is not the source of the surplus profit, but only its natural basis, because this natural basis permits an increase in the productive power of labor.

@art steam-cheapens
On Marx's definitions, the water adds no value. It lets the same labour make more. The surplus exists because the goods sell at the price set by steam. His test: if a new method cuts the steam users' cost to ninety, the price falls to a hundred and three and a half, and the waterfall's surplus is nothing. The waterfall hasn't changed. The rent is gone.

@math heading="A better fall: cost 80" items="against the steam user: 115 − 80 − 15 = 20@twenty;;against its own price: 115 − 92 = 23@twenty three"
Put the water user's cost at eighty instead. At the same hundred and fifteen, the surplus is twenty against the steam user, and twenty three against its own price of ninety two.

@art two-falls
A better natural advantage makes a bigger difference. That's what differential means: rent measured by a difference in productivity, one capital against the others in its branch.

@words text="10 / (1/20) = 200" sub="the price of the waterfall"
The waterfall even has a price. Ten a year at five percent interest is the income of a capital of two hundred pounds, and that appears as the capital value of the waterfall itself.

@chapter The other view
@art waterfall-productive
An economist after the eighteen seventies would accept every number here, and still say the waterfall is productive: remove it and output falls, so it earns its rent as machines do. Marx's answer is that the natural power raises labour's productivity, and value is labour. Which description is better, the course leaves open.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The waterfall creates the surplus profit@The first|The owner's title creates the rent@The second|Mixing up the two measures@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the waterfall creates the surplus profit. The second is thinking the landowner's title creates the substance of the rent. It only transfers it. And the third is mixing up the two measures.
