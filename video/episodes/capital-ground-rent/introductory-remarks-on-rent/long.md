@chapter Paying for the earth
@title text="Introductory Remarks on Ground Rent" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 37" art=thumb
If you rent a flat, or know someone who farms rented land, you already know what this Part is about: a payment made every year to someone, for the use of a piece of the earth they own and you don't.

@art three-classes
Marx sets it in Volume Three's world. The land is worked by wage labourers, employed by a capitalist farmer, who pays the landowner a sum fixed by contract for permission to invest his capital there. That sum is ground rent. Now all three classes are on stage: wage labourers, capitalists and landowners.

@chapter Not all of it is rent
@art drains-and-barns
First warning: a lease payment isn't all rent. A farmer may drain a field, put up buildings, manure it for years. That's land capital. The interest on it may be part of what the landowner collects, but it's interest on capital, not rent for the soil itself.

@math heading="A payment of £200" items="interest on drains and buildings: £50@fifty of interest;;true rent: £200 − £50 = £150@true rent"
In the lesson's illustration, a payment of two hundred pounds includes fifty of interest on drains and buildings. The true rent is one hundred and fifty. When the lease ends, the improvements stay with the land, and the next lease charges for them.

@chapter The price of land
@math heading="Marx's £200 a year" items="price at 5%: £200 / (1/20) = £4,000@four thousand;;years' purchase: £4,000 / £200 = 20@twenty years"
Second warning, about the price. Marx's figures: a rent of two hundred pounds a year, and interest at five percent. Someone who pays four thousand pounds for the land gets five percent on his money, as if he'd lent it out. In England this was reckoned in years' purchase. Four thousand is twenty years of rent.

@quote text="It is in fact the purchase price, not of the land, but of the ground-rent yielded by it, calculated on the ordinary rate of interest." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 37"
It is in fact the purchase price, not of the land, but of the ground rent yielded by it, calculated on the ordinary rate of interest.

@art rent-to-price
Read that phrase by phrase. The price is the price of the ground rent, a title to an income. And it's calculated on the ordinary rate of interest.

@math heading="The rate falls" items="price at 4%: £200 / (1/25) = £5,000@five thousand;;years' purchase: 25@twenty five"
So it moves when that rate moves. Let interest fall to four percent, and the same two hundred is the income of five thousand pounds. The land's price rises to twenty five years' purchase, and nothing on the farm has changed.

@art price-seesaw
And the explanation runs one way: rent first, price after. Rent cannot be explained from its own capitalisation. Since the rate of interest tends to fall over time, Marx adds, the price of land tends to rise even when rents don't.

@quote text="a category which is on its face irrational, just as the price of labor is, since the earth is not the product of labor and therefore has no value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 37"
That's why Marx calls the price of land a category which is on its face irrational, just as the price of labor is, since the earth is not the product of labor and therefore has no value. Yet a real relation in production hides behind it.

@chapter Rent as interest?
@art slave-and-deed
Two older views say rent simply is interest. Henry Carey argued it's interest on all the labour ever spent improving the soil. Marx replies that rent can exist with no capital sunk in the land, and a purchase only transfers a title. If paying for land justified rent, paying for a slave would justify slavery.

@chapter Rent from wages
@art irish-cottage
Whatever a tenant pays for leave to farm fixes the price of land. So lease money can contain a deduction from the farmer's normal profit, or from his labourers' wages, and still be capitalised as if it were rent. Marx's examples are the Irish smallholder, whose rent ate into his own wages, and English labourers whose depressed wages swelled rents during the French wars.

@words text="Marginal productivity" sub="land as one more factor"
The usual objection isn't to the arithmetic, which every school accepts. It's to the word irrational. On the marginal productivity account, land is a factor of production like any other, and its price values a scarce input. The course follows Marx's definitions without deciding.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Rent is a return on the land's value@The first|The whole lease payment is rent@The second|A purchase justifies the rent@And the third"
The first slip is thinking rent is the return on the land's value. It's the other way round. The second is counting the whole lease payment as rent. And the third is thinking a purchase justifies the rent.
