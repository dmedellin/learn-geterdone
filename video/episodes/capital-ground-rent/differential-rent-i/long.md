@chapter Ricardo's idea
@title text="Differential Rent I" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 39" art=thumb
David Ricardo was a London stockbroker who made a fortune in the Napoleonic wars, then wrote the most rigorous economics of his age. In eighteen fifteen, he and three others published the same idea within weeks.

@art descent-to-worse
As population grows, farming is pushed onto poorer land. The price of corn must rise until the poorest land in use pays its costs and the ordinary profit. Every better field then earns more than it needs, and its owner takes that as rent. Marx takes over the mechanism, and calls Ricardo quite right.

@chapter Four soils
@art four-fields
Here are Marx's soils, A, B, C and D. Each takes fifty shillings of capital, with an average profit of twenty percent, so sixty shillings of cost and profit per field. A yields one quarter of wheat, B two, C three, and D four.

@math heading="Marx's Table I" items="price: 60 / 1 = 60s a quarter (A)@sets the price;;B: 2 · 60 − 60 = 60@B's two;;C: 3 · 60 − 60 = 120@C's;;D: 4 · 60 − 60 = 180@D's;;total: 0 + 60 + 120 + 180 = 360s@the total"
A is the worst soil the market needs, so it sets the price: sixty shillings a quarter for all the wheat. B's two quarters fetch a hundred and twenty against its sixty: a rent of sixty. C's is a hundred and twenty. D's, a hundred and eighty. And the total is three hundred and sixty shillings.

@words text="360s = 6 quarters at 60s"
The three hundred and sixty shillings are six quarters at sixty: B's one extra quarter over A, C's two, and D's three.

@chapter Which came first?
@art two-histories
The table hides its history, and Marx tells it two ways. Suppose D was farmed first, when wheat was cheap. As demand grew, the price rose until C could be farmed, then B, then A, and at each step D's rent grew. Or suppose A came first, and settlers found the better soils later. Then the same table forms, with the price ending where it began.

@quote text="This does away with the primitive misconception of differential rent still found among men like West, Malthus, Ricardo, to the effect that it necessarily requires a progress toward worse and worse soil, or an ever decreasing productivity of agriculture." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 39"
That's Marx's quarrel with Ricardo. This does away with the primitive misconception of differential rent still found among men like West, Malthus, Ricardo, to the effect that it necessarily requires a progress toward worse and worse soil, or an ever decreasing productivity of agriculture.

@chapter Better land, lower price
@art clover-field
Replace the worst soil with one yielding one and a third quarters for the same sixty, or improve A with clover.

@math heading="A better worst soil: 1⅓ quarters" items="price: 60 / 1⅓ = 45s@forty five;;rents: B 30s · C 75s · D 120s@thirty"
The price falls from sixty shillings to forty five, by twenty five percent. With B, C and D unchanged, the rents fall to thirty, seventy five and a hundred and twenty.

@art all-improve
Now improve every soil, but the better ones more. That's Marx's Table Three: A yields two quarters, B four, C seven, and D ten.

@math heading="Marx's Table III" items="price: 60 / 2 = 30s@half;;B 60s · C 150s · D 240s@a hundred and fifty;;total: 450s@four hundred and fifty"
The price falls to thirty shillings, half what it was. And yet total rent rises. B's sixty is unchanged, C's goes to a hundred and fifty, and D's to two hundred and forty. The total is four hundred and fifty shillings.

@words text="Rent needs unequal soils, not worse ones"
So rent needs unequal soils, not worsening ones. It can grow as farming moves to better land, as the worst land improves, as agriculture gets more productive. Ricardo's growing country, with the next acre worse, is one case among several.

@chapter A false social value
@art false-value
One more consequence. The ten quarters of Table One cost two hundred and forty shillings to produce: sixty on each of four fields. They sell for six hundred, at the worst soil's price. Marx calls this a false social value. A planned society, he says, wouldn't pay two and a half times the labour the crop contains. Economists reply that the marginal price rations scarce good land.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Rent needs a move to ever worse land@The first|The worst soil pays rent too@The second|Rent per acre confused with total rent@And the third"
The first slip is thinking rent arises because farming must move to ever worse land. The second is thinking the worst soil's farmer pays rent too. Its rent is zero. And the third is confusing rent per acre with total rent.
