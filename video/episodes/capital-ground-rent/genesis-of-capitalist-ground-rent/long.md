@chapter The puzzle
@title text="Genesis of Capitalist Ground Rent" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 47, §2" art=thumb
For the economists of Marx's day, the difficulty was never that farming yields a surplus. It was explaining the excess of agricultural profit over the average profit.

@art physiocrats
Earlier writers didn't have that problem. For Petty and Cantillon, rent was the normal form of surplus, and profit wasn't yet a category of its own. For the physiocrats, the French economists around Quesnay, agriculture was the only productive labour, and rent the only surplus. To see why, Marx goes back to rent before capital.

@chapter Labour rent
@art split-week
In labour rent, the producer has his own holding, plough and cattle. He works part of the week on it, and the rest on the lord's estate, without pay. There's nothing to analyse. Rent and surplus value are identical, and the line between them is drawn on the ground and in the calendar.

@quote text="the identity of surplus-value with unpaid labor of others does not need to be demonstrated by any analysis in this case, because it still exists in its visible, palpable form" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 47, §2"
In Marx's words: the identity of surplus value with unpaid labor of others does not need to be demonstrated by any analysis in this case, because it still exists in its visible, palpable form.

@math heading="A six-day week" items="2 days for the lord, 4 his own: 2/4 = 50%@two days;;3 and 3: 3/3 = 100%@three and three;;4 and 2: 4/2 = 200%@four and two"
Marx's own example is forced labour that originally amounted to two days a week. In a six day week, that's two days of surplus labour against four of necessary labour: a rate of fifty percent. Three and three would be a hundred percent, and four and two, two hundred.

@art serf-improves
Because the lord's two days are fixed by custom, the days a peasant works for himself are his to make more productive. Better methods, new wants and a market for his surplus spur him on. So labour rent leaves room for a certain economic development on the serf's side.

@chapter Rent in kind, then money
@art produce-share
Rent in kind changes the form, not the substance. The producer now works his whole time on his own holding, and hands over a share of the product. His labour for himself and for the landlord are no longer separated by time and space.

@math heading="The same rate in kind" items="rate: 2 / 4 = 1/2 = 50%@fifty percent;;share of the week to the lord: 2 / 6 = 1/3@a third;;rent in kind at the same rate: 1/3 of the product@a third of the product"
If two days in six went to the lord, that's still fifty percent. The lord's share of the week is a third. So a rent in kind of a third of the product keeps the same rate.

@art money-rent
Money rent changes it again. He pays the price of the share, so he must sell, and his economy is drawn into the market. Some tributary peasants buy themselves free. Others hire day labourers and grow into capitalist tenants. And town money leases land to farm for profit.

@chapter The capitalist tenant
@words text="Rent: a surplus over profit"
Once the capitalist tenant stands between landlord and labourer, he extracts the surplus as profit. Rent becomes a mere surplus of that surplus labour over the part the capitalist takes as profit. Profit, instead of rent, has now become the normal form of surplus value.

@art serf-and-worker
That's why Marx compares them. A wage worker in Volume One who works twelve hours, six of them replacing his wage, gives the same hundred percent as a peasant on three days and three. The difference is that the peasant can count his days on the lord's field. In the factory, nothing marks which hours are which.

@words text="Clark, 1899: no hour is unpaid"
Most economists since reject that accounting. On John Bates Clark's marginal productivity theory of eighteen ninety nine, a competitive wage pays what the last hour adds, so no hour is unpaid. What the chapter supplies is the definition: labour beyond what replaces the value of labour power counts as unpaid.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The wage-worker gives no unpaid labour@The first|Rent in kind or money is a smaller exploitation@The second|Pre-capitalist rent as a surplus over profit@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the wage worker, unlike the serf, gives no unpaid labour, on Marx's definitions. The second is thinking rent in kind or money rent is a smaller exploitation than labour rent. It's the same substance. And the third is reading pre capitalist rent as a surplus over profit. It was the whole surplus.
