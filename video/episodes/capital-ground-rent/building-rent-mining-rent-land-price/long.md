@chapter The edge of town
@title text="Building Rent, Mining Rent, the Price of Land" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 46" art=thumb
If you've watched a field at the edge of a town become a street of houses, you've watched this chapter. The field's owner did nothing, and the plot is suddenly worth many times what it was.

@art town-reaches-field
A building plot's advantage is its location, and the town confers it. Location, Marx says, has an overwhelming influence on this kind of rent, and the owner is palpably passive: he exploits social development, contributes nothing, and risks nothing.

@math heading="A plot the town reaches" items="before: £50 / (1/20) = £1,000@a thousand;;after: £150 / (1/20) = £3,000@three thousand;;after, at 4%: £150 / (1/25) = £3,750@four percent"
In the lesson's illustration, a plot rents for fifty pounds a year at the edge of town. At five percent, it's priced at a thousand. When the streets reach it and its rent is a hundred and fifty, its price is three thousand. If the rate of interest then fell to four percent, it would be three thousand seven hundred and fifty.

@words text="Not a brick on it is the landowner's"
The first rise came from the rent, which the town created. The last came from the rate of interest alone. And not a brick on the plot is the landowner's.

@chapter A London builder
@art builder-houses
Marx's witness is a London builder, Edward Capps. A builder, he said, makes little out of the houses. He takes land at three hundred pounds a year, builds the right class of houses, and may make four or five hundred a year out of it. His profit lies in the increased ground rent.

@math heading="Capps's ground" items="£300 at 5%: £6,000@six thousand;;£400 at 5%: £8,000@eight thousand"
Take the lower figure. At five percent, the ground goes from six thousand pounds to eight thousand. And at the end of the usual ninety nine year lease, the land, the buildings and the raised rent all go back to the original landowner.

@chapter Mines
@art scottish-mine
Mining rent, Marx says, is determined the same way as agricultural rent: the best mines pay differential rent against the worst. His worst mine comes from Adam Smith. Some Scottish coal mines yield only enough to replace the capital with ordinary profit, so only their owners work them.

@quote text="The landowner does not permit anybody to work them without the payment of rent, but no one can pay any rent for them." cite="Adam Smith, in Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 46"
The landowner does not permit anybody to work them without the payment of rent, but no one can pay any rent for them. So such a mine has no price. A price of land is capitalised rent. Where there's no rent, there's nothing to capitalise.

@chapter Monopoly price
@art vineyard
A vineyard whose wine can be made only in small quantities sells at a price set by the wealth and appetite of rich wine drinkers, and the surplus becomes the owner's rent. Here, then, the monopoly price creates the rent. Building rent, Marx adds, often rests on monopoly price, and poverty is a more lucrative source of house rent than the mines of Potosi ever were for Spain.

@chapter Why land gets dearer
@list heading="A price of land can rise because" items="the rate of interest falls@the rate of interest|interest on sunk capital rises@sunk in the land|the rent rises@because the rent"
Marx sorts why the price of land can rise. Because the rate of interest falls. Because interest on capital sunk in the land rises. Or because the rent rises, and the rent can rise with the price of corn rising, steady, or even falling.

@math heading="Marx's last case" items="before: 1 quarter at 60s = 60s@sixty shillings;;after: 2 quarters at 40s = 80s@forty"
An acre that yielded one quarter at sixty shillings now yields two at forty. Its product is worth eighty, a third more, though each quarter is a third cheaper. So it will not do to conclude that a rise in land price means a rise in rent.

@chapter Good fathers of families
@art globe-hand-down
The chapter's most remembered sentences aren't arithmetic. A title to land is transferred by sale, never created by it. Even a whole society, or all societies together, are not the owners of the globe.

@quote text="They are only its possessors, its users, and they have to hand it down to the coming generations in an improved condition, like good fathers of families." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 46"
They are only its possessors, its users, and they have to hand it down to the coming generations in an improved condition, like good fathers of families.

@art single-tax
Henry George's Progress and Poverty, in eighteen seventy nine, read far more widely than Capital in its day, held that rent created by the community should be taken by a single tax. Economists still argue about land value taxes on his terms. This chapter's point is narrower: a site's rent is fixed by the development around it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Land prices reflect the owner's improvements@The first|Dearer land means higher rent@The second|All site and mine rent is monopoly price@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a rising land price reflects improvements by the owner. The second is thinking a rise in land prices means a rise in rent. And the third is thinking all rent on sites and mines is monopoly price. Mines follow the same laws as farms.
