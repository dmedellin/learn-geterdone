@chapter The worst soil drops out
@title text="Differential Rent II at a Falling Price of Production" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 42" art=thumb
Start from the last lesson's Table Two. Every soil has two equal doses: A yields one and one, B two and two, C three and three, D four and four. Twenty quarters at sixty shillings, and a rent of seven hundred and twenty.

@art a-drops-out
Now suppose the market wants only eighteen quarters. A's two aren't needed. Its capital is withdrawn, and the least productive dose still in use is one of B's: sixty shillings for two quarters, or thirty a quarter. B now pays no rent, as A did before.

@math heading="Marx's Table IV" items="price: 60 / 2 = 30s (B)@thirty shillings a quarter;;rents: A 0 · B 0 · C 60s · D 120s@C and D;;total: 180s, down from 720s@a quarter of"
So the price is thirty shillings a quarter. C and D pay their extra quarters at thirty: sixty and a hundred and twenty. The total is a hundred and eighty, a quarter of what it was. One small slip in the translation: the text names D as the new regulator. It's B. D's own price of production is only fifteen shillings.

@chapter Add capital to the best soil
@art more-on-d
The price halved and rent fell by three quarters, but only because nothing was added to make up for A. Now Marx lays more capital on the best soils, at the same productivity per dose. A third dose on D lifts the total to two hundred and forty.

@math heading="Marx's Table IV d" items="price: 60 / 2 = 30 shillings (B)@thirty shillings;;C: 6 · 30 − 2 · 60 = 60@C's;;D: 20 · 30 − 5 · 60 = 300@five doses;;total: 0 + 60 + 300 = 360 shillings@three hundred and sixty;;in grain: 360 / 30 = 12 quarters@twelve quarters"
Give D five doses, twenty quarters. The price is still thirty shillings. C's rent is sixty, D's three hundred, and the total is three hundred and sixty shillings. In grain, that's twelve quarters.

@quote text="We find that the total money rental has remained the same, namely 18 pounds sterling, while the price of production has fallen by one-half, from 60 shillings to 30 shillings per quarter" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 42"
That's Table One's money rent again, at half the price and twice the grain. We find that the total money rental has remained the same, namely eighteen pounds sterling, while the price of production has fallen by one half, from sixty shillings to thirty shillings per quarter.

@words text="D with 44 quarters: 720s at 30s"
And Marx goes on. If D took eleven doses, producing forty four quarters, the total rent would reach Table Two's seven hundred and twenty shillings again, at thirty shillings a quarter. Half the price, the same money rent.

@chapter Why it works
@art surplus-quarters
Each dose on D yields four quarters where B's yields two, so each makes two surplus quarters, worth sixty shillings at the new price. Halve the price, and you need twice the surplus quarters to keep the money rent. In Marx's two clauses: the price of grain falls by fifty percent, the surplus product increases by a hundred percent.

@art capital-placement
And the more of the extra capital goes to the best soil, the less it takes. Table Four c reaches the same three hundred and sixty by putting more on C, and needs five hundred and fifty shillings of capital, where Table Four d needs four hundred and fifty.

@chapter Grain or money?
@art grain-vs-coin
Marx is ironic about counting rent in grain, a habit merely due to tradition. One might as well show that a manufacturer buys more of his own yarn with five pounds of profit than he used to with ten. The tables keep both counts. Money is what the lease is written in.

@chapter The prairies arrive
@art prairie-steamship
Engels, editing after Marx's death, wrote a passage that reads like this case come true. American prairie, Argentine pampas, and Russian and Indian grain reached Europe by steamship and railway. Part of Europe's soil fell definitely out of the competition for raising grain, and rents fell everywhere: a falling price with nothing added on the better land.

@words text="A possibility, not a verdict"
Marx admits his yields are chosen to make the sums easy, and Engels complains they lead to utterly forced results. What the tables establish is a possibility: the same falling price is compatible with rent falling, holding or rising. Which happened depends on where the capital went.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A falling price must lower total rent@The first|The text's D as the new regulator@The second|The dropped soil is lost to rent for good@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a falling price must lower total rent. The second is reading the translation's D as the new regulator. And the third is thinking the dropped soil is lost to rent for good. A rising price can bring it back.
