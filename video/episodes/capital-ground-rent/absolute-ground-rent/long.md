@chapter A landlord's question
@title text="Absolute Ground Rent" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 45" art=thumb
Ricardo's theory says the worst land in use pays no rent. Marx asks a landlord's question: why would anyone lend land for nothing?

@art closed-gate
If the price of corn just covers a tenant's capital and the average profit on the worst soil, will the landlord rent such lands for nothing? By no means. The market price must rise a little above the price of production before the land is let. So the worst soil pays a rent. The question is what it's a share of.

@chapter Not a tax on bread
@art value-above-price
The obvious answer is a monopoly price, a tax on bread. Marx says it needn't be. A capital that employs more labour per pound than the average produces more surplus value than the average profit it receives. Its value is above its price of production. If farming is such a capital, corn can sell above its price of production, and still at or below its value.

@chapter Marx's two capitals
@art two-compositions
Here are Marx's figures. Industry's capital is eighty five constant and fifteen variable. Agriculture's is seventy five and twenty five, more labour per pound.

@math heading="Industry and agriculture, s/v = 100%" items="industry's value: 85 + 15 + 15 = 115@a hundred and fifteen;;agriculture's value: 75 + 25 + 25 = 125@a hundred and twenty five;;average profit: (15 + 25) / 200 = 20%@twenty percent;;agriculture's price: 100 + 20 = 120@a hundred and twenty;;absolute rent: at most 125 − 120 = 5@at most five"
With surplus value at a hundred percent, industry's product is worth a hundred and fifteen, agriculture's a hundred and twenty five. Equalise them, and forty of surplus value is twenty percent on two hundred. Agriculture's price of production is a hundred and twenty. So absolute rent is at most five.

@art barrier
Agriculture's product would sell five below its value, and industry's five above. Landed property, by refusing to let land below a rent, holds farming's price up toward its value. The gap is the room for absolute rent.

@quote text="Private property in land prevents such an equalization among capitals invested in the soil" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 45"
In industry, surplus value is levelled into an average profit. Private property in land prevents such an equalization among capitals invested in the soil, and catches a portion of the surplus value that would otherwise help form the average rate.

@chapter Up to five
@words text="Market bears 122 → rent up to 2"
Up to five, because how much becomes rent depends wholly on supply and demand. If the most the market will pay is a hundred and twenty two, absolute rent is up to two.

@art monopoly-price
Either way, Marx still calls it a monopoly price, not because the price exceeds the value, but because it's equal to the value, or between the value and the price of production. And here the causal order flips. In differential rent, price causes rent. Here, the rent is the cause of the increase in the price.

@chapter It hangs on one assumption
@math heading="Agriculture at industry's composition" items="both: 85c + 15v → value 115@eighty five;;price of production: 115@Value and price;;absolute rent: up to 0@disappears"
The whole construction hangs on one assumption, and Marx says so. Give agriculture industry's eighty five and fifteen. Value and price of production are both a hundred and fifteen. And absolute rent, in this sense, disappears.

@words text="“a question which can be decided only by statistics”"
Whether farming's composition really is lower than average is a question which can be decided only by statistics. And Marx adds that, with the progress of civilization, it may gradually become the same as the social average.

@chapter Colonies and uncultivated land
@art colony-vs-owned
One more criticism of Ricardo. He set out to study the effect of owning land, then took his examples from colonies, where land isn't yet appropriated and anyone may farm new ground. Where land is owned, the owner's power to withhold it is the point. In every civilised country, Marx notes, a considerable portion of land always remains uncultivated.

@words text="The most disputed chapter of the Part"
Economists after Marx mostly hold that marginal land pays no rent, or only what its best other use would pay. Others question the construction from inside Marx's terms, since it reads values and prices with inputs at value, the assumption the transformation debate disputes.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A monopoly price set at will@The first|Absolute rent contradicts differential rent@The second|The lower composition is established@And the third"
The first slip is thinking absolute rent is a monopoly price the landlord can set at will. It's capped by value and by the market. The second is thinking absolute rent contradicts differential rent. Marx calls them the only normal ones, side by side. And the third is treating the lower composition as established.
