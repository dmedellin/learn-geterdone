@chapter The rule you were taught
@title text="When the Last Letter Doubles" sub="Tense Tables" art=thumb
Here's a rule you've probably met. A verb that ends with a consonant, a vowel and then another consonant doubles its last letter before ing or e-d. Stop becomes stopping. Plan becomes planned.

@art cvc-blocks
Now let's test it. In the word list this course uses, there are two hundred and seventeen verbs that end that way. Those are the only verbs the doubling rule can touch.

@chapter No better than a guess
@math heading="217 verbs" items="double every one  →  107 right  ·  49.3%@double all of them;;double none  →  110 right  ·  50.7%@double none of them"
If you double all of them, you're right one hundred and seven times. That's forty-nine point three percent. If you double none of them, you're right one hundred and ten times, fifty point seven percent.

@art coin-balance
For any use you could put them to, those two numbers are the same. The rule, as it's usually given, tells you nothing about the word in front of you. But the rule isn't wrong. It's half of something that works.

@chapter The missing half
@words text="adMIT · TRAvel" sub="which part do you say harder?"
Say admit out loud. Then say travel. In admit, you push harder on the second part. In travel, you push harder on the first. English spelling cares about that difference here, and only here.

@art strong-part
So here's the full rule. Double the last letter when the word ends consonant, vowel, consonant, and the last part is the part you say harder. That's the strong part.

@math heading="Same 217 verbs" items="double if the last part is strong  →  199 right  ·  91.7%@one hundred and ninety-nine"
Score that on the same two hundred and seventeen verbs, and it's right one hundred and ninety-nine times. That's ninety-one point seven percent. One extra question, and the rule goes from a guess to something you can use.

@words text="the only rule that needs the sound" sub="every other rule works on letters alone"
Every other rule in this course works on letters alone. You could use them with the sound turned off. This one needs you to know how the word is said, and that's why learners get it wrong more often than any other.

@chapter Four questions
@list heading="Step by step" items="Consonant, vowel, consonant? (w, x, y count as no)@end consonant, vowel|Say it out loud@Say the word|Strong part last → double@If the strong part|Check the short list@check the short list"
First, does the word end consonant, vowel, consonant? A last w, x or y counts as no. Second, say the word out loud. Third, if the strong part is the last one, double the letter. Fourth, check the short list of words that break it.

@art fork-of-two
If the answer to the first question is no, stop there. The rule has nothing to say, and you simply add the ending. If it's yes, the strong part decides: two letters, or one.

@words text="admit → admitting · visit → visiting" sub="strong part last · strong part first"
Admit and visit both end in t, and both have two parts. In admit, the strong part is last, so it doubles: admitting. In visit, it's first, so it doesn't: visiting.

@chapter What's left over
@math heading="18 still wrong" items="cancel, channel, counsel, label, level, model, panel, rival, signal, total, travel@Eleven of them;;benefit, bus, focus, format, input, output, program@That leaves seven"
Eighteen verbs are still wrong. Eleven of them end in l, like cancel, travel and model. That leaves seven: benefit, bus, focus, format, input, output and program.

@art two-roads
The l words aren't broken. British writers double a final l whatever the strong part is, and American writers don't. Travelling and traveling are both right. The rule writes the American form, so it's marked wrong here where nothing is wrong.

@words text="4 true exceptions" sub="format · input · output · bus"
Of the seven, benefit, focus and program are the same story, with both spellings in the list. Format, input and output double against the rule. And bus doesn't double when the rule says it should. Four words. Learn them in a minute.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Using the short rule you were taught@The first|Scoring a rule on words it can't touch@The second|Treating travelling as a mistake@the third"
The first slip is using the short rule you were taught. It's right half the time. The second is scoring a rule on words it can't touch. And the third is treating a spelling difference as a mistake. Travelling isn't wrong. It's British.
