@chapter A rule needs a number
@title text="Scoring a Rule on 1,210 Real Verbs" sub="Tense Tables" art=thumb
Anyone can give you a rule for English. The real question is how often it's right. A rule with no number beside it is only an opinion.

@art long-list
So here the rules are counted. They're run over twelve hundred and ten regular verbs, taken from a list of the twenty-eight hundred most common English words. For each verb, the rule writes a form, and the form is checked against the forms the list records.

@chapter The scores
@math heading="Right out of 1,210" items="-s  ·  1209  ·  99.92%@for s;;-ing  ·  1202  ·  99.34%@for ing;;-ed  ·  1201  ·  99.26%@for the past"
A match is a hit, and anything else is a miss. The rule for s is right twelve hundred and nine times. That's ninety-nine point nine two percent. The rule for ing is right twelve hundred and two times. And the rule for the past, twelve hundred and one times.

@art hit-meter
Every rule is right more than ninety-nine times in a hundred. But the past rule still misses nine verbs. And those nine words are the interesting part.

@chapter Reading the misses
@list heading="Nine misses, five kinds" items="British spelling: initial, counsel@initial and counsel|A -k no rule knows: panic, traffic@panic and traffic|Doubles against the strong part: format, input, output@format, input and output|Both spellings in use: bus@bus|No consonant before the vowel: up@And up"
The nine misses sort into five kinds. Initial and counsel have a British spelling, and the list has only that one. Panic and traffic take a k: panicking. Format, input and output double even though the strong part comes first. For bus, both spellings are in use. And up has no consonant before its vowel, so the rule never doubles it.

@art five-bins
Each kind has a reason you can say out loud. That's what a hit rate should come with. A kind with one word is a word to learn. A kind with ten words is a new rule.

@words text="stomach → stomachs, not stomaches" sub="-ch that does not hiss"
The s rule misses just one word: stomach. It ends in c h, but it doesn't hiss, so the rule writes the wrong form. The rule reads letters, and it can't hear. And the ing rule misses the same words as the past rule, without counsel. That's eight.

@chapter Clean the list first
@art cleaning-list
There's a catch. The word list was made by a machine, and it had spellings nobody writes, like offerring with two r's, and comed and maked. Scored against that list, a right rule looks wrong, and a wrong rule looks right.

@words text="146 words left out" sub="90 of them irregular verbs"
So the list was cleaned with one test. A spelling stays if a dictionary has it, or if it's the British spelling of one the dictionary has. In all, one hundred and forty-six words were left out. Ninety of them are irregular verbs.

@chapter A rule that sounded right
@math heading="The -s rule" items="every -o takes -es  ·  1207  ·  99.75%@every o took;;-f becomes -ves  ·  1205  ·  99.59%@f becomes v-e-s"
When the s rule first ran, every o took e-s, and that broke radio and video. The score was twelve hundred and seven. The fix: o takes e-s only after a consonant, as in goes. Another rule looked right too: f becomes v-e-s. With it, the score fell to twelve hundred and five.

@art falling-bar
For nouns, that rule is often true: one leaf, two leaves. But brief, golf, proof and roof are verbs, and verbs simply take s. A rule can sound right and still cost you. The only way to know is to count.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Believing a rule because it sounds right@The first|Scoring against a list that is wrong@The second|Reading a high score as a finished rule@The third"
The first slip is believing a rule because it sounds right. The ear missed the f rule. The count caught it. The second is scoring a rule against a list that is wrong. And the third is reading a high score as a finished rule. The score says how much is left. The words say what it is.

@chapter Try it
@art rule-plus-list
Here's the saving. A rule plus a short list of misses beats a long list of everything. The three lists of misses are short enough to learn directly. Your turn: take the past rule, read its nine misses, and name the kind each one belongs to.
