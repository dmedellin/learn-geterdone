@chapter One price
@title text="Market Value and Surplus Profit" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 10" art=thumb
You know this from any market stall. Two growers sell the same apples at the same price, though one grows them on better land with newer equipment.

@art apple-stall
The price doesn't ask how each was grown. This chapter asks what fixes that one price, and how competition does what the last chapter assumed.

@quote text="We shall have to regard the market-value on one side as the average value of the commodities produced in a certain sphere, and on the other side as the individual value of commodities produced under the average conditions of their respective sphere of production and constituting the bulk of the products of that sphere." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 10"
Two answers, which coincide in the ordinary case: the average value of the trade's goods, and the value of goods made under the conditions most of them are made under.

@chapter Bulk at average
@art three-groups
Picture a trade's output as groups made under different conditions: some better than average, some average, some worse. Each group's own value is the hours its pieces cost. The market value is one line drawn across them all, and every piece sells at it.

@art bulk-average
Marx's first case has the bulk made under average conditions, with small balancing groups above and below.

@math heading="10,000 pieces" items="1,000 at 3h + 8,000 at 4h + 1,000 at 5h@eight thousand;;market value = 40,000 / 10,000 = 4h@four hours;;best +1h a piece;  worst −1h@an hour"
A thousand pieces at three hours, eight thousand at four, a thousand at five. The market value is four hours. The best group gains an hour a piece, and the worst loses an hour.

@words text="Surplus profit = market value − own value"
That gain is surplus profit. The best group doesn't charge more. It sells at the same price as everyone, but its own value is lower.

@chapter Bulk at the worst
@quote text="In that case the mass produced under the least favorable conditions determines the market-value, or social value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 10"
That's the second case: the worst placed group makes a large share of the output.

@art bulk-worst
Strictly speaking, Marx says, the market value is then the total value divided by the quantity, which lies above the average group's and below the worst group's.

@math heading="Worst dominates" items="1,000 at 3h + 3,000 at 4h + 6,000 at 5h@six thousand;;market value = 45,000 / 10,000 = 9/2 h@nine halves;;gains:  +3/2,  +1/2,  −1/2@half an hour"
A thousand at three hours, three thousand at four, six thousand at five. The market value is nine halves of an hour. Now even the middle group gains half an hour a piece.

@words text="Best dominates: 7/2 h"
The third case reverses it. Six thousand at three hours, three thousand at four, a thousand at five, and the market value falls to seven halves, below the middle group's own. Nothing about demand changed; only the mix of conditions did.

@chapter Where demand comes in
@quote text="If the demand exceeds the supply but slightly, then the individual value of the unfavorably produced commodities regulates the market-price." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 10"
Marx gives demand a definite role. Short supply lets the worst conditions regulate. A glut lets the best conditions regulate. So demand decides which group sets the price, and how far market price departs from value.

@words text="When they balance, they cease to explain anything"
What it doesn't decide is the value prices move around. When supply and demand balance, Marx argues, they cease to explain anything. Two equal forces pulling opposite ways leave the result to be explained by something else.

@art scissors
Marshall's reply deserves full strength. Asking whether utility or cost governs value, he said, is like asking which blade of the scissors cuts. His curves are drawn at every price, not just at balance. Marx's argument works against a theory that explains price at balance only. Whether it reaches Marshall's is disputed.

@chapter Between trades
@art capital-moves
The chapter's second half returns to the whole economy. Capital withdraws from spheres with low rates of profit and invades others with higher. That changes supply in each until rates equalise, and values become prices of production. So every capitalist has a direct interest in the exploitation of the whole working class.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Supply and demand determine value@The first|Surplus profit is a mark-up@The second|Market value is always the middle@And the third"
The first slip is thinking supply and demand determine value. In every preset the same ten thousand pieces sell, and the market value moves with the mix. The second is reading surplus profit as cheating or a mark-up. And the third is assuming the market value is always the middle group's value.

@words text="2,000 at 2h · 2,000 at 4h"
Your turn. A trade sells two thousand pieces at two hours each and two thousand at four hours. What's the market value of a piece? And what does each group gain or lose on it?
