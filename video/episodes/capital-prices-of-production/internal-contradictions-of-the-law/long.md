@chapter Making and selling
@title text="The Internal Contradictions of the Law" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 15" art=thumb
This chapter follows the falling rate of profit into crisis.

@art make-and-sell
It starts by drawing a line you can see in any recession. Producing surplus value is the first act. Selling the goods that contain it is the second. A firm can exploit its workers fully and still fail to realise the profit, if the goods don't sell.

@words text="Limited by consumption, held down by distribution"
The first act is limited by productive power. The second is limited by the proportions between trades, and by consumption, which is held down by the very distribution that makes the profit. The falling rate, Marx says, promotes overproduction, speculation, crises, surplus capital and surplus population.

@chapter The real barrier
@quote text="The real barrier of capitalist production is capital itself." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 15"
Capital and its self expansion are the starting and closing point. Production is undertaken when it promises profit at the going rate, and halted when it doesn't, whatever the need for the goods.

@art capital-barrier
The means, an unlimited growth of productive power, comes into conflict with the limited end, the growth of the existing capital's value.

@words text="It is not a fact that too much wealth is produced"
Marx isn't saying too much is produced for people's wants. He says the opposite. What's produced in excess is wealth in the form of capital: means of production that can't be employed at a sufficient rate of profit.

@chapter Absolute over-accumulation
@art swollen-capital
Then a thought experiment. Suppose capital has grown so far relative to the working population that neither the working day nor the rate of exploitation can be raised. With labour scarce, wages rise. Then the increased capital would produce no more profit than before, or less. That's absolute over accumulation.

@quote text="If a total capital of 1,000 yielded a profit of 100, and after its increase to 1,500 still yielded 100, then 1,000 in the second case would yield only 66 2/3." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 15"
Those are Marx's figures.

@math heading="The same 100 of profit" items="before:  100 / 1,000 = 10%@ten per cent;;after the addition:  100 / 1,500 = 1/15@one fifteenth"
Before, a hundred on a thousand is ten per cent. After the capital grows to fifteen hundred with no more profit, a hundred on fifteen hundred is one fifteenth, six and two thirds per cent.

@art idle-plant
In reality, Marx adds, part of the capital would lie fallow while the rest worked at a lower rate.

@chapter Hostile brothers
@art hostile-brothers
How is it resolved? Not peacefully. While profits grow, competition is a practical brotherhood of the capitalist class. When losses have to be shared, it becomes a fight of hostile brothers, each trying to push the idle capital onto someone else.

@art written-down
But under all circumstances, Marx says, the equilibrium is restored by making capital unproductive or destroying it. Factories close, stocks are sold at a loss, fixed capital is written down.

@math heading="Write off a third" items="1,500 · 2/3 = 1,000@a third;;100 / 1,000 = 10%@ten per cent again"
Write off a third of the fifteen hundred, and the same hundred of profit stands on a thousand: ten per cent again.

@words text="Momentary and forcible solutions"
So crises are momentary and forcible solutions of the existing contradictions. Unemployment pushes wages down, depreciated plant raises the rate, and the cycle starts again on a larger scale.

@chapter The objections
@words text="Underconsumption · Okishio · Keynes"
The objections come from several sides. Many Marxists trace crises not to the falling rate but to the realisation problem, from Rosa Luxemburg to Baran and Sweezy. Okishio's theorem denies technical change alone lowers the rate. And Keynes located slumps in a shortfall of investment demand that policy could correct. Whether the rate has fallen in fact is argued with data this course doesn't carry.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Crises come from a shortage of capital@The first|Too many goods for people's needs@The second|The crisis is the end of the system@And the third"
The first slip is thinking crises come from a shortage of capital. In over accumulation there's too much. The second is reading it as too many goods for people's needs. And the third is treating the crisis as the end of the system. In this chapter, the mechanism restores the rate, and the cycle runs again.
