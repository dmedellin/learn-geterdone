@chapter Two businesses
@title text="Different Compositions, Different Rates of Profit" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 8" art=thumb
Think of two businesses you could put a hundred pounds into. One is a power station, almost all plant and fuel, with a handful of staff. The other is a cleaning company, almost all wages.

@art power-and-cleaning
You'd expect either to pay you about the going return, and Marx agrees that's how it works. This chapter explains why, on the theory of the first two volumes, that's a puzzle.

@chapter Wages index labour
@art pound-a-worker
The chapter holds the rate of surplus value the same everywhere, since workers move between trades. With the wage and working day fixed, one pound sterling stands for one labourer a week. At a hundred per cent, each worker paid a pound adds two pounds of new value.

@words text="v measures the living labour employed"
So the variable capital is an index of the living labour a capital sets in motion, and of the surplus labour in it.

@chapter Ninety and ten
@art ninety-ten
Take a capital of ninety c and ten v. Ten pounds of wages employ ten workers, who add twenty, of which ten is surplus value. Now ten c and ninety v. Ninety workers add a hundred and eighty, of which ninety is surplus value.

@math heading="£100 each · s′ = 100%" items="90c + 10v:  s = 10,  p′ = 10%@ten per cent;;10c + 90v:  s = 90,  p′ = 90%@ninety per cent"
At value, the first earns ten per cent, and the second ninety per cent, on the same hundred pounds.

@quote text="If a capital, consisting of percentages of 90 c + 10 v, produced as much surplus-value, or profit, with the same degree of exploitation, as a capital consisting of percentages of 10 c + 90 v, then it would be as plain as daylight that the surplus-value, and value in general, must have an entirely different source than labor, and that political economy would then be without a rational basis." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 8"
That's Marx's verdict on the alternative.

@chapter Same technique, dearer materials
@art copper-and-iron
The technique needn't differ at all. Let two trades employ the same hundred workers on the same machines, but the materials are dearer in the second, as copper is dearer than iron.

@math heading="Same workers · s = 100 each" items="200c + 100v:  p′ = 33⅓%@thirty three;;400c + 100v:  p′ = 20%@twenty"
Then one works with two hundred c and a hundred v, the other with four hundred c and a hundred v. Both make a hundred of surplus value, at thirty three and a third per cent and twenty. A difference in what the means of production cost separates the rates.

@chapter Europe and Asia
@art two-countries
Then the comparison of countries, with the chapter's one surprise. Marx's European capital is eighty four c and sixteen v, at a hundred per cent. His Asiatic capital is sixteen c and eighty four v, with the worker labouring four fifths of the day for himself and one fifth for the employer: twenty five per cent.

@math heading="Two national capitals of £100" items="Europe:  s = 100% · 16 = 16,  p′ = 16%@sixteen per cent;;Asia:  s = 25% · 84 = 21,  p′ = 21%@twenty one"
Europe makes sixteen of surplus value, sixteen per cent. Asia, twenty five per cent of eighty four, is twenty one, and twenty one per cent. The capital with a quarter of the rate of exploitation earns the higher rate of profit.

@words text="Carey and Bastiat: the opposite conclusion"
Men like Carey and Bastiat, Marx says, would come to the opposite conclusion. On their kind of view, a high return to capital signals a large share for capital. On Marx's accounting, the European worker is exploited four times as intensely, and the low rate comes from the means of production each worker sets in motion.

@chapter Only equal compositions
@art equal-shape
The one unpuzzling case is equal composition. A hundred at eighty c and twenty v, and a thousand at eight hundred and two hundred, both earn twenty per cent. Only then do profits stand in proportion to the capitals.

@words text="Irreconcilable with the actual process?"
Everything so far assumes commodities sell at their values. But in reality, setting passing differences aside, rates of profit don't differ by trade, or capital would leave the low ones. It would seem, Marx says, as though the theory of value were irreconcilable at this point with the actual process.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Higher s′ means higher p′@The first|Composition is technique@The second|The rate depends on size@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a higher rate of surplus value always means a higher rate of profit. Europe and Asia show otherwise. The second is reading a difference in composition as a difference in technique. It's measured in value. And the third is treating the rate as depending on size.

@words text="70c + 30v · s′ = 100%"
Your turn. At a hundred per cent, what's the rate of profit of seventy c and thirty v, selling at value? Then compare it with ninety c and ten v, and say why the theory seems to break here.
