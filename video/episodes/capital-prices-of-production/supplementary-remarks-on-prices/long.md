@chapter Claims for compensation
@title text="Supplementary Remarks on Prices of Production" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 12" art=thumb
Insurance makes the idea familiar. A shipowner pays a higher premium than a baker, and the freight he charges covers it.

@art ship-at-sea
Marx's example is the same. Capitals exposed to considerable danger, in shipping for instance, should be compensated by a rise in prices. Once insurance is developed, the danger is spread over everyone, and the dangerous trades pay higher premiums and recover them in their prices.

@art slow-turnover
The other standard claim is turnover. A capital turned over slowly, because production is long or markets far away, should charge the profit it loses that way.

@words text="Not denied, but read differently"
Marx doesn't deny these allowances are made, or that they belong in prices of production. What he denies is the capitalist's reading of them. Each claim, read alone, seems to create profit.

@quote text="The capitalist simply forgets, or rather he does not see, because competition does not show it to him, that all these claims for compensation mutually advanced by the capitalists in the calculation of the prices of commodities of different lines of production repeat in another way the idea that all capitalists are entitled, in proportion to the magnitude of their respective capitals, to equal shares of the common loot, the total surplus-value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 12"
The claims repeat the stock company from two lessons ago.

@chapter One pool
@art common-pool
The lab makes that a sum. Take Marx's five spheres, a hundred and ten of surplus value on five hundred of capital. With no premiums, everyone gets twenty two per cent.

@art premium-draw
Now allow sphere five a five per cent premium for danger. Its five has to come from somewhere.

@math heading="Sphere V: +5% for danger" items="premium:  5% · 100 = 5@takes five;;base rate:  (110 − 5) / 500 = 21%@twenty one;;V's price:  15 + 26 = 41@forty one;;total profit:  4 · 21 + 26 = 110@a hundred and ten"
The premium takes five of the hundred and ten. What's left, a hundred and five over five hundred, is a base rate of twenty one per cent. Sphere five gets twenty six on its hundred, so it sells at forty one. Total profit is still a hundred and ten.

@words text="V up 4 · every other sphere down 1"
Sphere five gains four over its price without the premium, and the four others lose one each. The premium moved four of surplus value. It created none.

@math heading="Sphere I: +10% for slow turnover" items="base rate:  (110 − 10) / 500 = 20%@twenty per cent;;I sells at 100 instead of 92@a hundred"
Give sphere one ten per cent for slow turnover instead. The base rate falls to twenty per cent, sphere one sells at a hundred instead of ninety two, and the totals don't move.

@chapter Price changes trace to value
@quote text="All changes in the price of production of commodities reduce themselves in the last analysis to changes in value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 12"
The chapter's first section makes the same point from the other side. A price of production changes only if the general rate changes, or the commodity's own value does. If it's the general rate, its own value may be unchanged, but some other commodity's value has changed.

@art average-anchor
The second section shows why the sphere of average composition is the anchor. Its surplus value equals the average profit, so a change in wages moves its profit and never its price. The last lesson's average sphere, unchanged at a hundred and twenty, was this rule at work.

@chapter Upside down
@art upside-down
Why doesn't the capitalist see the pool? Because competition shows him the average rate, prices moving with wages, and market prices gathering round prices of production. None of these shows labour time. Everything appears upside down in competition.

@words text="Profit as the reward for uncertainty?" sub="Frank Knight, 1921"
There's a strong objection. A modern economist would say a risk premium is a price: investors who could earn the safe rate must be paid more to hold a risky asset. Frank Knight argued profit itself rewards uncertainty no insurance covers. On that account, the premium is earned, not redistributed.

@art common-pool
What in the chapter bears on it is its accounting: any premium in one place is a deduction somewhere else. The lab computes inside that accounting, and can't say whether the total is fixed as the chapter assumes.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Each compensation adds new profit@The first|The allowances are illegitimate@The second|Every price change is its own value@And the third"
The first slip is thinking each compensation adds new profit. The second is thinking Marx calls the allowances illegitimate. He objects only to reading them as a source of profit. And the third is treating every change in a price of production as a change in its own value.
