@chapter The table
@title text="The Falling Rate of Profit: The Law as Such" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 13" art=thumb
This is the most debated law in volume three. And it starts with a very simple table.

@art growing-c
A hundred pounds of wages employ the same workers at the same rate of exploitation, a hundred per cent, in every line. They make a hundred of surplus value each time. Only the means of production behind them grow.

@math heading="v = 100 · s′ = 100%" items="50c:  100/150 = 66⅔%@fifty;;100c:  100/200 = 50%@a hundred c;;200c:  100/300 = 33⅓%@two hundred c;;300c:  100/400 = 25%@three hundred c;;400c:  100/500 = 20%@four hundred c"
With fifty c, the rate of profit is a hundred over a hundred and fifty, two thirds. With a hundred c, a half. With two hundred c, a third. With three hundred c, a quarter. And with four hundred c, a fifth.

@chapter Rising productivity
@quote text="This progressive tendency of the average rate of profit to fall is, therefore, but a peculiar expression of capitalist production for the fact that the social productivity of labor is progressively increasing." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 13"
That sentence carries the argument.

@art more-material
The same number of workers work up more and more raw material with more and more machinery, so each product holds less labour and is cheaper. If paid and unpaid hours keep their proportion, the unpaid labour shrinks relative to everything the capital advances.

@words text="A tendency, not an absolute fall"
The rate falls. But Marx adds that it doesn't show itself in an absolute form, rather as a tendency toward a progressive fall. The reasons it doesn't fall faster are the next lesson.

@chapter Rate and mass
@art rate-and-mass
A falling rate doesn't mean falling profits, and Marx spends much of the chapter on the difference.

@math heading="Two million workers become three" items="before:  4c + 2v + 2s;  p′ = 2/6 = 1/3@before;;after:  15c + 3v + 3s;  p′ = 3/18 = 1/6@after;;mass of profit:  2 → 3 million@two million to three"
His illustration, in millions: before, four c, two v, two s, a rate of a third. After, fifteen c, three v, three s, a rate of a sixth. The mass of profit goes from two million to three.

@quote text="While the mass of surplus-value has increased by one-half, the rate of profit has fallen by one-half." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 13"
The capital has trebled while the rate has halved, so the mass rises by half.

@art two-to-three
The rule behind it: to keep the mass the same when the rate halves, the capital must double. To raise it, the capital must grow faster than the rate falls. And the reverse is possible. A hundred at twenty five per cent make twenty five, while four hundred at five per cent make only twenty.

@chapter Composition, not size
@art steady-growth
A rising composition isn't the same as accumulation. A capital of eight hundred c and two hundred v doubling four times at the same composition keeps its rate at a fifth, while its mass of profit grows from two hundred to thirty two hundred. Only a change in proportions lowers the rate.

@chapter The objection
@art cheaper-goods
Now the step critics refuse. Marx holds the rate of surplus value fixed while productivity rises. But rising productivity also cheapens the workers' necessities, which on volume one's own account raises the rate of surplus value.

@words text="Robinson · Sweezy, 1942"
Joan Robinson and Paul Sweezy both pressed this. Hold s prime fixed, and real wages must be rising with productivity. Let it rise, and nothing in the table says which wins. Marx says the rate will fall, with few exceptions, in spite of a higher rate of surplus value. Whether he's right is argued to this day, and this course doesn't decide it.

@words text="Smith: competition · Ricardo: worse land"
Marx claims the law as his own discovery. Smith expected profits to fall from competition among growing capitals, Ricardo from feeding workers off worse and worse land. Marx's law needs neither. On his definitions, it follows from productivity itself.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A falling rate means falling profits@The first|Exploitation must be falling@The second|Accumulation alone lowers the rate@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a falling rate means falling profits. The second is thinking the law says workers are exploited less. In the table the rate of surplus value is a hundred per cent throughout. And the third is thinking accumulation by itself lowers the rate.
