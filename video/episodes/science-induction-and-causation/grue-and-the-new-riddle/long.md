@chapter A new word
@title text="Grue and the New Riddle" sub="Science, Induction and Causation" art=thumb
Every emerald ever examined has been green. That supports all emeralds are green, and it supports the next emerald will be green. Now let's introduce a new word, and watch that support split in two.

@words text="grue" sub="examined before t and green, or not examined before t and blue"
Fix a time t in the future. An object is grue if it's examined before t and is green, or isn't examined before t and is blue. It's a strange word. But it's perfectly well defined.

@art green-and-grue
Every emerald examined so far was examined before t, and was green. So every one of them was grue as well. All emeralds are grue has exactly as much observed support as all emeralds are green.

@math heading="An emerald examined…" items="before t:  green says green,  grue says green@before t;;after t:  green says green,  grue says blue@after t"
And the two hypotheses part company in the future. For an emerald examined before t, both say green. For the first emerald examined after t, green says green, and grue says blue.

@chapter A Bayes factor of one
@art identical-rows
Write down what each hypothesis predicts for the observations you actually have. Before t, both give a green emerald probability one. So their likelihood rows are identical, outcome for outcome.

@math heading="A hundred green emeralds" items="likelihood under green:  1¹⁰⁰ = 1@under green;;likelihood under grue:  1¹⁰⁰ = 1@under grue;;Bayes factor:  1/1 = 1@the Bayes factor;;posterior of green:  3/4, the prior@three quarters"
Put a prior of three quarters on green and a quarter on grue, and enter a hundred green emeralds. The likelihood under green is one. Under grue, also one. So the Bayes factor is one over one, which is one. And the posterior of green is three quarters, exactly its prior.

@words text="posterior ratio = prior ratio" sub="3 to 1 before, 3 to 1 after"
The prior ratio was three to one, and the posterior ratio is three to one, to the digit. A thousand emeralds do the same, because each multiplies both likelihoods by one. The data are neutral.

@art balance-level
Think of it as a balance that never tips. Each green emerald goes on both pans at once, with exactly the same weight. Pile on as many as you like. The balance stays where the prior left it. If you favoured green while the Bayes factor was one, that preference came from somewhere else, and you should say what justified it.

@chapter What would separate them
@art after-t
Only one kind of observation can change that: one where the rows disagree. Suppose the next emerald is examined after t, and it's green. Grue gave that probability zero. Green gave it one. Now the Bayes factor is infinite, and green is proved against this one rival. But until an observation like that arrives, nothing in the evidence tells the two hypotheses apart.

@chapter Goodman's riddle
@art grue-bleen
Goodman's riddle is that no formal feature of the data prefers green to grue. One reply says grue is gerrymandered, because it mentions a time. But a speaker of a language with grue and bleen as primitives would call green the gerrymandered word.

@art natural-kinds
Another reply says natural kinds are the projectible ones. That relocates the question to what makes a kind natural. Each reply is really a claim about the prior. Treating green as projectible, and grue as not, is a judgement made before the data, because the data are the same for both.

@words text="Projectibility lives in the prior."
And nothing in the calculation says that three quarters on green is the right number. Set the prior on grue at three quarters instead, and you get the mirror result.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking more green emeralds favour green@The first|Treating grue as a trick the data can sweep aside@The second|Concluding induction is hopeless@the third"
The first slip is thinking more green emeralds favour green over grue. The count is no argument at all. The second is treating grue as a trick of language the data can sweep aside. And the third is concluding that induction is hopeless. It needs a prior that treats some predicates as projectible. You can hold one, and defend it. You just can't derive it from the observations it's applied to.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Find the outcome where the rows differ"
Here's one to try. Take any two rival hypotheses. Write what each predicts for your observations, and compare the rows. If they agree everywhere, the data are idle. Then find the observation, possible in principle, where they disagree.
