@chapter A theory never tests alone
@title text="The Duhem-Quine Problem" sub="Science, Induction and Causation" art=thumb
A prediction fails. So the theory is wrong? Not so fast. A theory by itself forbids nothing you can observe. It needs help to make a prediction at all.

@art auxiliaries-stack
To get a prediction, a theory is joined to auxiliary hypotheses, assumptions other than the theory that are used to derive the prediction. And to a bridge: the assumption that links a computed quantity to what an instrument will report.

@list heading="A failed test, in five lines" items="1. h: the theory@the theory|2. a: nothing unseen disturbs the system@the auxiliary|3. b: the bridge to the instrument@the bridge|4. (h ∧ a ∧ b) → e@if the theory|5. ¬e: e did not occur@did not occur"
Write h for the theory, a for the auxiliary that nothing unseen disturbs the system, and b for the bridge. The prediction is a conditional: if the theory, the auxiliary and the bridge are all true, then e. And the observation is that e did not occur.

@chapter The whole set is refuted
@words text="1–4 entail e · 5 denies it"
Sentences one to four entail e, and sentence five denies it. So the five can't all be true. That's the whole logical content of a failed test. And the smallest subset that can't be true is all five sentences.

@words text="The refuted unit is the set."
Every member is needed for the inconsistency, so dropping any one of them makes the rest consistent. A failed prediction refutes the conjunction of everything used to derive it, and no single member by itself.

@art five-repairs
So there are five repairs, all equally consistent. Drop the theory, the auxiliary, the bridge, the conditional, or the observation. Disbelieving the observation is a repair too, though a costly one when the measurement was careful.

@chapter Duhem and Quine
@art group-and-web
Duhem made this point for physics: an experiment can condemn only a whole theoretical group, never an isolated hypothesis, because the physicist reads the instrument through the theory being tested. Quine extended it to everything we believe. Any statement can be held true come what may, if enough is adjusted elsewhere. Where they differ is whether the group's boundary can be drawn anywhere short of everything.

@chapter Uranus and Mercury
@art uranus-neptune
By the eighteen forties, the orbit of Uranus disagreed with the position computed from Newton's laws and the seven known planets. Astronomers kept Newton, and dropped the auxiliary that no further planet exists. They computed where an eighth would have to be, and Neptune was found close to that place in eighteen forty six.

@art mercury-vulcan
The same repair was tried for Mercury's orbit. A planet inside Mercury's orbit, Vulcan, was proposed. None was found. And the repair that worked dropped the theory instead, in nineteen fifteen.

@math heading="Two repairs of one failure" items="replace a by ¬a:  1 model, h true@keep the theory;;replace h by ¬h:  1 model, a true@keep the auxiliary"
Write both repairs as sets. Keep the theory, and deny the auxiliary: an eighth planet exists. One model. Keep the auxiliary, and deny the theory. Also one model. The count is the same, and nothing in the arithmetic prefers either.

@chapter What does choose
@art testable-repair
What chooses is outside the set. A repair that predicts something new and checkable, like a planet at a stated place, can be tested on its own. One that only restores the old prediction can't. That's a reason to try the first kind first, and it's a reason of method, not of logic.

@words text="Hold the auxiliaries fixed, for now"
The falsificationist can say science proceeds by a decision to hold the auxiliaries fixed for a test, so the theory is exposed. The holist can answer that no such decision is forced by the logic, and the next test can revise it. Both are right: the set is refuted, and the choice of what to blame is made outside it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a failed test refutes the theory@The first|Concluding tests prove nothing@The second|Reading inconsistency as all false@the third"
The first slip is thinking a failed test refutes the theory. It does only if the auxiliaries and the bridge are held fixed. The second is concluding that any theory can be saved, so tests prove nothing. A repair is a new claim, and it can be tested. And the third is reading an inconsistent set as saying every member is false. In each of the five repairs, four sentences are kept.

@chapter Try it
@words text="List what the derivation used"
Here's one to try. Take a test that didn't come out as predicted. List the theory, every auxiliary, and the bridge. If you can't say what the auxiliaries were, you haven't yet said what was tested. Then ask which repair makes a prediction of its own.
