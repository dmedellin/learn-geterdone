@chapter What a theory forbids
@title text="Falsification and What a Theory Forbids" sub="Science, Induction and Causation" art=thumb
A theory passes a hundred tests. Is it proven? No. A theory fails one test it said couldn't fail. Is it finished? Yes. Here's the arithmetic behind that asymmetry.

@art forbidden-outcome
Take a hypothesis H and three possible outcomes. H gives probability a half to each of the first two, and zero to the third. It forbids the third. An outcome is forbidden by a hypothesis when the hypothesis gives it probability zero. And a hypothesis is falsifiable to the extent that it forbids something observable.

@chapter One forbidden outcome
@math heading="Prior 99/100, observe o3" items="H and o3:  99/100 · 0 = 0@zero;;not H and o3:  1/100 · 1/3 = 1/300@a three hundredth;;P(H | o3) = 0 / (0 + 1/300) = 0@posterior is zero"
The rival, just not H, gives each outcome a third. Give H a prior of ninety nine hundredths, and observe the forbidden outcome. The joint probability of H and that outcome is ninety nine hundredths times zero, which is zero. For not H, it's a hundredth times a third, a three hundredth. So the posterior is zero.

@words text="Any prior × 0 = 0"
Nothing about the prior mattered. Had it been nine hundred and ninety nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety nine millionths, the result would be the same. Multiplying anything by zero gives zero. One observation, and the hypothesis is refuted for good.

@chapter No number of successes proves it
@math heading="Ten allowed observations of o1" items="prior:  99/100@ninety nine hundredths;;posterior:  5845851/5846875 ≈ 0.9998@close to one"
Now feed the same hypothesis ten observations of the first outcome, which it allows. Its posterior rises from ninety nine hundredths to about nought point nine nine nine eight. Close to one, and not equal to it, because the rival never gave that outcome probability zero.

@art refute-vs-confirm
That's the asymmetry. One refuting observation does what no number of confirming ones can. A confirmation leaves the hypothesis short of one as long as some rival allowed the data. And a later forbidden outcome would still send it to zero.

@chapter A theory that forbids nothing
@art no-zeros
Now the opposite case: a hypothesis with no zero in its row, and the same row as its rival, a third on each outcome. Whatever is observed, the two likelihoods are equal at every step. So the posterior is the prior, and the data are neutral.

@list heading="Two separate facts" items="No zero: no observation can refute it@no single observation|Same row as the rival: nothing can confirm it@nothing can confirm it"
Two things are true of it, and they should be kept apart. Because it has no zero, no single observation can refute it. Because its row matches its rival's, nothing can confirm it either. Change its row to a half, a quarter, a quarter, and it still forbids nothing, but the same four observations now confirm it, by a factor of eighty one sixty fourths.

@art distance-from-rival
So the slogan, a theory that forbids nothing risks nothing, is exact about refutation. The general measure of what a theory risks is how far its row departs from its rival's, and a zero is the furthest it can go.

@chapter The more it forbids
@art all-on-one
Now let H forbid two of the three outcomes, and give all its probability to the first. That's a bold theory. It's staked almost everything.

@math heading="H forbids two of three outcomes" items="P(o1 | H) = 1,  P(o1 | rival) = 1/3:  factor 3@factor is three;;prior 1/2  →  posterior 3/4@three quarters"
You observe the first outcome. H said that would happen, and the rival, which spread its probability, gave it only a third. The factor is three, and H goes from a half to three quarters. The more a hypothesis forbids, the more its survival counts.

@chapter Popper's own view
@art corroboration
Popper himself refused this reading. On his account, a theory that survives a severe test is corroborated, not made more probable. He held that every universal law has probability zero on any evidence. A reader who follows him keeps the zero from the first case, and treats the other posteriors as bookkeeping. What that reader then owes is an account of why a corroborated theory should be relied on, which Popper agreed he couldn't give.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a well confirmed theory is proven@The first|Thinking an unfalsifiable theory is best supported@The second|Reading a likelihood of zero as a prior of zero@the third"
The first slip is thinking a theory confirmed many times is proven. The second is thinking an unfalsifiable theory is the best supported one. It's never contradicted, and never confirmed either. And the third is reading a likelihood of zero as a prior of zero. The prior is what you believed. The likelihood is what the hypothesis says about an outcome.
