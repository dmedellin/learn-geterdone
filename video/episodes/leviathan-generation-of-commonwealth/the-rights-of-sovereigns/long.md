@chapter Instituting a sovereign
@title text="The Rights of Sovereigns by Institution" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 18" art=thumb
Chapter eighteen draws the consequences of founding a commonwealth, one by one. And the second has been argued over ever since.

@art vote-and-authorise
First, how it's done. A multitude covenant, every one with every one, that whoever the major part gives the right to bear their person shall be authorised, as well by him that voted for it as him that voted against it. The sovereign is the actor. The subjects are the authors.

@art twelve-keys
From that, Hobbes draws twelve rights of the sovereign, counted in order from first to lastly. Each one follows from the covenant of every man with every man.

@list heading="Some of the twelve rights" items="Subjects can't change the form of government@change the form|The sovereign power can't be forfeited@forfeited|His acts can't injure a subject@injure|He judges doctrines, makes property rules, judges disputes@doctrines|He makes war and peace, chooses officers, rewards and punishes@war and peace"
Subjects can't change the form of government without his leave. The power can't be forfeited. Nothing he does can injure a subject. He judges doctrines, sets the rules of property, and judges disputes. He makes war and peace, chooses officers, rewards and punishes, and gives titles of honour.

@chapter No covenant, no breach
@quote text="there can happen no breach of Covenant on the part of the Soveraigne; and consequently none of his Subjects, by any pretence of forfeiture, can be freed from his Subjection." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 18"
The right to bear their person is given to him by covenant only of one to another, and not of him to any of them. So there can happen no breach of covenant on the part of the sovereign; and consequently none of his subjects, by any pretence of forfeiture, can be freed from his subjection.

@art no-judge
Suppose he had covenanted beforehand to rule well. With whom? Not with the multitude as one party, since they aren't yet one person. With each separately? Then any act called a breach is the act of every one of them. And if one claims a breach and another denies it, there's no judge. It returns to the sword.

@words text="The People of Rome made a covenant with the Romans?"
He offers a test. Nobody imagines a sovereign assembly made a covenant. No man is so dull as to say the people of Rome made a covenant with the Romans. We think otherwise of a monarch, he says, out of ambition to share in an assembly's rule.

@chapter As an argument
@math heading="The hobbes preset" items="c: the sovereign is party to a covenant@let c;;b: he breaches it@b;;f: subjects are freed by forfeiture@f;;¬c,  b → c,  f → b  ∴  ¬f   valid@valid"
Write it down. Let c be: the sovereign is party to a covenant with his subjects. Let b be: he breaches it. And f: subjects are freed by forfeiture. There's no such covenant. A breach needs a covenant. Forfeiture needs a breach. So no one is freed. The lab finds it valid.

@art held-in-trust
But valid isn't true. John Locke held that those in power hold it in trust, for ends the people set, and may be removed when they abuse it. That makes c true. Change the first premise to c, and one row breaks the argument: c, b and f all true.

@words text="Where there is no judge on earth, the appeal lies to heaven"
Drop the third premise instead, and subjects might be freed short of a breach, say by failure to protect them. Again one row fails. Hobbes's answer to a trust is the sword again: no judge between the parties. Locke replied the people shall judge, and where there's no judge on earth, appeal lies to heaven.

@chapter Iniquity, not injury
@quote text="It is true that they that have Soveraigne power, may commit Iniquity; but not Injustice, or Injury in the proper signification." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 18"
The fourth right runs on authorship. A subject who complains of injury from his sovereign complains of what he himself authored. But that doesn't mean the sovereign can do no wrong. It is true that they that have sovereign power may commit iniquity, but not injustice, or injury in the proper signification.

@chapter Indivisible
@art three-pillars
The rights can't be divided. Give away the militia, and judging is in vain, with no one to enforce it. Give away the power to raise money, and the militia is in vain. Hobbes points at England. If people hadn't believed power was divided between King, Lords and Commons, they'd never have fallen into this civil war.

@art scales-war
And to the charge that subjects of such a power are miserable, he answers by comparison. The worst any government does to the people is scarcely felt next to the horrible calamities of civil war. The lab can't weigh that comparison.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The sovereign promises to rule well@The first|A valid argument is sound@The second|No injury means no wrong@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the sovereign promises to rule well and can be deposed. He makes no covenant. The second is thinking a valid argument is sound. The lab only tells you c is where the dispute lies. And the third is reading no injury as no wrong. He may commit iniquity.
