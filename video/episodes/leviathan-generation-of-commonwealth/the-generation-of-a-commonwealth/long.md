@chapter What commonwealths are for
@title text="The Generation of a Commonwealth" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 17" art=thumb
People naturally love liberty, and dominion over others. So why would they put a restraint on themselves? Hobbes says: foresight of their own preservation, and a more contented life. To get out of the misery of war.

@quote text="Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 17"
The laws of nature bind a man to want peace. They don't make it safe to act peacefully while others don't. Left alone, they're contrary to our passions for partiality, pride and revenge. Covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all.

@chapter Three tempting answers
@art small-band
Is it enough to join a few people together? No. Small additions on one side or the other make the advantage great enough to carry the victory. How many you need is fixed by comparison with the enemy you fear.

@art tug-of-war
Is a very large multitude enough? Not if each acts on his own judgement. Then they hinder one another, and reduce their strength by mutual opposition to nothing. And one judgement for a single war isn't enough either. When the enemy goes, they fall back into war among themselves.

@chapter Bees and ants
@art beehive
Then the objection everyone makes. Bees and ants live sociably. Aristotle called them political creatures. Why can't mankind do the same?

@list heading="Why men are not bees" items="Men compete for honour and dignity@honour and dignity|For bees, common good is private good@common good|Bees agree by nature, men by covenant@by covenant"
Hobbes gives six answers. First, men are continually in competition for honour and dignity, and bees aren't. Second, for bees the common good is the private good, while man, who compares himself with others, relishes nothing but what's eminent. And lastly, their agreement is natural, and men's is by covenant only, which is artificial.

@chapter Ten people, no sword
@art ten-dots-split
Write it as a table, with the lesson's own numbers, not Hobbes's. Ten people each choose to keep the peace or to break it.

@math heading="The men preset" items="keep: 1 + k@one plus k;;break: 5 + k@five plus k;;break − keep = 4, whatever k is@four more"
Let k be how many of the other nine keep it. A keeper gets one plus k. A breaker gets five plus k, the same security, plus plunder. So breaking pays four more, whatever the others do.

@math heading="The equilibrium" items="nobody keeps: 5 each, 50 in all@Each gets five;;all keep: 10 each, 100 in all@give each ten"
So the one equilibrium is that nobody keeps. Each gets five, fifty in all, when all keeping would give each ten, a hundred in all. That's the multitude of the chapter, ruined not by what anyone wants, but by what each one does.

@chapter The covenant
@quote text="I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 17"
The only way out is to confer all their power on one man or one assembly, reducing all their wills to one. As if every man said to every man: I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man, or this assembly, on this condition, that thou give up thy right to him, and authorise all his actions in like manner.

@art leviathan-rising
That's more than consent or concord. It's a real unity of them all in one person. The multitude so united is a commonwealth. This is the generation of that great Leviathan, or, more reverently, of that mortal god, to which we owe, under the immortal God, our peace and defence.

@chapter What the sword does
@math heading="A penalty on breaking" items="break − keep = 4 − p@four minus the penalty;;p = 3: breaking still pays 1 more@three;;p = 4: a tie@four;;p = 5: keeping pays 1 more → all ten keep@five"
Now subtract a penalty from the breaker. Breaking minus keeping becomes four minus the penalty. At three, breaking still pays one more. At four, it's a tie. At five, keeping pays one more, whatever the others do.

@art ten-dots-joined
So the one equilibrium is that all ten keep the peace. The private good and the common good now coincide. The table has become the bees' table.

@words text="Not a tenth player who punishes"
What the lab doesn't show is where the penalty comes from. The sovereign isn't a tenth player who happens to punish. He's the one person every one has authorised, and by terror of the strength each gave him, he brings their wills to peace. Critics ask who holds a sword over him.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A covenant of peace keeps itself@The first|A big enough crowd needs no sovereign@The second|It's a covenant between people and ruler@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a covenant of peace keeps itself. Everyone prefers peace, and each still breaks. The second is thinking a big enough crowd needs no sovereign. And the third is reading it as a covenant between the people and a ruler. It's every man with every man, and the sovereign isn't a party.
