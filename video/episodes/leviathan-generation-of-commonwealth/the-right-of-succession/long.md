@chapter The founder dies
@title text="The Right of Succession" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 19" art=thumb
Think of a company's articles saying who runs the firm when the founder dies. A commonwealth needs the same thing. Governments are mortal. Not only monarchs, but whole assemblies, die.

@art hourglass-crown
So as there was order taken for an artificial man, there must be order taken for an artificial eternity of life. Without it, a people ruled by one man falls back into war at his death. That's the right of succession.

@list heading="Where it's hard" items="Democracy: the whole can't die@democracy|Aristocracy: the assembly elects a replacement@aristocracy|Monarchy: who appoints, and whom?@monarchy"
In a democracy the question doesn't arise, since the whole assembly can't fail unless the multitude fails. In an aristocracy, the assembly elects another member. The greatest difficulty is in monarchy, where it isn't plain who appoints the successor, or whom.

@chapter Who appoints?
@art dissolved-crowd
Either the present holder has the right, or it returns to the dissolved multitude. Not the second. A sovereign's death leaves the multitude without any representative, unable to act at all. Every man has an equal right to submit to whoever he thinks can protect him, and that's war again.

@words text="Left to the judgment and will of the present possessor"
So by the institution of monarchy, disposing of the successor is always left to the judgment and will of the present possessor.

@chapter How is his will known?
@list heading="In order" items="Express words or testament@testament|A custom he has not controlled@custom|Natural presumption@presumption"
How is that will known? First, by express words or testament, in speech or writing. And the word heir doesn't of itself mean his children, but whoever he declares. Second, by a custom he hasn't controlled. Last, where neither exists, by natural presumption.

@art sealed-testament
A testament can be spoken or written, as the first emperors of Rome declared who should be their heirs. Whoever it names has the right, whether kin or not.

@quote text="whatsoever Custome a man may by a word controule, and does not, it is a naturall signe he would have that Custome stand." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 19"
Why does custom count? Because the monarch could have declared otherwise in his life. Whatsoever custom a man may by a word control, and does not, it is a natural sign he would have that custom stand.

@art custom-path
So where the custom is that the next of kin succeeds, the next of kin has the right. The same goes for a custom of the male line, and for one that advances the female.

@chapter The presumptions
@list heading="Without testament or custom" items="The government stays a monarchy@stays a monarchy|A child of his own before any other@child of his own|A male before a female, among his own@male|Then the nearer in blood@nearer in blood"
Only then do the presumptions apply. The government stays a monarchy. A child of his own, male or female, is preferred before any other. Of his own, rather a male than a female. And where his issue fails, a brother before a stranger, and the nearer in blood before the more remote.

@words text="An opinion of 1651, stated as a presumption"
The reasons are Hobbes's, and his one about men and women is an opinion of sixteen fifty one, stated as a guess about what a monarch would want. The lab applies the order and doesn't endorse the reason. It also adds two things Hobbes doesn't say here: elder before younger, and a dead child's children in his place.

@chapter A royal family
@art family-tree
Take the lesson's family. The king has a prince, a princess and a younger son. His brother, the duke, has a son. With no testament and no custom, the order is the prince, then the younger son, then the princess, then the duke, then the duke's son.

@math heading="Four cases" items="testament names the nephew → the nephew@names his nephew;;the prince dies without issue → the younger son@the prince dies;;only a daughter, no custom → the daughter@only a daughter;;male-line custom → the brother@male line"
If the king names his nephew in writing, the nephew succeeds, over the king's own children. If the prince dies without issue, the younger son succeeds. In a second family, a king with only a daughter and a brother leaves her the throne. But under an uncontrolled custom of the male line, the brother succeeds.

@art stranger-crown
The usual objection is that a king could leave the kingdom to a stranger, who'd oppress the people. Hobbes answers that oppression comes from unskilful governors, not strangers, and the same could come by marriage. In sixteen fifty one this wasn't academic. Charles the first was executed two years before, and his son crowned in Scotland that January.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The eldest son has a right of his own@The first|The presumptions are primogeniture@The second|The people share in choosing@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the eldest son has a right independent of the king's will. A testament overrides him. The second is reading the presumptions as primogeniture. Hobbes doesn't say elder before younger here. And the third is thinking the people share in choosing. That would dissolve the commonwealth.
