@chapter A crowd has no errand
@title text="A Multitude Made One Person" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 16" art=thumb
Picture a crowd on a station platform. Ask what the crowd intends, and there's no answer. Each person has an errand. The crowd has none.

@art crowd-platform
Hobbes says the same of a multitude. It's naturally not one, but many, so it can't be understood as one. Unity comes only through someone who bears its person.

@quote text="For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the Unity of the Represented, that maketh the Person One." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 16"
A multitude is made one person when one man, or one person, represents it, with the consent of every one of them in particular. For it is the unity of the representer, not the unity of the represented, that maketh the person one.

@art many-authors
Each member then stands to the representer as an author to an actor. Every man gives authority from himself in particular, and owns what the representer does, if he gave it without limit. If he limited it, he owns only what he gave.

@chapter The greater number
@words text="The voice of the greater number is the voice of them all"
But what if the representer is a body of many men? Hobbes's first rule is simple. The voice of the greater number must be considered as the voice of them all.

@art negatives-cancel
His reason is about what's left over. If the lesser number says yes and the greater says no, there are negatives more than enough to destroy the affirmatives. The excess of noes, uncontradicted, is the only voice the assembly has.

@chapter When the voices are equal
@list heading="Equal voices" items="Usually: mute, incapable of action@mute|In a trial: absolve@In a trial|On executing now or later: delay@later"
An even assembly, especially a small one, is often mute and incapable of action. Two cases are exceptions. In a trial, equal voices absolve, because not to condemn is to absolve. And on whether to execute now or later, equal voices mean delay.

@art three-vetoes
Third rule. If every member of three or more holds a negative voice, able to cancel all the rest, the body is no representative. On the lesson's model, with each member as likely to say yes as no, three vetoes pass a motion one time in eight, against four in eight by the greater number.

@chapter Counting voices
@art even-split
The lab works the voices. Each member says yes, no, or abstains, and the lab reports whether the motion passes, fails, or the assembly is mute.

@math heading="Three presets" items="six-even: 3 yes, 3 no → mute@three yes, three no;;seven: 4 yes, 3 no → passes@four to three;;one-abstains: 3 yes, 2 no, 1 abstains@abstaining"
Six members, three yes, three no: the assembly is mute. Add a seventh who says yes, and the motion passes four to three. Now six members with three yes, two no, and one abstaining.

@words text="Greater number: passes · more than half of all: fails"
Under the greater number of voices given, it passes. Under a rule needing more than half of all members, it fails, because three isn't more than half of six. Hobbes counts voices given. The second rule is the lab's, to show a rule must be chosen.

@chapter How often a tie?
@math heading="Chance of an even split" items="4: 6/16 = 3/8@four;;6: 20/64 = 5/16@six;;10: 63/256@ten;;20: 46189/262144 ≈ 17.62%@twenty"
How often does an even assembly tie? Assume each member is equally likely to say yes or no, a model that's the lesson's, not Hobbes's. For four members, six sixteenths, three eighths. For six, twenty sixty fourths, five sixteenths. For ten, sixty three over two fifty six. For twenty, about seventeen point six two percent.

@art shrinking-ties
The chance falls as the assembly grows, which fits Hobbes's especially when the number is not great. An odd assembly can't split evenly at all. But real members aren't coins, and a body split into parties will tie more often than this.

@words text="Is the dissenter an author?"
The old difficulty: Locke accepted that the majority's act is the whole's act, and Rousseau asked what's left of a dissenter's freedom. Hobbes's answer is that the dissenter is an author, because he gave the authority. Whether a person can give that for good is the next two chapters.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="An even assembly is always mute@The first|The crowd itself is one person@The second|An odd assembly can't go wrong@And the third"
The first slip is thinking an even assembly is always mute. Hobbes says often, and names two exceptions. The second is thinking the crowd itself is one person. Only the representer is. And the third is thinking an odd assembly can't go wrong. Give every member a veto, and it's often mute.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Eleven: 5 yes, 4 no, 2 abstain" sub="greater number? more than half of all?"
Your turn. An assembly of eleven has five yes, four no, and two abstaining. Decide it under the greater number, then under more than half of all members, and give the margin.
