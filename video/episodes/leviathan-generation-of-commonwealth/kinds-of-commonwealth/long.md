@chapter Only three
@title text="The Several Kinds of Commonwealth" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 19" art=thumb
How many kinds of government are there? Aristotle counted six, three right and three corrupt. Hobbes counts three, and proves there can't be more.

@quote text="Other kind of Common-wealth there can be none: for either One, or More, or All, must have the Soveraign Power (which I have shewn to be indivisible) entire." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 19"
One man as representative is a monarchy. An assembly of all who will come is a democracy. An assembly of a part only is an aristocracy. Other kind of commonwealth there can be none: for either one, or more, or all, must have the sovereign power, which is indivisible, entire.

@list heading="Names of dislike" items="Monarchy misliked: tyranny@tyranny|Aristocracy misliked: oligarchy@oligarchy|Democracy misliked: anarchy@anarchy"
So tyranny, oligarchy and anarchy aren't other forms. They're the same three, misliked. Those discontented under monarchy call it tyranny. Those displeased with aristocracy call it oligarchy. And those grieved under democracy call it anarchy.

@art same-colleague
You know the pattern. The same colleague is decisive when you agree with him and bossy when you don't.

@chapter No mixed forms
@art sparta-kings
The same rule dissolves mixed government. A king whose power is limited isn't sovereign. The sovereignty was always with whoever could limit him, as in Sparta, where the kings led the armies and the Ephori held the sovereignty. In sixteen forty two, the king's own answer to Parliament had called England a mixture of king, lords and commons.

@chapter Which is best?
@words text="Not power, but aptitude to produce peace"
The three differ not in power, but in their aptitude to produce the peace and security of the people. And Hobbes compares monarchy with assemblies on six points.

@art crown-and-coffers
First, interest. Whoever bears the people's person also bears his own. Where public and private interest are most closely joined, the public is most advanced, and in a monarchy they are: a king is rich only when his subjects are. Second, a monarch hears counsel from whom he pleases, an assembly only its members.

@art assembly-benches
Third, besides the inconstancy of human nature, an assembly has an inconstancy from number. The absence of a few, or the diligent appearance of a few on the other side, undoes today what was concluded yesterday.

@math heading="Inconstancy from number" items="Monday: 5 for, 4 against → passes@Monday;;Tuesday, two of the five away: 3 for, 4 against → fails@Tuesday"
Nine members vote five to four for a motion on Monday. On Tuesday, two of the five are away, and the same motion fails, three to four.

@chapter A cycle
@art one-throne
Fourth, a monarch can't disagree with himself out of envy or interest. But an assembly may, even to the height of civil war.

@art wall-fleet-army
Here's another way it can, which Hobbes doesn't describe. The Marquis de Condorcet found it a century and a third later. Nine voters, three proposals: a wall, a fleet and an army.

@math heading="The cycle preset" items="4 voters: wall, fleet, army@Four want;;3 voters: fleet, army, wall@three want;;2 voters: army, wall, fleet@two want;;wall beats fleet 6–3; fleet beats army 7–2; army beats wall 5–4@six to three"
Four want the wall first, then the fleet, then the army. Three want the fleet, then the army, then the wall. Two want the army, then the wall, then the fleet. Now the wall beats the fleet six to three, the fleet beats the army seven to two, and the army beats the wall five to four.

@words text="Whoever sets the agenda picks the outcome"
No proposal beats both the others. So the winner depends on the order. Vote fleet against army first, and the wall wins. Vote wall against fleet first, and the army wins. Each proposal wins under one order. And by plurality, the wall wins; by Borda count, the wall and fleet tie at ten.

@words text="Milton: safer with many?"
Republicans of Hobbes's own time, Milton among them, answered that rule by one leaves a nation at one man's mercy. Hobbes's reply is his first point: men's passions are commonly more potent than their reason, so it matters where public and private interest are joined. The lab can't say who's right.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Tyranny and oligarchy are further kinds@The first|Hobbes's inconstancy is the cycle@The second|A cycle proves assemblies worse@And the third"
The first slip is thinking tyranny and oligarchy are further kinds. The second is thinking Hobbes's inconstancy is the cycle. His is about who's present. And the third is thinking a cycle proves assemblies worse than monarchs. It shows one can happen, not how often.
