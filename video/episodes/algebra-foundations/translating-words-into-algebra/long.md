@chapter Before any algebra
@title text="Translating Words into Algebra" sub="Algebra Foundations" art=thumb
Most word problem errors happen before any algebra does. They happen in deciding what the letter stands for, and which way round a subtraction goes. The translation itself is a short vocabulary, plus two rules.

@art name-tag
So start by naming the unknown, with its units. Write a full sentence: let n be the number of quarters. It takes one line, and it settles what's being counted, and in what units.

@words text="25n" sub="right if n counts quarters, meaningless if n is dollars"
Here's why. If n is the number of quarters, then twenty five n is their value in cents, and the expression is right. If n is already an amount in dollars, twenty five n is meaningless. You can't check an expression without knowing what the letter means.

@chapter The vocabulary
@list heading="Phrase → expression" items="the sum of a and b   →   a + b@the sum of a and b|a decreased by b   →   a − b@decreased by|the product of a and b   →   ab@the product|twice a   →   2a@twice a|the quotient of a and b   →   a/b@the quotient|the square of a   →   a²@the square of a"
Most phrases are what you'd expect. The sum of a and b is a plus b. a decreased by b is a minus b. The product is a b, and twice a is two a. The quotient of a and b is a over b, and the square of a is a squared.

@words text="Conventions of English" sub="if a sentence is ambiguous, say which reading you took"
These are conventions of English, not of mathematics. Learn them, because everyone writing problems uses them the same way. But when a sentence is genuinely ambiguous, say which reading you took. Don't guess silently.

@chapter Phrases that reverse the order
@art order-reverse
Addition and multiplication don't care about order. Subtraction and division do. And a few phrases name the second quantity first.

@math items="five less than n   →   n − 5@five less than a number;;n less than five   →   5 − n@n less than five;;five subtracted from n   →   n − 5@subtracted from"
Five less than a number n is n minus five, not five minus n. n less than five is five minus n. And five subtracted from n is n minus five. The English puts the subtracted amount first, and the algebra puts it second.

@words text="test n = 8:   8 − 5 = 3" sub="5 − 8 = −3, the other phrase"
Test it at eight. Five less than eight is three, and eight minus five is three. Five minus eight is minus three, so that's the other phrase. Watch for the word into, too: n divided into four is four over n.

@chapter Phrases that make a bracket
@art package-bracket
The sum of n and three packages two things into one quantity. Anything you do to that package applies to all of it, and in algebra, that means a bracket.

@math items="twice the sum of n and 3   →   2(n + 3)@twice the sum;;the sum of twice n and 3   →   2n + 3@the sum of twice n;;at n = 5:   16 against 13@at five"
Twice the sum of n and three is two times, n plus three. The sum of twice n and three is two n plus three. Almost the same words. But at five, the first gives sixteen and the second gives thirteen.

@art only-first-doubled
Drop the bracket, and you don't get a slightly different answer. You get a different quantity, because only the first part gets doubled.

@math items="three less than the square of n   →   n² − 3@three less than the square;;the square of three less than n   →   (n − 3)²@the square of three less;;at n = 5:   22 against 4@at five"
Same again with squares. Three less than the square of n is n squared minus three. The square of three less than n is n minus three, all squared. At five, that's twenty two against four.

@chapter Families of unknowns
@art consecutive-steps
Some phrases describe several unknowns at once, and one letter covers them all. Three consecutive integers, the smallest being n, are n, n plus one, and n plus two. For consecutive even integers, it's n, n plus two, and n plus four.

@words text="25q + 10d" sub="two unknowns, two letters"
A rectangle three centimetres longer than it is wide, with width w, has length w plus three. And the value in cents of q quarters and d dimes is twenty five q plus ten d. Two unknowns need two letters.

@chapter Test with a number
@art test-scale
To check a translation, pick a number, read the sentence aloud with it, and compare with your expression. Avoid zero and one, which make too many wrong answers agree. And make sure your number separates the readings: at five, n minus five and five minus n both give zero.

@chapter Try it
@words text="three times the difference of n and 4"
Try three times the difference of n and four. The packaged quantity is the difference, so it's three times, n minus four. At six, that gives six. If you wrote three n minus four, you'd get fourteen, because only the first part got tripled.
