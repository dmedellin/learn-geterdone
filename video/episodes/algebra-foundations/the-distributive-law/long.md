@chapter The bridge
@title text="The Distributive Law" sub="Algebra Foundations" art=thumb
Commutativity and associativity rearrange within one operation. The distributive law is the only property that lets multiplication and addition talk to each other. That makes it the licence behind expanding, factoring, and nearly every rearrangement you'll make.

@words text="a(b + c) = ab + ac" sub="for all real a, b, c"
Here it is. For all real numbers a, b and c, a times, b plus c, equals a b plus a c. Since multiplication is commutative, it works with the factor on the other side too.

@art area-rectangle
Here's a picture to make it feel inevitable. A rectangle three high and x plus four wide splits into a three by x piece and a three by four piece. Its area is three times, x plus four, and also three x plus twelve. That's a reason to believe the law, not a proof.

@chapter The invisible minus one
@words text="b − c = b + (−c)"
The law is stated for addition, and that's enough. Subtracting c is adding minus c. So distributing over a subtraction is just distributing over a sum.

@math items="3(x + 4) = 3x + 12@three times;;−2(x − 5) = (−2)x + (−2)(−5) = −2x + 10@minus two times;;−(x − 7) = (−1)x + (−1)(−7) = −x + 7@minus, x minus seven;;(1/2)(4x − 6) = 2x − 3@one half times"
Three times, x plus four, is three x plus twelve. Minus two times, x minus five, is minus two x, plus ten, because minus two times minus five is ten. Minus, x minus seven, is minus x plus seven. And one half times, four x minus six, is two x minus three.

@art minus-one
Study the third one. A minus sign in front of a bracket is a factor of minus one, and it multiplies every term inside. So it's minus x plus seven, not minus x minus seven. The habit worth forming is to write the minus one in. It turns a sign question into a multiplication.

@chapter More terms, more brackets
@art reach-every-term
There's no limit on how many terms sit inside the bracket. Two times, x plus y plus three, is two x plus two y plus six. The factor outside reaches every term inside.

@math heading="(x + 2)(x − 3)" items="= x(x − 3) + 2(x − 3)@distribute the sum;;= x² − 3x + 2x − 6@inside each piece;;= x² − x − 6@then collect"
Two brackets is the same law, used twice. First distribute the sum: x times, x minus three, plus two times, x minus three. Then distribute inside each piece: x squared minus three x plus two x minus six. Then collect, to get x squared minus x minus six.

@art product-grid
So there's no separate rule for multiplying two brackets. Every term of the first meets every term of the second, once. Two terms times two terms makes four products.

@chapter What isn't the law
@math heading="Not the distributive law" items="(a + b)² ≠ a² + b²:    16 against 10@squaring;;√(a + b) ≠ √a + √b:    5 against 7@roots;;a(bc) ≠ (ab)(ac):    24 against 48@multiplication over multiplication;;12/(x + 6) ≠ 12/x + 12/6:    1 against 4@a sum in a denominator"
Four things look like the law and aren't. Squaring over a sum: at one and three, that's sixteen against ten. Roots over a sum: at nine and sixteen, five against seven. Multiplication over multiplication: twenty four against forty eight. And a sum in a denominator: at x equals six, one against four.

@art bar-split
Division is where the law is one sided. x plus six, over two, does split into x over two, plus three, because dividing by two is multiplying by a half. But twelve over, x plus six, doesn't. A sum you're dividing splits. A sum you're dividing by does not.

@chapter Right to left
@art factor-out
Read the law backwards, a b plus a c equals a times, b plus c, and it's called factoring. Six x plus fifteen is three times, two x plus five, because both terms share a factor of three.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Distributing to the first term only@the first term only|Splitting a sum in the denominator@splitting a sum|Expecting squares and roots to distribute@squares and roots"
Three slips. Distributing to the first term only: minus two times, x minus five, is minus two x plus ten, not minus two x minus five. Splitting a sum that's in the denominator. And expecting squares and roots to distribute.

@words text="check at x = 1" sub="−2(1 − 5) = 8,    −2 + 10 = 8"
A quick check catches nearly every sign error. Put x equals one into the original and into your answer. Minus two times, one minus five, is eight. Minus two plus ten is eight. Agreement isn't proof, but a mismatch would prove you wrong.

@chapter Try it
@words text="−3(2x − 5)"
Try minus three times, two x minus five. Write minus three times two x, then minus three times minus five. You should get minus six x plus fifteen. If you got minus six x minus fifteen, you kept the inner sign instead of multiplying it.
