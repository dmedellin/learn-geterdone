@chapter The question
@title text="Real Numbers and the Number Line" sub="Algebra Foundations" art=thumb
Which numbers are there, and where does each one sit? The answer is the map the rest of algebra is drawn on.

@art nested-sets
The number systems are built outward, each one inside the next, because the one before couldn't answer a question.

@list heading="Each system fails a question" items="3 − 5@three minus five|3 / 5@three over five|the side of a square of area 2@the side of a square"
The counting numbers can't answer three minus five. The integers can't answer three over five. And the fractions can't tell you the side of a square whose area is two. Each answer forces a bigger system.

@chapter Four systems
@list heading="The four systems" items="Naturals: 1, 2, 3, …@natural numbers|Integers: …, −1, 0, 1, …@integers add|Rationals: p/q, with q ≠ 0@rational number|Reals: every point on the line@real numbers"
The natural numbers are one, two, three, and on. The integers add their negatives, and zero. A rational number is one that can be written as p over q, where p and q are integers and q isn't zero. And the real numbers are all the numbers on the line.

@words text="Can be written" sub="4 = 4/1 · 0.25 = 1/4"
Notice the words can be written. Four is rational because it's four over one. Zero point two five is one quarter. The fraction just has to exist.

@chapter Decimals tell you which kind
@math heading="Stops, repeats, or neither" items="1/4 = 0.25   stops@one quarter;;1/3 = 0.333…   repeats@one third;;3/7 = 0.428571428571…   repeats@three sevenths;;√2 = 1.41421356…   neither@the square root of two"
Every rational number has a decimal that either stops or falls into a repeating block. One quarter is zero point two five, and stops. One third repeats a single three forever. Three sevenths repeats a block of six digits. And the square root of two does neither. That's what makes it irrational.

@words text="0.333 ≠ 1/3" sub="0.333 = 333/1000, a smaller number"
Those dots aren't an approximation. But zero point three three three, stopped, is a different number: three hundred thirty three thousandths, which is smaller. The fraction is the number. The decimal is a report on it.

@art square-diagonal
And square roots? The root of a whole number is whole when that number is a perfect square, and irrational otherwise. The root of nine is three. The roots of two and ten are irrational.

@chapter The line
@art number-line
That means the fractions have gaps, and the line doesn't. Fix a point for zero and a length for one. Now every real number matches exactly one point, and every point exactly one real number. That second half is an assumption about the reals, not something proved here.

@art one-point
A number is a place, not a spelling. One half, two quarters, zero point five and three sixths are four ways of writing one number, and it sits at one point on the line.

@art zoom-between
When a is less than b, a sits to the left of b. Between any two different reals there's always another one. Their average will do. So no real number has a next one. The integers do: after four comes five.

@chapter Comparing exactly
@math heading="1.41 against √2" items="1.41² = 1.9881@one point four one squared;;1.9881 < 2@less than two;;so 1.41 < √2@so one point four one"
To compare two numbers exactly, use a common denominator, or square them when both are positive. Take one point four one against the root of two. One point four one squared is one point nine eight eight one, which is less than two. So one point four one is less than the root of two.

@art mirror-line
Squaring is safe here only because both are positive. On negatives it reverses the order: minus three is less than minus two, but nine is bigger than four. A minus sign reflects the line. Five quarters is bigger than one point two, so minus five quarters is smaller than minus one point two.

@chapter Six numbers, sorted
@words text="Six numbers, in order" sub="no decimal trusted beyond its digits"
Now the lesson's worked example. Six numbers to put in order, trusting no decimal beyond the digits it has.

@math items="−5/4 = −25/20,  −1.2 = −24/20@minus twenty five twentieths;;so −5/4 < −1.2@the leftmost;;0.6 = 9/15,  2/3 = 10/15@nine fifteenths;;so 0.6 < 2/3@so zero point six"
Start with the negatives. Minus five quarters is minus twenty five twentieths, and minus one point two is minus twenty four twentieths. So minus five quarters is the leftmost. Near zero point six, it's nine fifteenths against ten fifteenths, so zero point six is less than two thirds.

@math items="1.41 < √2   by squaring@squaring test;;−5/4 < −1.2 < 0.6 < 2/3 < 1.41 < √2@the full order"
And the squaring test already put one point four one below the root of two. So the full order is minus five quarters, minus one point two, zero point six, two thirds, one point four one, and the root of two. Only one of the six is irrational.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating a rounded decimal as the number@The first|Assuming a root sign means irrational@The second|Ordering negatives by their digits@The third"
Three slips are worth naming. The first is treating a rounded decimal as the number. One point four one four and the root of two agree to three places, and they're different numbers. The second is assuming a root sign means irrational. The third is ordering negatives by their digits.
