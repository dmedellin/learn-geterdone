@chapter The idea
@title text="Variables, Expressions, and Terms" sub="Algebra Foundations" art=thumb
Before you can do algebra, you have to be able to read it. That means knowing what a letter is, what an expression is, and how to take one apart without changing it.

@art recipe-machine
An expression is a recipe for a number. It says what to do, and given values for the letters, it produces a value. It doesn't claim anything. Two x plus five is an expression. Two x plus five equals eleven is an equation, and that does make a claim.

@words text="Solve an equation, never an expression"
That difference matters straight away, because you can solve an equation, and you can't solve an expression. An expression gets read, rewritten, or evaluated.

@chapter Variables and constants
@list heading="The vocabulary" items="Variable: a letter for a number not fixed@a variable|Constant: a number written as itself@a constant|Expression: both, joined by arithmetic@an algebraic expression"
A variable is a letter standing for a number whose value isn't fixed. A constant is a number written as itself. And an algebraic expression is any combination of the two, built with the operations of arithmetic.

@words text="x is one number" sub="it varies across problems, not across lines"
The word variable is a little misleading. Within one calculation, the letter stands for one number, and it doesn't change while you work. It varies across problems, not across lines. And it obeys no new rules.

@chapter Terms
@art term-split
The terms of an expression are the pieces it splits into at its top level plus and minus signs. Top level means not inside any bracket. And the sign just in front of a piece belongs to that piece.

@math heading="3x² − 5x + 7" items="3x² − 5x + 7 = 3x² + (−5x) + 7@always a sum;;term 1:  3x²@the first term;;term 2:  −5x@the second term;;term 3:  7@the third"
So an expression is always a sum of its terms, and some of them are negative. In three x squared minus five x plus seven, the first term is three x squared. The second term is minus five x, sign included. The third is seven.

@art sign-tag
Why insist on the sign? Because later you'll reorder terms. Call the second term five x and remember the minus separately, and it works, until the terms move. Then the sign lands on the wrong one. When a term moves, its sign moves with it.

@chapter Coefficients
@math heading="Coefficient and variable part" items="−5x:  coefficient −5, variable part x@minus five x;;(2/3)x²:  coefficient 2/3@two thirds x squared;;7:  a constant term@like seven"
A term splits into a coefficient, the number in front with its sign, and a variable part, the letters with their exponents. In minus five x, the coefficient is minus five. In two thirds x squared, it's two thirds. A term with no letters, like seven, is a constant term.

@words text="x = 1x      −x = (−1)x" sub="invisible, not zero"
Two coefficients are written invisibly, and they're misread all the time. x means one x, so its coefficient is one. Minus x means minus one times x, so its coefficient is minus one. Neither is zero, and neither is missing.

@chapter Terms and factors
@art factors-vs-terms
Terms are what an expression is added out of. Factors are what a term is multiplied out of. Four x y is one term, with factors four, x and y. Confusing the two is what makes people cancel across a plus sign.

@art bracket-one-term
A bracket makes this visible. Five times, x plus two, is one term, because there's no top level plus or minus in it. Multiply it out and you get five x plus ten, which is two terms. Same value for every x, different number of terms. So the honest answer names the form: one as written, two expanded.

@chapter Degree
@math heading="Degree of a term" items="4xy:   1 + 1 = 2@four x y;;7 = 7x⁰:   degree 0@degree zero"
The degree of a term is the sum of the exponents on its variables. Four x y has degree one plus one, which is two. A constant like seven has degree zero. The degree of an expression is the largest degree among its terms, once like terms are combined.

@art cancel-out
That last part matters. In five x minus five x plus four, the highest degree on the page is one. But the two x terms add to zero, and the expression is just four, of degree zero. Degree describes the expression, not the ink.

@math heading="4xy − y² + 2" items="three terms@three terms;;coefficients:  4,  −1,  2@their coefficients;;degrees:  2,  2,  0@their degrees;;degree of the expression:  2@has degree two"
Let's read one, part by part. Four x y minus y squared plus two has three terms. Their coefficients are four, minus one and two. Their degrees are two, two and zero. So the expression has degree two.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Leaving the minus sign behind@leaving the minus|Reading an invisible coefficient as zero@an invisible coefficient|Counting factors as terms@counting the factors"
Three slips to watch. Leaving the minus sign behind when you name a term. Reading an invisible coefficient as zero, which deletes a term. And counting the factors of a term as terms. Multiplication never starts a new term.

@chapter Try it
@words text="−3a²b + 4b − 7" sub="mark the top-level signs first"
Here's one to try. Minus three a squared b, plus four b, minus seven. Mark the top level signs first, then name each term and its coefficient. You should find three terms, with coefficients minus three, four and minus seven. If you wrote three for the first, you left its sign behind.
