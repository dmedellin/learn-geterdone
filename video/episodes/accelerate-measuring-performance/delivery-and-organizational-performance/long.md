@chapter Does delivery matter?
@title text="Delivery and Organizational Performance" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 2" art=thumb
The four delivery measures only matter if delivery matters to the organization. So the last part of chapter two asks exactly that.

@art self-rating
Respondents rated their organization's relative performance on profitability, market share and productivity. The authors say that scale has been validated several times, correlates with return on investment, and holds up through economic cycles.

@quote text="high-performing organizations were consistently twice as likely to exceed these goals as low performers" cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 2"
The finding, across several years, is one sentence: high performing organizations were consistently twice as likely to exceed these goals as low performers. The authors read it as showing that delivery capability can be a competitive advantage.

@chapter How to hold it
@list heading="Two things about the measure" items="Self-reported@self reported|Relative@relative"
Two things about the measure matter. It's self reported: both the delivery measures and the organizational rating are survey answers. And it's relative: respondents place their organization against others, not against a number.

@art predictive-arrows
In twenty seventeen they added noncommercial goals, such as customer satisfaction and the organization's mission, and high performers were again twice as likely to exceed them. The book draws arrows from delivery performance to both. Each arrow is a predictive relationship, read as drives, predicts or affects.

@chapter Three consequences
@list heading="What follows" items="Small batches let you experiment@small batches|Don't outsource strategic software@outsourcing|Change approval boards don't help@change approval boards"
Small batches let a team collect user feedback quickly, for example by A B testing. Delivery performance is an argument against outsourcing software that's strategic, while payroll or office tools can be bought as a service. And a spoiler: change approval boards don't improve delivery. They're correlated negatively with both tempo and stability.

@chapter Driving change
@art fear-numbers
With the measures defined, teams can be compared, tracked over time and tested for what moves them. But the chapter ends with a caution, from a passage the authors quote from Lean Enterprise. In pathological and bureaucratic cultures, measurement becomes a means of control, and people keep back what would unsettle those in charge.

@quote text="whenever there is fear, you get the wrong numbers" cite="Deming, as quoted in Accelerate, Chapter 2"
Or, in Deming's words as that passage gives them: whenever there is fear, you get the wrong numbers. Culture first, then.

@chapter The arithmetic of twice
@art two-groups
Now, what does twice as likely actually mean? The lesson's lab answers that with invented teams. It takes two groups, how many teams each has, and how many met a goal, and prints three different comparisons.

@math heading="40 invented teams" items="high: 12 of 20 = 60%@twelve of twenty;;low: 6 of 20 = 30%@six of twenty;;ratio: 60 / 30 = 2.00@twice as likely;;difference: 60 − 30 = 30 points@thirty points"
Take forty invented teams, twenty high and twenty low. Twelve of twenty high performers met a goal, sixty percent. Six of twenty low performers did, thirty percent. Sixty over thirty is two, so twice as likely. The difference is thirty points.

@art odds-scale
People often swap in a third comparison, the odds. Odds weigh the teams that met the goal against the ones that didn't, in the same group.

@math heading="Odds are something else" items="odds high: 12 to 8 = 3/2@twelve to eight;;odds low: 6 to 14 = 3/7@six to fourteen;;odds ratio: (3/2) ÷ (3/7) = 7/2 = 3.50@three and a half"
The odds ratio is a different number. The high group's odds are twelve to eight, three halves. The low group's are six to fourteen, three sevenths. Divide them and you get seven halves, three and a half. Twice as likely is the ratio of shares, not this.

@math heading="A rarer goal" items="high: 4 of 20 = 20%@Four of the high;;low: 2 of 20 = 10%@two of the low;;ratio 2.00 · difference 10 points · odds ratio 2.25@still two"
Change the goal. Four of the high group and two of the low met it, twenty percent and ten percent. The ratio is still two. But the difference is ten points, not thirty, and the odds ratio is two point two five.

@art same-ratio
So twice as likely fits a big difference or a small one, depending on how common the outcome is. The book's sentence gives no underlying shares, so it can't tell you which.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Twice as likely means twice the result@The first|Using the measures to rank and reward@The second|Treating the arrows as proof of cause@And the third"
The first slip is reading twice as likely as twice the result. The second is using the measures to rank and reward teams. And the third is treating the arrows as proof of cause. The ratio compares groups drawn by clustering. It doesn't show that moving a team would change its organization's results.
