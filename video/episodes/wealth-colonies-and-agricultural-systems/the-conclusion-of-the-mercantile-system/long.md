@chapter The question
@title text="The Conclusion of the Mercantile System" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 8" art=thumb
The mercantile system wanted to export more and import less. So why did it encourage importing some goods, and ban exporting others? Smith's answer leads to the sharpest maxim in Book Four.

@words text="Cheap materials, dear exports"
The puzzle has a simple answer. The system discouraged exporting the materials of manufacture, and encouraged importing them, so that home manufacturers could buy cheap and sell dear. The aim was the same favourable balance.

@chapter Linen yarn
@art yarn-and-cloth
Take linen yarn. Manufacturers got the duty on foreign yarn cut and then abolished, while getting a bounty on exporting linen. More than four fifths of the labour in linen is in the yarn. But the spinners were poor women, scattered about the country, without support or protection. The masters profit from the weavers, so they want yarn cheap.

@quote text="It is the industry which is carried on for the benefit of the rich and the powerful, that is principally encouraged by our mercantile system." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 8"
The lesson is general. It is the industry which is carried on for the benefit of the rich and the powerful, that is principally encouraged by our mercantile system. The work done for the poor is too often neglected or oppressed.

@chapter Laws written in blood
@art sheep-and-wool
The wool laws are where Smith's anger shows. The woollen manufacturers persuaded parliament that the nation's prosperity depended on their business. They got a monopoly against consumers, and another against wool growers, by banning the export of sheep and wool. Like the laws of Draco, he says, these laws may be said to be all written in blood.

@math heading="Three shillings a pound · four or five times the value" items="3s = 36d@thirty six pence;;36 / 5 = 7.2d@five;;36 / 4 = 9d@four"
Under Elizabeth, exporting sheep could cost a man his left hand. He hopes those statutes were never executed. The later penalty was three shillings for every pound of wool, about four or five times its value. Three shillings is thirty six pence. Divided by five, seven point two pence. By four, nine. So a pound of wool was worth between those.

@chapter What it actually did
@art fleece-and-carcase
Then Smith asks what the ban actually did. The claim that English wool was needed for fine cloth was perfectly false. The ban depressed wool prices, but probably not the quantity, because a sheep farmer expects his profit more from the carcase than the fleece. So the growers were hurt less than you'd think.

@words text="A considerable tax, not a prohibition"
That's no excuse. It won't justify an absolute prohibition, he says, but it will justify a considerable tax on exporting wool. To hurt one order of citizens, just to promote another, is contrary to the justice and equality of treatment the sovereign owes them all. And the ban helps nobody but the smuggler.

@art scattered-and-gathered
Why do the weak lose? Graziers, dispersed through every corner of the country, can hardly combine. Manufacturers, collected in great cities, easily can. Later economists, like Mancur Olson, built on that observation about small and large groups.

@chapter The maxim
@quote text="Consumption is the sole end and purpose of all production" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 8"
Then the maxim. Consumption is the sole end and purpose of all production, and the producer's interest should be attended to only so far as it serves the consumer's. It's so self evident, he says, it would be absurd to prove it. Yet the mercantile system almost constantly sacrifices the consumer to the producer.

@math heading="A bounty of £1 on 10,000 exported" items="treasury: 10,000 · 1 = 10,000@treasury;;home buyers: 40,000 · 1/2 = 20,000@home buyers;;total: 30,000 · £3 a unit exported@total"
With a bounty, the consumer pays twice. Here are figures written for the lesson. The treasury pays a pound on each of ten thousand units exported, ten thousand. The price at home rises by half a pound on forty thousand units, so home buyers pay twenty thousand more. The total is thirty thousand, three pounds for each unit exported.

@art two-payments
Smith calls the second payment the still greater tax. Here it is. But let the price rise only an eighth, and home buyers pay five thousand, less than the treasury. So that claim is about facts, not arithmetic.

@chapter The architects
@art empire-of-customers
His largest case is the empire itself, built to raise a nation of customers. For it, he says, more than two hundred millions were spent in two wars, and a new debt of more than a hundred and seventy millions contracted. Who contrived the system? Not the consumers, but the producers, and above all the merchants and manufacturers.
