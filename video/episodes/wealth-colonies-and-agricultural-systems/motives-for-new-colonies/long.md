@chapter The question
@title text="The Motives for New Colonies" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 7, Part 1" art=thumb
Why did Europeans settle America at all? You might expect a simple answer. Smith gives an uncomfortable one, and to see it, he starts with older colonies whose reasons were plain.

@quote text="The establishment of the European colonies in America and the West Indies arose from no necessity; and though the utility which has resulted from them has been very great, it is not altogether so clear and evident." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 7, Part 1"
The establishment of the European colonies in America and the West Indies arose from no necessity; and though the utility which has resulted from them has been very great, it is not altogether so clear and evident.

@chapter Greek and Roman colonies
@art greek-colony
A Greek state had a very small territory. When its people outgrew it, some went out to a new home. The mother city treated the colony as an emancipated child. It made its own laws and its own peace and war. Nothing could be more plain and distinct than the interest behind it.

@art roman-garrison
Rome's reason was land. The rich had gathered up the estates, and the poor citizen had neither land nor work, since slaves did it. So the answer was to send a colony to a conquered province of Italy. It could never form an independent state, and often served as a sort of garrison.

@chapter A voyage for the Indies
@art westward-voyage
The American case began elsewhere. The Venetians had a rich spice trade with the East, and the Portuguese were sailing round Africa toward India. Columbus reasoned that the longer the way by the east, the shorter it would be by the west. A project of commerce to the East Indies gave occasion to the discovery of the West.

@words text="The sole motive: the hope of gold"
He didn't find what he'd gone for. To make the new lands look important to the court of Spain, there was only gold. So the council of Castile decided to take possession of lands whose people couldn't defend themselves. Conversion, Smith says, sanctified the injustice, but the hope of gold was the sole motive.

@art crown-share
Columbus proposed that half of all the gold should belong to the crown. While it came from plundering the natives, that could be paid. In six or eight years they were stripped of all they had, and the gold had to be dug.

@math heading="The crown's share of 1,000 ounces of gold" items="½ → 500@a half;;⅓ → 333⅓@a third;;⅕ → 200@a fifth;;⅒ → 100@a tenth;;1/20 → 50@a twentieth"
So the tax fell from a half to a third, a fifth, a tenth, and at last a twentieth. On a thousand ounces, from five hundred down to fifty.

@chapter The lottery
@art lottery-tickets
Then the verdict. Of all expensive and uncertain projects, none is perhaps more perfectly ruinous than the search for new silver and gold mines. It is, perhaps, the most disadvantageous lottery in the world. The prizes are few, the blanks many, and the common price of a ticket is the whole fortune of a very rich man.

@math heading="A thousand tickets, ten prizes" items="chance: 10 / 1,000 = 1 in 100@chance;;prizes: 10 · 2,000 · 20 = 400,000@prizes;;tickets: 1,000 · 500 = 500,000@tickets;;short by 100,000@short"
The lesson makes it a sum, with figures written for the lesson. A thousand adventurers each lay out five hundred pounds. Ten strike a vein worth two thousand a year for twenty years. The chance is one in a hundred. The prizes come to four hundred thousand, the tickets to five hundred thousand. So the lottery is short by a hundred thousand.

@art prizes-and-tickets
Yet each winner takes forty thousand over twenty years, eighty times his ticket. Both are true at once. A winner would need twenty five hundred a year for the whole lottery to break even. And cutting the crown's share to a twentieth still leaves it a hundred and twenty thousand short. The vein, not the tax, decides it.

@chapter Fortune's exception
@words text="A course of accidents which no human wisdom could foresee"
Smith is fair here. In Mexico and Peru, fortune gave the Spaniards something not unlike the profusion they sought, by a course of accidents which no human wisdom could foresee. Others were less lucky. Brazil found no mine for over a century, and the English, French and Dutch colonies none worth working.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading it as no mine ever paid@The first|Thinking a lower crown share fixes it@The second|Taking the Greek colony as the model@And the third"
The first slip is reading the verdict as no mine ever paid. Smith says Mexico and Peru did, by chance. The second is thinking a lower crown share would repay the tickets. And the third is taking the Greek colony as the model for the American one. The contrast is his point.
