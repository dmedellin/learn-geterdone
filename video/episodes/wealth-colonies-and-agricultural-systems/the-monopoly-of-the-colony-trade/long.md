@chapter The question
@title text="The Monopoly of the Colony Trade" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 7, Part 3" art=thumb
What did Europe gain from America? Smith's answer turns on one distinction. The trade with the colonies is one thing. The monopoly of that trade is another, and he judges them in opposite ways.

@art new-equivalents
First, Europe as one great country gained enjoyments and industry. Even a Hungarian who never sent a cargo to America, but buys sugar and tobacco that came from it, has a new market for his own produce. Those goods are new values, new equivalents to exchange for it.

@art dead-weight
Each mother country's exclusive trade works against that. It's a dead weight upon one of the great springs that puts into motion much of the business of mankind. And the colonies, he says, gave no military force and, except for Spain and Portugal, no revenue. They were a source of expense. That leaves only the monopoly.

@chapter Absolute and relative
@words text="An absolute advantage lost for a relative one"
England bought tobacco cheaper than France could, because it could go nowhere else. But with free trade the colonies would have grown more, and tobacco might be cheaper to England now too. She might perhaps have gained an absolute advantage, but she'd certainly have lost a relative one.

@art capital-drawn-off
Here's how. Shutting out foreign capital meant English capital had to supply the whole colony trade, and couldn't. So its profit rose above the ordinary level, and drew capital out of other trades. That raised profit everywhere in Britain, making her sell dearer in trades where she had no monopoly.

@chapter A thousand pounds
@quote text="A capital of a thousand pounds, for example, employed in a foreign trade of consumption, of which the returns are made regularly once in the year, can keep in constant employment, in the country to which it belongs, a quantity of productive labour, equal to what a thousand pounds can maintain there for a year." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 7, Part 3"
Now the heart of it. An employment's advantage to its country is the labour it keeps at work there, and that depends on how often the capital comes home. A capital of a thousand pounds, returned once a year, keeps at work what a thousand pounds can maintain for a year.

@math heading="Labour kept at work in a year" items="once a year: 1,000 · 1 = 1,000@once a year;;twice a year: 1,000 · 2 = 2,000@twice;;once in five years: 1,000 · 1/5 = 200@five years;;2,000 / 200 = 10@ten times"
Returned once a year, a thousand pounds' worth. Twice a year, two thousand. But the colonies were always short of capital and in debt, so a capital might come home only once in five years. Then it keeps at work only what two hundred pounds can maintain. The fastest trade employs ten times what the slowest does.

@art returns-a-year
And here's the sentence that carries the point. In a trade with very distant returns, the merchant's profit may be as great or greater than in a near one. But the advantage to the country is less. Profit to a merchant isn't the gain to a nation.

@chapter Round-about tobacco
@art round-about-tobacco
It compounds. Maryland and Virginia send upwards of ninety six thousand hogsheads of tobacco to Britain, which consumes about fourteen thousand. The other eighty two thousand go on to France, Holland and the Baltic. That round about capital isn't likely to come home in less than four or five years.

@chapter In spite of the monopoly
@words text="Beneficial, in spite of the monopoly"
Then Smith turns. The trade is always and necessarily beneficial, the monopoly always and necessarily hurtful. But the trade is so beneficial that even under the monopoly, it's still, on the whole, greatly beneficial. If it did good, it's not by means of the monopoly, but in spite of it.

@art shopkeepers-empire
To found a great empire just to raise up a people of customers may look fit only for a nation of shopkeepers. It is, Smith says, altogether unfit for a nation of shopkeepers, but extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers. That's a criticism, not a compliment.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading him as calling the trade a loss@The first|Confusing profit with employment@The second|Taking nation of shopkeepers as praise@And the third"
The first slip is reading Smith as calling the colony trade a loss. The second is confusing a merchant's profit with the labour a capital keeps at work. And the third is taking nation of shopkeepers as praise. Historians still argue about how much the Navigation Acts actually cost the colonies.
