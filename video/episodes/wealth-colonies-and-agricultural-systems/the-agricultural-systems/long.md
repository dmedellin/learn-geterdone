@chapter The question
@title text="The Agricultural Systems" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 9" art=thumb
After the mercantile system, Smith takes on its opposite: a French system that says only land produces wealth. He thinks it's wrong in one capital way, and yet calls it the nearest approximation to the truth yet published.

@words text="Never done, and probably never will do, any harm"
It had never been adopted by any nation. It existed only in the speculations of a few learned men in France. A system, he says, which never has done, and probably never will do, any harm in any part of the world.

@art bent-rod
It began as a reaction. Colbert, Louis the Fourteenth's minister, favoured the towns and banned exporting corn altogether. The economists answered with a proverb. If the rod be bent too much one way, to make it straight you must bend it as much the other.

@chapter Three classes
@art three-classes
The system has three classes. Proprietors, who improve the land. Cultivators, honoured with the name of the productive class. And artificers, manufacturers and merchants, degraded with the humiliating name of the barren or unproductive class.

@math heading="Net product = produce − consumption − advances" items="cultivators: 1,000 − 400 − 400 = 200@cultivators;;artificers: 400 − 200 − 200 = 0@artificers"
Why barren? A farm's produce, after it replaces costs and profit, leaves a surplus, the landlord's rent. Take figures written for the lesson. Cultivators produce a thousand, consume four hundred and advance four hundred, leaving two hundred. Artificers produce four hundred, consume two hundred and advance two hundred, leaving nothing.

@art net-product
On the system's terms, only the cultivators show a net product. A manufacturer's stock just continues its own value. And merchant nations like Holland, it says, can grow rich only by parsimony and privation. But the barren class is still greatly useful, and the remedy is perfect liberty.

@chapter The lace-maker
@art lace-and-flax
Smith gives the system's best illustration, the lace maker. He can turn a pennyworth of flax into lace worth thirty pounds. Thirty times two hundred and forty pence is seven thousand two hundred times its value. But the work takes two years, and the thirty pounds only repays two years of his subsistence. What he makes, he eats.

@chapter Smith's answer
@words text="Natural effort to better one's condition"
Smith's first reply is to Quesnay the physician. Bodies stay healthy on many diets, not just a precise regimen. And every man's natural effort to better his condition can correct much bad policy. If a nation needed perfect liberty and justice to prosper, no nation ever could have.

@quote text="We should not call a marriage barren or unproductive, though it produced only a son and a daughter, to replace the father and mother." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 9"
Then the capital error, calling the artificers barren. We should not call a marriage barren or unproductive, though it produced only a son and a daughter, to replace the father and mother. Farmers may be more productive, like three children against two. That doesn't make the others barren.

@art six-months
His sharpest point is about time. An artificer who, in the six months after harvest, does ten pounds of work and eats ten pounds of corn has still added ten pounds. The value consumed and produced is twenty pounds, not ten. And a trading country can buy more food than its land grows, as Holland does.

@words text="Perhaps the nearest approximation to the truth"
Still, his verdict is generous. With all its imperfections, the system is perhaps the nearest approximation to the truth yet published on political economy. It treats wealth as the goods each year's labour reproduces, and perfect liberty as the way to make them greatest.

@chapter Natural liberty
@art three-duties
Then he turns on both systems. Take away every preference and restraint, and the obvious and simple system of natural liberty establishes itself. Every man, within the laws of justice, is free to pursue his own interest his own way. The sovereign keeps three duties: defence, justice, and certain public works no individual would find it profitable to support.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking barren to mean useless@The first|Thinking Smith denies land yields more@The second|Taking the lab's classes for Quesnay's table@And the third"
The first slip is taking barren to mean useless. It's a claim about surplus. The second is thinking Smith denies land yields more. He grants it. And the third is taking the lab's figures for Quesnay's own table, which isn't printed in this edition.
