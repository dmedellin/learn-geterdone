@chapter The question
@title text="The Prosperity of New Colonies" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 7, Part 2" art=thumb
The British colonies in North America grew faster than almost any society on record. Smith wants to know why, and his answer has a condition built into its very first sentence.

@quote text="The colony of a civilized nation which takes possession either of a waste country, or of one so thinly inhabited that the natives easily give place to the new settlers, advances more rapidly to wealth and greatness than any other human society." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 7, Part 2"
The colony of a civilized nation which takes possession either of a waste country, or of one so thinly inhabited that the natives easily give place to the new settlers, advances more rapidly to wealth and greatness than any other human society.

@words text="Land for the taking"
Notice the condition. Everything depends on land for the taking, and Smith's own pages show what the taking meant, from the plunder of St Domingo to the cruel destruction of the natives in Mexico and Peru. His claim that those lands are more populous now is among the most disputed in the chapter.

@chapter Cheap land, dear labour
@art more-land
Now the mechanism. A colonist gets more land than he can cultivate, and pays no rent and scarce any taxes. But he can seldom make it produce a tenth of what it could. So he's eager to collect labourers from all quarters, and to reward them with the most liberal wages.

@art labourer-to-landlord
Those wages, joined to cheap land, soon make the labourers leave him to become landlords themselves. High wages encourage marriage, the children are well fed, and when they're grown, the value of their labour greatly overpays their maintenance. In old countries, rent and profit eat up wages. Here, the two upper orders must treat the labourer better.

@chapter Doubling
@math heading="Three millions, doubling" items="every 25 years: 100 / 25 = 4 doublings@twenty five;;2 · 2 · 2 · 2 = 16 → 48,000,000@sixteen;;every 20 years: 5 doublings → 96,000,000@twenty years"
In Book One, Smith says Europe isn't supposed to double in less than five hundred years, but the colonies double in twenty or twenty five. Take his three millions, doubling every twenty five years for a century. That's four doublings, a factor of sixteen, forty eight million. Double every twenty years, and it's five doublings, ninety six million.

@art doubling-bars
That's arithmetic, not a forecast. Malthus later took this American rate as his model of what people do when food is unlimited. Whether any population keeps it up is a question about land and food, which the rate itself can't answer.

@chapter Why the English colonies?
@list heading="Four heads" items="Engrossing of land restrained@engrossing|Estates divided by inheritance@inheritance|Moderate taxes@taxes|A wider market for the surplus@market"
Why the English colonies, when Spain and Portugal had more land? Smith gives four heads. Engrossing of land was restrained. Inheritance divided estates, with no primogeniture in Pennsylvania. Taxes were moderate. And the surplus was allowed a wider market.

@art small-government
On taxes, Smith adds up the civil establishments of eleven provinces, about sixty four thousand seven hundred pounds a year. An ever memorable example, he says, at how small an expense three millions of people may be well governed. But defence, the biggest cost, fell on the mother country.

@chapter Trade and its restraints
@art enumerated-goods
England left the trade open to all her merchants, and colonists could sell grain, lumber, fish and rum anywhere. But enumerated goods, like sugar, tobacco and cotton, could go only to Britain. And making more advanced goods was prohibited.

@words text="A manifest violation of the most sacred rights of mankind"
Smith calls those prohibitions a manifest violation of the most sacred rights of mankind. Then he qualifies it. With land so cheap and labour so dear, the colonies could import such goods more cheaply anyway, so for now they were only impertinent badges of slavery. In a more advanced state, they might be really oppressive.

@chapter Slavery in the sugar colonies
@art two-regimes
In all European colonies, sugar was grown by enslaved Africans. Smith argues that a slave's condition depends on government. In a free colony, the master may sit in the assembly, so a magistrate dares protect a slave only with great caution. He concludes their condition was better under arbitrary government. He doesn't argue here for abolition, and the lesson reports him exactly.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading waste country as the whole story@The first|Reading the slavery passage as a defence@The second|Treating a doubling rate as a forecast@And the third"
The first slip is reading waste country as the whole story. The second is reading his remark on slavery as a defence of it. He calls it an unfortunate law, and in Book Three says slave work is in the end the dearest of any. And the third is treating a doubling rate as a forecast.
