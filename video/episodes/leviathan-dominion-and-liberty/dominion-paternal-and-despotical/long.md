@chapter A strange claim
@title text="Dominion Paternal and Despotical" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 20" art=thumb
A man has been beaten in war, and his life is in the winner's hands. Hobbes says the winner's right over him doesn't come from winning. It comes from something the beaten man does afterwards.

@quote text="It is not therefore the Victory, that giveth the right of Dominion over the Vanquished, but his own Covenant." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 20"
It is not the victory that gives the right of dominion over the vanquished, but his own covenant. For Hobbes, every obligation comes from an act of the person obliged, and being overpowered isn't an act.

@chapter Two ways to make a commonwealth
@art two-fears
By institution, a multitude agree among themselves to authorise a sovereign. By acquisition, the power is taken by force. They differ only in this. In the first, men fear one another. In the second, they fear the man they submit to. In both cases they do it for fear.

@words text="Does fear void a promise?" sub="then no one, anywhere, could be obliged"
So what about the objection that a promise squeezed out by fear is void? Hobbes says that if it were, no one in any commonwealth could be obliged, since everyone obeys for fear of the law. A promise to do what the law forbids doesn't bind, but because you had no right in the thing, not because you were afraid.

@chapter The family
@art parent-and-child
Then the family. A parent's dominion over a child doesn't come from begetting it. It comes from the child's consent, given outright or shown by other signs. That's aimed at royalists like Sir Robert Filmer, who rooted a king's power in Adam's fatherhood. Hobbes argues from covenant instead.

@list heading="Whose is the child?" items="Not both: no man can obey two masters@two masters|Not the father by sex@more excellent sex|No contract: the mother@no contract|Whoever saves it@finds and nourishes"
Two parents beget a child, but no man can obey two masters. Those who give it to the father as the more excellent sex misreckon. In a commonwealth, civil law decides, usually for the father. But in the state of nature, with no contract, the dominion is the mother's. And if someone else finds and nourishes the child, it's his.

@chapter Captive or servant
@art captive-in-bonds
That last rule is the hinge. Every man is supposed to promise obedience to whoever has the power to save or destroy him. Now Hobbes applies it to the vanquished. A captive, kept in prison or bonds, has no obligation at all. He may break out, and even kill his master, justly.

@art trusted-servant
A servant is different. He's been taken, then allowed his bodily liberty, and trusted on his promise not to run or do violence. He's a servant only once he's covenanted, and not before. Asking quarter only defers your life. Notice who goes first: the victor trusts, and then the vanquished owes service.

@chapter The lab's sum
@words text="Victor: −5, +60 · Vanquished: −20, +100" sub="figures invented for the lesson"
The lab turns this into a sum, with figures the lesson invents. The victor gives up five to spare and keep the man, and gains sixty in service. The vanquished gains his life and liberty, worth a hundred, and serving costs him twenty.

@math heading="Even odds: fine 100, applied half the time" items="keep: 100 − 20 = 80@keeping is;;break: 100 − 100/2 = 50@breaking is;;80 > 50, so he keeps@he keeps;;victor: 60 − 5 = 55 > 0@fifty five"
Keeping is a hundred minus twenty, eighty. Breaking is a hundred minus half the fine of a hundred, fifty. Eighty beats fifty, so he keeps. And the victor who went first comes out at sixty minus five, fifty five, so going first was reasonable.

@math heading="When the sword is weak" items="no fine: break = 100 > 80@no fine;;odds 1 in 10: break = 90 > 80@one time in ten;;least fine: 20 ÷ 1/10 = 200@two hundred"
With no fine at all, breaking pays a hundred, and the victor loses his five. Apply the fine one time in ten, and breaking is worth ninety, so he breaks again. The least fine that holds him then is twenty divided by a tenth, two hundred.

@art weak-sword
Two hundred is twice the fine in force. That's what the sum shows: why Hobbes says trust is needed, and a sword behind it.

@chapter Critics and conclusion
@art scales-of-consent
Locke disagreed. He argued that a conqueror in an unjust war gets no right over those he beats, because a power to kill isn't a right to rule, and rights come from free consent. Hobbes's reply is already on the page: if fear voided consent, there'd be no obligation anywhere. The lab can't settle that.

@words text="Absolute over both alike" sub="or else there is no sovereignty at all"
Hobbes's conclusion is that a master's and a parent's rights are the very same as a sovereign's by institution. A monarch who asks more of a conquered nation is ignorant of sovereignty, since he's absolute over both alike, or else there's no sovereignty at all.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The victory gives the right@The first|Fear voids a covenant@The second|Begetting gives dominion@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the victory gives the right. Without a covenant, he's only a captive. The second is thinking a covenant made from fear is void. And the third is thinking a father rules because he begat the child.
