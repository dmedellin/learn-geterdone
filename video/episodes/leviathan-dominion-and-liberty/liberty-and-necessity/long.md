@chapter What liberty is
@title text="Liberty and Necessity" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 21" art=thumb
Think of water held in a channel, or a dog tied on a lead. The water isn't at liberty to spread out, and the dog isn't at liberty to run. Hobbes says that's the whole idea of liberty, and it works the same for people.

@quote text="LIBERTY, or FREEDOME, signifieth (properly) the absence of Opposition; (by Opposition, I mean externall Impediments of motion;)" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 21"
Liberty, or freedom, signifies properly the absence of opposition, and by opposition he means external impediments of motion. A free man is one who isn't hindered from doing what he has a will to, in the things he's able to do.

@art stone-and-channel
The impediment has to come from outside. A stone lying still, or a man kept in bed by sickness, lacks the power to move, not the liberty. And free will, he says, tells you about the man, who finds no stop in doing what he wills. It says nothing about a liberty of the will itself.

@chapter Two claims that made enemies
@art goods-overboard
First, fear and liberty are consistent. A man who throws his goods into the sea to save the ship does it very willingly, and could refuse. So does a debtor who pays for fear of prison. Everything men do in a commonwealth for fear of the law, they had liberty to leave undone.

@art chain-of-causes
Second, liberty and necessity are consistent. Every act of will has a cause, and that cause another, in a chain whose first link is in the hand of God. Water both flows down its channel freely and must.

@words text="Hobbes vs Bramhall" sub="Paris, mid-1640s"
That second claim had a history. In Paris in the mid sixteen forties, Hobbes debated free will with John Bramhall, the exiled Bishop of Derry. Bramhall held that if every act is necessary, praise, blame and just punishment fall apart. Later philosophers call Hobbes's view compatibilism, and it's never lacked opponents.

@chapter Artificial chains
@art artificial-chains
Now the political turn. Men made an artificial man, the commonwealth, and with it artificial chains called civil laws, fixed at one end to the sovereign's lips and at the other to their own ears. They're weak in themselves. They hold by the danger of breaking them, not by the difficulty.

@list heading="Where the law is silent" items="Buy, sell and contract@buy and sell|Choose abode, diet and trade@choose where|Bring up your children@bring up"
No commonwealth has rules for every action. So wherever the laws are silent, you're free to do what your own reason suggests. His list is ordinary. To buy and sell and contract with one another. To choose where you live, what you eat, and your trade. And to bring up your children as you think fit.

@chapter Counting the free part
@art sorting-actions
The lab sorts eight actions, seven from Hobbes's examples plus paying tax, into commanded, forbidden and free. In the first preset, a tax law commands paying tax, and a marriage law forbids many wives.

@math heading="Eight actions, two laws" items="commanded: pay tax = 1@one is commanded;;forbidden: many wives = 1@one forbidden;;free: 8 − 1 − 1 = 6@eight minus one;;free share: 6/8 = 75%@seventy five"
One is commanded, one forbidden. Free is eight minus one minus one, which is six. Six out of eight is seventy five percent.

@math heading="Add the statute" items="forbidden: many wives, forcible entry = 2@forcible entry;;free: 8 − 1 − 2 = 5@five;;5/8 = 62.5%@sixty two point five"
Hobbes notes that a man in England could once enter his own land by force, until a statute took that away. Add it, and forcible entry is forbidden too. Now five are free, sixty two point five percent. Drop the marriage law instead, and six are free again, but not the same six.

@chapter What liberty doesn't do
@words text="No injury to the author" sub="David and Uriah · Athens and the oyster-shell"
This liberty doesn't limit the sovereign's power of life and death. Every subject is author of what the sovereign does, so nothing he does to you is an injury to you. David killing Uriah wronged God, Hobbes says, not Uriah. And Athens had the right to banish men by oyster shell.

@art lucca-towers
And the liberty praised by the Greeks and Romans? That was the liberty of the commonwealth, not of particular men. Lucca writes liberty on its towers, but a man there has no more immunity from service than one in Constantinople. Monarchy or popular state, the freedom is the same.

@words text="Laws you make vs a master's will" sub="the republican objection"
Republicans like James Harrington argued that a city ruled by laws its citizens make is free in a way one ruled by a master's will isn't. Hobbes answers with his definition: both meet the same sort of chain. The lab can count what's left free. It can't say whether who makes the chains should matter.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Fear takes liberty away@The first|Liberty and necessity can't meet@The second|Rome's liberty was each man's@And the third"
The first slip is thinking fear takes liberty away. A threat leaves you free to refuse. The second is thinking an act can't be both free and necessary. And the third is reading the liberty of the ancients as each man's liberty.
