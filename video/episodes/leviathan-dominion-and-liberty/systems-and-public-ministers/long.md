@chapter A council borrows
@title text="Systems Subject and Public Ministers" sub="Leviathan, Chapters 22 and 23" art=thumb
A town council votes to borrow money for a new market. Two members voted against it, one was away, and now the lender wants repayment. Who owes? Hobbes's answer is a rule about authorship from Chapter sixteen: you're bound by what you've authorised.

@chapter The muscles of the body
@art muscles-of-the-state
The commonwealth is an artificial man, and Chapter twenty two is about its systems, which resemble the muscles of a natural body. A system is any number of men joined in one interest or one business.

@list heading="Sorting systems" items="Regular or irregular@regular|Political or private@political|Lawful or unlawful@lawful"
A system is regular if one man or assembly represents it, and irregular otherwise. The only absolute regular system is a commonwealth. The rest are subordinate: political, made by the sovereign's authority, or private, made by subjects. And private ones are lawful if the commonwealth allows them.

@art letters-patent
Here's the principle that makes the rest work. A body politic's representative is always limited, by its letters from the sovereign and by the law. Power unlimited is absolute sovereignty, and giving that to a subordinate body would divide the dominion. One man acting beyond his letters represents no one but himself.

@chapter Votes and liability
@quote text="all they, and onely they are responsible for the debt, that gave their votes to the borrowing of it" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 22"
When the representative is an assembly and the debt is to a stranger, all they, and only they, are responsible for the debt that gave their votes to the borrowing of it. Those who voted against, and those absent, are innocent. And each voter is answerable for the whole debt, until one pays.

@art assembly-votes
So the council's answer is plain. The two dissenters and the absentee owe nothing. Each of the others is liable for all of it.

@chapter Counting the lab
@math heading="Six members, a loan of 120" items="yes 4, no 2@four vote yes;;4 > 2: passes by 2@passes;;answerable: Ashe, Bell, Cole, Dunn@the four yes;;excused: Eyre, Ford@excused;;each owes 120; shared equally, 120 ÷ 4 = 30@thirty"
Six members vote on a loan of a hundred and twenty from a stranger. Four vote yes and two no, so it passes by two. The four yes voters are answerable, and the two others excused. Each of the four owes the lender the whole hundred and twenty. Shared equally, that's thirty each, which is the lesson's assumption, not Hobbes's.

@math heading="Other presets" items="3 yes, 3 no: mute, no one liable@three and three;;7 members: 3 yes, 2 no, 2 abstain: passes, 3 answerable@two abstain;;absolute rule: 3 of 7 is not more than half: fails@more than half"
Make it three and three, and the assembly is mute. Nothing is decreed, and nobody's liable. With seven members, three yes, two no and two abstain, it passes by the greater number of voices, and three are answerable. On the absolute rule you need more than half of all seven, and three isn't that, so it fails.

@art protest-registered
Hobbes draws the working conclusion. In a subordinate body, it's sometimes expedient to protest openly and have your dissent registered, or you may end up paying for what others did. In a sovereign assembly that liberty is gone, because protesting there denies its sovereignty.

@chapter Merchants, families and factions
@words text="A double monopoly" sub="sole buyers and sole sellers"
Then the variety of bodies. A corporation of merchants is a double monopoly, sole buyers abroad and sole sellers at home. And here a debt to a stranger falls on every member, for the whole, since a stranger takes no notice of their private laws.

@art faction-and-sword
Families are lawful private bodies. Corporations of beggars and thieves are unlawful. Leagues between commonwealths are lawful, but leagues of subjects go by the name of factions, a taking of the sword out of the sovereign's hand.

@chapter Public ministers
@words text="Represents the person of the commonwealth" sub="not ushers, stewards or counsellors"
Chapter twenty three turns to the organs. A public minister is employed by the sovereign with authority to represent the person of the commonwealth: a viceroy, a treasurer, a judge. Ushers and stewards serve the sovereign's natural person, so they aren't.

@art ministers-as-organs
Hobbes likens most ministers to parts of the body: the nerves, the hands, the organs of voice. A judge gives the sovereign's sentence. But a counsellor who only advises isn't a public person, because no one can represent the sovereign to himself, in his own presence.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A decree binds every member@The first|A body's power equals the sovereign's@The second|Every servant is a public minister@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a decree binds every member. The dissenters owe nothing. The second is thinking a body's power is as wide as the sovereign's. And the third is counting every servant of the sovereign as a public minister.
