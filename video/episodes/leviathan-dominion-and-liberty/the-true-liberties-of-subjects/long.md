@chapter A limit on an absolute power
@title text="The True Liberty of Subjects" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 21" art=thumb
Hobbes's sovereign is absolute. So how could there be anything a subject may refuse? He doesn't reach for a bill of rights. He asks what rights we passed away when we made the commonwealth.

@words text="Obligation and liberty lie in the act of submission"
No one is bound except by an act of his own. So the limit has to be read from the words of the submission, or from its end. Whatever the covenant never reached, you still have.

@chapter What no covenant could transfer
@quote text="yet hath that man the Liberty to disobey." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 21"
Chapter fourteen showed that a covenant not to defend your own body is void. So if the sovereign commands a man, even justly condemned, to kill or wound himself, not to resist his attackers, or to go without food, air or medicine, yet hath that man the liberty to disobey.

@art self-defence-shield
A second follows. If you're questioned about your own crime, you're not bound to confess it without assurance of pardon. And Hobbes reads the words closely. Saying kill me if you please isn't saying I will kill myself. No one is bound by those words to kill himself or anyone else.

@chapter The end of the commonwealth
@art soldier-and-substitute
Then the qualification. A duty to do a dangerous office depends on the end of the submission. If refusing would defeat the end the sovereignty was made for, there's no liberty to refuse. Otherwise there is. A soldier may often refuse without injustice, say by sending a sufficient substitute.

@words text="Fear, not treachery" sub="dishonourable, but not unjust"
Allowance is made for natural timorousness. Armies run away, and when it's fear rather than treachery, it's dishonourable, not unjust. But a man who enrols, or takes the pay, takes away that excuse. And when the whole commonwealth is in danger, everyone able to bear arms is obliged.

@art rebels-together
Two more cases sharpen the line. You may not resist the commonwealth's sword in defence of another, guilty or innocent. But men who have already rebelled may join to defend their own lives, until the sovereign offers pardon, which takes away their plea.

@chapter The lab
@list heading="Five actions" items="Pay tax@paying tax|Serve in the war@serving in the war|Kill oneself@killing oneself|Confess one's own crime@confessing|Buy and sell@buying and selling"
The lab takes five actions: paying tax, serving in the war, killing oneself, confessing one's own crime, and buying and selling. In the first preset, four laws command the first four.

@math heading="Four commands, two liberties" items="commanded: 4 of 5@four of the five;;may still be refused: kill self, confess = 2@may still refuse;;free by silence: buy and sell = 1 of 5 = 20%@twenty percent"
That's four of the five commanded. Two of those, killing oneself and confessing, are inalienable, so a subject may still refuse them. Buying and selling is left alone, so it's the only free action: one of five, twenty percent.

@words text="Ordinary laws: 3 of 5 free = 60%" sub="the muster law is not marked"
In the second preset only the tax and muster laws are in force. No command touches an inalienable liberty. Killing oneself and confessing are then simply free, so three of five actions are free, sixty percent.

@art muster-drum
Why isn't the muster law marked inalienable? Because Hobbes says the soldier may refuse in some cases, with a substitute, and not in others, once he's enrolled. Which case holds is a judgement, and the lab doesn't make it.

@chapter As long as protection lasts
@quote text="The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign, is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 21"
Then the principle that was dangerous to say in sixteen fifty one. The obligation of subjects lasts as long, and no longer, than the power by which the sovereign can protect them. 

@art protection-umbrella
The end of obedience is protection, and wherever a man sees it, in his own sword or another's, nature applies his obedience to it. A banished man, or a prisoner given his life on condition of becoming the captor's subject, is freed from the old sovereign.

@art soul-departing
Hobbes calls sovereignty the soul of the commonwealth, and once it departs from the body, the members no longer take their motion from it. A royalist could be unsettled by that. A supporter of Parliament could read it as a reason to submit to the power that beat the King.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A subject must obey every command@The first|Liberties limit the sovereign's right@The second|The soldier may always refuse war@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a subject must obey every command. The second is reading these liberties as limits on the sovereign's right. They're places the covenant never reached, and he may still punish the refusal. And the third is thinking the soldier may always refuse.
