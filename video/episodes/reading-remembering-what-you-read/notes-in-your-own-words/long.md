@chapter The question
@title text="Notes in Your Own Words" sub="Reading Faster · Remembering What You Read" art=thumb
Highlighting a text feels like studying. So does writing a summary. But do they help you remember? It depends on one thing: whether the page is open while you do it.

@chapter The drill
@art autumn-leaf
The drill times one reading of Where Autumn Colours Come From, three hundred and seventy seven words, at your usual pace. When you press Done, the passage disappears.

@art three-sentences
Then write a summary of three sentences in your own words. Tick the passage's ten idea units that your summary holds, and the kit counts the words you wrote. That gives two figures: coverage, the units over ten, and cost, the words written per unit.

@chapter Tight and copied
@math heading="Two summaries" items="tight: 40 / 6 ≈ 6.7 words per unit, 6/10 = 60%@forty words;;copied: 90 / 4 = 22.5 words per unit, 4/10 = 40%@ninety words"
The tight preset is a summary of forty words that holds six of the ten units. That's sixty percent coverage, and about six point seven words per unit. The copied preset is ninety words holding four units. Forty percent, and twenty two point five words per unit.

@art tight-vs-copied
The copied summary is more than twice as long and holds fewer ideas. Picture it as the passage's opening, nearly word for word: the chlorophyll, the sunlight, the sugar, the summer's repairs. The three sentences have run out before the reds and the falling leaves. The tight summary names the cause and the colours, and stops.

@math heading="Both read at 240 wpm" items="377 × 60 / 94.25 = 240@two forty;;240 × 6/10 = 144 · 240 × 4/10 = 96@one forty four"
Both readers took ninety four point two five seconds over the three hundred and seventy seven words: two forty a minute. So their recalled rates are one forty four, and ninety six words a minute.

@chapter What to leave out
@list heading="A good summary leaves out" items="Examples, once the claim is kept@examples|The members of a list@the members of a list|Mechanism the claim doesn't need@detail of a mechanism|The passage's own phrasing@own phrasing"
So what does a good summary leave out? Roughly, anything whose loss leaves the passage's claims standing. First, examples, once the claim is kept: the maple and the birch can go. Then the members of a list: the word tannins can go. Then detail of a mechanism the main claim doesn't need. And the passage's own phrasing.

@art trim-list
The brown can stay: it's what is left when the other pigments break down. The layer of cells at the base of the leaf stalk can go. Three sentences force the choice. Aim for the main claim, the most important reason or mechanism, and what follows from it.

@chapter What the review found
@art highlighter
Dunlosky and colleagues, in twenty thirteen, rated common learning techniques by how well the evidence supports them. They rated highlighting, rereading and summarisation all of low utility, and practice testing and distributed practice high.

@words text="Trained summaries helped · untrained helped little"
Summarisation helped learners who'd been trained to do it well. Untrained summaries helped little.

@art closed-then-check
That answers the misconception. Highlighting marks a text while you look at it, and a summary copied from the open page is little more. But a summary written from memory, with the passage hidden, is a different act. It's retrieval practice, the kind of study the review rated high.

@chapter Keep it apart
@words text="Compare a summary with your next summary"
The drill logs this reading as other, apart from your free recall, because the two shares aren't the same measure. A three sentence summary isn't asked to hold every unit, so expect a lower share here. Compare a summary with your next summary.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Highlighting is studying@The first|A longer summary is a better one@The second|Writing with the text open@And the third"
The first slip is thinking highlighting is studying. The second is thinking a longer summary is a better one. Length is cost. And the third is writing the summary with the text open. Close it first, write, and check against it afterwards, so you can fix what matters.

@chapter Try it
@words text="A: 45 words, 5 units · B: 30 words, 5 units" sub="which is the better trade?"
Your turn. Summary A has forty five words and holds five units. Summary B has thirty words, and holds the same five. Which is the better trade? Same coverage, so the cost decides. B is six words per unit, against A's nine. B wins.
