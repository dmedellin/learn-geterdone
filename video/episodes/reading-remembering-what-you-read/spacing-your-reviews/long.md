@chapter The question
@title text="Spacing Your Reviews" sub="Reading Faster · Remembering What You Read" art=thumb
You've got fifty minutes to review a text you need to remember. Do you spend them all tonight, or spread them out? And if you spread them, how far apart?

@art forgetting-curve
The first measurements of forgetting were Hermann Ebbinghaus's, published in eighteen eighty five. Testing his own memory for lists of nonsense syllables, he measured how quickly it faded. And he reported that learning spread over several days held better than the same learning massed together.

@words text="Spaced beats massed" sub="Cepeda et al. (2006), review of verbal recall"
Cepeda and colleagues, in two thousand six, gathered the later studies of verbal recall into one review. Spaced practice beat massed practice. And the best gap grew with how long the material had to be remembered.

@art need-and-gap
So the longer you need a text, the longer the gaps between reviews can be. A text you need next week and a text you need in a year want different gaps.

@chapter A schedule is arithmetic
@words text="first gap · multiplier · reviews · minutes"
A review schedule has four parts: a first gap, a rule for the next gap, a number of reviews, and their length. Each gap is the last one times the multiplier, and the review days are the running totals of the gaps.

@math heading="Expanding preset" items="gaps: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16@the gaps are;;days: 1, 3, 7, 15, 31@days one, three;;5 × 10 = 50 minutes@fifty minutes"
The lab opens with a first gap of one day, each gap twice the last, and five reviews of ten minutes. So the gaps are one, two, four, eight and sixteen days, and the reviews fall on days one, three, seven, fifteen and thirty one. Five times ten is fifty minutes.

@art expanding-gaps
Each review day is the last one plus the next gap. One plus two is three, three plus four is seven, seven plus eight is fifteen, and fifteen plus sixteen is thirty one.

@chapter Equal and massed
@art equal-gaps
The equal preset spends the same fifty minutes with a first gap of six days and a multiplier of one. Every gap is six days, so the reviews fall on days six, twelve, eighteen, twenty four and thirty. It ends near the same day, with all its gaps the same size.

@art massed-day
The massed preset uses a multiplier of zero, so every later gap is zero, and all five reviews fall on day one, one after another. Same fifty minutes, all in one sitting.

@chapter Why cramming feels better
@words text="Massed feels best · spaced keeps more"
Massed review is the one that feels best. By the fifth pass in a row, the text is fluent, a self test scores high, and each review is easier than the last. Spaced reviews feel worse, because each one starts with something forgotten. But the spaced schedule is the one retained better at a delay.

@chapter Should the gaps grow?
@words text="Expanding vs equal: mixed" sub="Karpicke and Roediger (2007)"
Should the gaps grow? Expanding gaps are one reasonable schedule, but they're not what the review found. On expanding against equal gaps, the evidence is mixed. Karpicke and Roediger, in two thousand seven, found expanding retrieval helped in the short term, and equally spaced retrieval did as well or better over the longer term.

@chapter How to review
@art recall-review
And review by recalling, not rereading. Write what you remember with the text closed, then look back at what you missed. Ten minutes covers an article. If the total is more than the text is worth to you, drop a review rather than closing the gaps.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Massed works better because it feels better@The first|The expanding schedule is the proven one@The second|Reviewing by rereading@And the third"
The first slip is thinking massed review works better, because it feels better. The second is taking the expanding schedule to be the proven one. It's a reasonable choice, not a finding. And the third is reviewing by rereading, which brings back the fluency problem.
