@chapter The question
@title text="Retrieval Beats Rereading" sub="Reading Faster · Remembering What You Read" art=thumb
You want to remember a passage. Should you read it again, or close it and try to write down what it said? The answer depends on when you're tested, and that's the whole lesson.

@chapter The drill
@art read-twice
The drill has two parts, and both happen today. Condition one is The Mould on the Plate. Read it at your usual pace, then read it again. There's no test.

@art recall-once
Condition two is The Tower That Was Meant to Come Down. Read it once, then write what you remember with the page hidden, and tick its idea units, as in Free Recall. Pulling something out of memory like this is a way of studying it, not only of testing it. That's retrieval practice.

@art calendar-wait
Then you wait. Two days on, the Subject offers both passages' questions, twelve in all, with neither passage shown. It's offered once, and it writes your two delayed scores into the boxes.

@chapter The study
@words text="Roediger and Karpicke (2006)" sub="read twice, or read once and recall · tested 5 minutes, 2 days or a week later"
The design follows Roediger and Karpicke, from two thousand six. In their first experiment, students studied short prose passages by reading them twice, or by reading once and then taking a free recall test with no feedback. They were tested five minutes, two days, or a week later.

@math heading="Read twice vs recalled" items="5 minutes: ≈ 81% vs ≈ 75%  (−6)@At five minutes;;2 days: ≈ 54% vs ≈ 68%  (+14)@At two days"
At five minutes, the rereaders recalled more, about eighty one percent against seventy five. At two days, the order had reversed: about fifty four against sixty eight, a difference of fourteen points. And at a week, the tested group was still ahead.

@art crossover
So the way of studying that did better at five minutes did worse at two days. On the free recall test that took the place of a second reading, students wrote down about seventy percent of the idea units.

@chapter Why the reversal
@art fluency
Why would rereading win at first? A second reading comes just after the first. Every idea has been met twice, and the text is fresh. The recall group met their passage once, and wrote down only what they could find, with no feedback.

@words text="Fluent fades · retrieved is easier to retrieve again"
One reading of the reversal is that what was only fluent fades over two days, while what was once retrieved is easier to retrieve again.

@chapter Your own result
@math heading="Reader-example preset (an illustration)" items="read twice: 2/6 ≈ 33.3%@two of six;;recalled: 4/6 ≈ 66.7%@four of six;;4/6 − 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ +33.3 points@one third"
Here's an illustration, not a study. Two days later, a reader gets two of six on the passage read twice, and four of six on the passage recalled. The difference is one third, about thirty three point three points. That's two questions, and on one pair of passages, it's still a single measurement.

@art confounds
Your drill is a gentler test than theirs. Yours is multiple choice, a recognition test, which tends to show a smaller testing effect. And the two passages differ in content and difficulty, and the one you read first may matter. So a small difference, or none, is not a contradiction.

@chapter It feels better
@words text="Fluency feels like knowing"
Rereading also feels better. The second pass is smooth, and every sentence is familiar, and that fluency is easy to take for knowing. The recall is effortful, and shows you the gaps. The five minute test agrees with the feeling. The two day test doesn't.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading it again is the best way to remember@The first|Judging a method by how it feels@The second|One pair of passages as your result@And the third"
The first slip is believing that reading it again is the best way to remember it. The second is judging a way of studying by how it feels. And the third is taking one pair of passages as your result. One question is about sixteen point seven points, so swap the order and test again.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Read twice: 3 of 6 · Recalled: 5 of 6" sub="difference in points?"
Your turn. Two days after studying, a reader scores three of six on the passage read twice, and five of six on the one recalled. What does the lab print? Five sixths minus three sixths is one third: about plus thirty three point three points. Not plus two. That's questions, not points.
