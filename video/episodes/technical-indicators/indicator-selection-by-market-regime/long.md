@chapter The question
@title text="Indicator Selection by Market Regime" sub="Technical Indicators" art=thumb
An RSI reading of seventy. A fast moving average crossing a slow one. Do those mean the same thing in every market? The lesson's answer is no. The same threshold or crossover is read differently in a trend, a range, a contraction, and in high volatility.

@words text="Classify the environment, then read the signal"
Here's the lesson's rule. Indicator settings don't have a fixed meaning across every market regime. So classify the environment, and define the job you want the indicator to do, before you read the signal.

@chapter Regimes change signal behavior
@art trend-regime
In a persistent trend, trend following calculations work as intended. A crossover happens, and then the averages stay apart. But mean reversion thresholds, like an oscillator's overbought level, can stay extreme for a long time while price keeps going.

@art range-regime
In a range, it flips. Trend following calculations tend to whipsaw, crossing back and forth as price oscillates. Fast crossovers in a narrow range create repeated state changes. Meanwhile oscillators swing between their levels the way they were designed to.

@words text="Trend-following whipsaws in ranges" sub="mean-reversion stays extreme in trends"
So each family has a regime where it behaves, and a regime where it misleads. That's why the same template can't be applied everywhere. A rule that reads RSI at seventy as a reason to sell may behave as intended in a range, and keep fighting the move in a strong uptrend.

@chapter Volatility is a separate dimension
@art volatility-grid
Volatility is a different question from trend. A market can trend with high or low volatility, and a range can be calm, or unstable. So think of it as two separate dimensions: directional behavior, and the size of the movement. A calm trend and a violent trend can need different filters, even though both are trends.

@art squeeze-regime
A contraction, where volatility squeezes down, is its own case. Treating that contraction as a direction is a common mistake. A squeeze can precede movement in either direction, or fail to expand at all for a while.

@chapter Match indicators to purpose
@list heading="Classify, then choose" items="Describe price behavior@Describe|Choose the analysis purpose@purpose|Select a compact set@compact set|Reassess on regime change@reassess"
So work in order. Describe the price behavior: directional persistence, oscillation, volatility level, contraction, or transition. Choose the analysis purpose: a trend filter, a range trigger, a volatility filter, or a breakout boundary. Select a compact set. And reassess when the regime changes.

@art compact-pick
A compact set here means one or two primary tools, and one distinct supporting measure when it's needed. Selection follows purpose. Use the indicators that measure the regime and decision you actually need, rather than the same template everywhere. In a trend you might lean on a trend filter. In a range, a range trigger. In a contraction, a breakout boundary.

@art regime-shift
And the last step is the one people skip. When price behavior no longer matches your assumptions, stop applying the old interpretation. A trend reading that served you well becomes a liability once the market starts going sideways.

@chapter Synthetic scenarios
@words text="Intended answers for synthetic scenarios" sub="real regimes are continuous and sometimes ambiguous"
The lesson's challenge gives you synthetic scenarios to classify, and scores your choices against intended answers. That's for teaching. Real market regimes are continuous, multi timeframe, and sometimes ambiguous, so any selection has to be validated, not treated as universal.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Mean-reversion thresholds in a persistent trend@The first|Fast crossovers in a narrow range@The second|Volatility contraction as direction@The third"
The first mistake is using mean reversion thresholds in a persistent trend without adjustment. The second is using fast crossovers in a narrow range, which can create frequent whipsaws. And the third is treating volatility contraction as direction. All three come from the same root: reading a signal before classifying the market it appeared in.
