@chapter The values not allowed
@title text="Simplifying Radical Expressions" sub="Rational and Radical Expressions" art=thumb
Radicals start the same way fractions did, with the values that aren't allowed. An even root needs a radicand that isn't negative. And the root sign names a single number, not two. Both facts meet in one identity that catches almost everyone.

@math heading="nth root" items="bⁿ = a  ⟹  b is an nth root of a@b to the n;;ⁿ√a:  n is the index,  a is the radicand@the index;;√a means index 2@no index"
For a whole number n of two or more, b is an nth root of a if b to the n equals a. In the nth root of a, n is the index and a is the radicand. With no index written, the index is two.

@art one-root
Both three and minus three square to nine. But the square root of nine is three, not plus or minus three. When n is even and a isn't negative, the root sign means the non negative root, the principal root. That's what makes it a function.

@chapter Even and odd
@art even-domain
When n is even and a is negative, the root isn't a real number. An even power is never negative, so the square root of minus four names nothing real. This is the radical version of the denominator is not zero. Quadratics and Complex Numbers later gives it a value, using i.

@art odd-domain
An odd power keeps its sign, so odd roots have no such trouble. The cube root of minus eight is minus two. When n is odd, the root is the unique real root, with the same sign as a.

@chapter The identity that catches everyone
@math heading="For every real x" items="√(x²) = |x|@absolute value of x;;x = −5:  √25 = 5,  but x = −5@at minus five"
For every real x, the square root of x squared is the absolute value of x. The left side is never negative, by definition, so the right side can't be either. Writing it as just x is false for every negative x. At minus five, the left side is five and x is minus five.

@art fold-to-positive
The absolute value isn't decoration. Squaring wipes out the sign, and the root can't bring it back. In general, the nth root of x to the n is the absolute value of x when n is even, and just x when n is odd.

@chapter Products, quotients, not sums
@math heading="Product and quotient rules" items="ⁿ√a · ⁿ√b = ⁿ√(ab)@the product;;ⁿ√a / ⁿ√b = ⁿ√(a/b)@the quotient;;n even:  a ≥ 0,  b ≥ 0@for even n"
The product of two nth roots is the nth root of the product, and the same goes for quotients, if every radical is defined. For even n, that means a and b aren't negative, and b isn't zero in the quotient. Otherwise, root minus four times root minus nine would come out as six.

@art sum-not-split
What's missing is a rule for sums. Roots split over multiplication and division, and nothing else. The square root of nine plus sixteen is the square root of twenty five, five. But the square root of nine plus the square root of sixteen is seven.

@chapter Simplified form
@art perfect-square
Simplified form means no perfect nth power left under the radical. The square root of seventy two becomes six root two, because seventy two is thirty six times two. It also means no fraction under the radical, and no radical in a denominator.

@math heading="Two numbers" items="√200 = √100 · √2 = 10√2@two hundred;;∛(−108) = ∛(−27) · ∛4 = −3∛4@minus one hundred and eight"
Two examples. Two hundred is a hundred times two, so its square root is ten root two. The cube root of minus one hundred and eight: the index is odd, so a negative radicand is fine. It's minus twenty seven times four, giving minus three times the cube root of four.

@math heading="A variable" items="√(48x⁵):  defined only where x ≥ 0@forty eight;;48x⁵ = 16x⁴ · 3x@sixteen x;;√(48x⁵) = 4x²√(3x)@four x squared"
Now the square root of forty eight x to the fifth. It's defined only where x is at least zero. Split it as sixteen x to the fourth, times three x, and it becomes four x squared times root three x. x squared is never negative, so it needs no bars.

@words text="√(50x³) = 5x√(2x)" sub="50x³ ≥ 0 forces x ≥ 0"
The bars really are at stake in the square root of fifty x cubed, which is five x root two x. Taking out the root of x squared gives the absolute value of x. But for it to be defined at all, x is at least zero, and then that is just x. The domain did the job.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Writing √(x²) = x@The first|Splitting a root over a sum@The second|Reading √9 as ±3@the third"
The first slip is writing the square root of x squared as x. It's only true when x isn't negative. The second is splitting a root over a sum. Test nine and sixteen once, and the habit dies. And the third is reading root nine as plus or minus three. The symbol names one number.

@chapter Try it
@words text="√(18t²)" sub="18t² = 9 · 2 · t²"
Your turn. Simplify the square root of eighteen t squared. It factors as nine times two times t squared. Decide the domain first. Then take out both square factors, and keep the principal root's sign. Ask yourself whether negative t is allowed.
