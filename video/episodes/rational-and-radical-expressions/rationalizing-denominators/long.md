@chapter Why bother?
@title text="Rationalizing Denominators" sub="Rational and Radical Expressions" art=thumb
A radical in a denominator can be moved out by multiplying the fraction by a carefully chosen form of one. The value never changes. That's exactly what makes the move legal.

@art same-number
So why do it? Three over root seven is a perfectly good number. But three root seven over seven is easier to estimate by hand. And a single agreed form lets you compare answers. Three over root seven, three root seven over seven, and three sevenths root seven are the same number, written three ways.

@words text="Rationalize the denominator" sub="an equal fraction with no radical below the bar"
To rationalize the denominator is to rewrite the fraction as an equal fraction whose denominator has no radical in it.

@chapter One square root
@math heading="3/√7" items="= (3 · √7) / (√7 · √7)@multiply top and bottom;;= 3√7 / 7@three root seven over seven;;check: 1.13389… both ways@one point one"
With one square root below, multiply top and bottom by that radical. Three over root seven becomes three root seven, over root seven times root seven. And root seven times root seven is seven, so it's three root seven over seven. Check: both come to one point one three three eight nine.

@art radical-moves-up
Notice the radical didn't disappear. It moved to the numerator, where the convention allows it.

@art form-of-one
And you have to multiply the top too. Root seven over root seven is one, so multiplying by it changes the form, not the value. Multiply only the bottom by root seven, and you've changed the number.

@chapter A higher index
@math heading="1/∛2" items="✗  × ∛2/∛2  →  ∛2/∛4@by cube root two;;✓  × ∛4/∛4  →  ∛4/∛8 = ∛4/2@cube root of four"
Here the reflex fails. For one over the cube root of two, multiplying by cube root two over itself gives cube root two over cube root four. No better. The bottom must become a perfect cube, so use the cube root of four. That gives cube root four over cube root eight, which is cube root four over two.

@art complete-the-power
So ask what the radicand is missing, not what it already is. For root seven, you need one more root seven. For the cube root of two, you need the cube root of four, because cube root two times cube root four is cube root eight, which is two.

@chapter Two terms: the conjugate
@art conjugate-pair
For a denominator a plus root b, multiply top and bottom by a minus root b. The new denominator is a squared minus b, with no radical. For root a plus root b, the conjugate is root a minus root b, and the new bottom is a minus b. Both are the difference of two squares.

@math heading="6/(√5 − √2)" items="× (√5 + √2)/(√5 + √2)@multiply above and below;;denominator: (√5)² − (√2)² = 5 − 2 = 3@the denominator;;= 6(√5 + √2)/3 = 2(√5 + √2)@becomes;;check: 7.3006… both ways@seven point three"
Take six over root five minus root two. Multiply above and below by root five plus root two. The denominator becomes five minus two, which is three. So the fraction becomes six times root five plus root two, over three, which reduces to two times root five plus root two. Both sides come to seven point three oh oh six.

@words text="Simplify and combine first" sub="√2 + √2 = 2√2 · √2 − √2 = 0 is not a form of 1"
For number denominators like these, simplify and combine before you choose the multiplier. Root two plus root two is first two root two. Its so called conjugate, root two minus root two, is zero, and zero is not a form of one.

@chapter Flip one sign
@art flip-one-sign
One warning about signs. The conjugate of root five minus root two is root five plus root two. The conjugate of three minus root two is three plus root two. Only the sign between the terms flips. Negate the whole thing instead, and the product is still full of radicals.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Multiplying a cube root by itself@The first|Negating the whole denominator@The second|Multiplying only the denominator@the third"
The first slip is multiplying a cube root denominator by itself. Cube root two times cube root two is cube root four, not two. The second is negating the whole denominator instead of one sign. And the third is multiplying only the denominator. The multiplier has to go on top and bottom, because only then is it one.

@chapter Try it
@words text="2/∛9    and    4/(3 + √5)" sub="what completes 9 to a cube? which sign makes a difference of squares?"
Your turn. Rationalize two over the cube root of nine, and four over three plus root five. For the first, ask what factor completes nine to a cube. For the second, ask what sign turns the denominator into a difference of squares.
