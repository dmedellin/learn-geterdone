@chapter Like terms in disguise
@title text="Adding, Subtracting and Multiplying Radicals" sub="Rational and Radical Expressions" art=thumb
Adding radicals is collecting like terms, in disguise. Only matching radicals combine, and you can't tell which ones match until everything is simplified. Multiplying is friendlier, and one product, the conjugate pair, is worth memorising.

@art like-terms
Two radicals are like when they have the same index and the same radicand. Three root two and minus seven root two are like. Root two and root three are not. And root two and the cube root of two are not either.

@math heading="Adding like radicals" items="a·ⁿ√c + b·ⁿ√c = (a + b)·ⁿ√c@a times;;3√2 + 5√2 = (3 + 5)√2 = 8√2@three root two"
For like radicals, a times the nth root of c, plus b times the nth root of c, is a plus b, times the nth root of c. It's the distributive law, with the radical as the common factor. Three root two plus five root two is eight root two, for the same reason three k plus five k is eight k.

@chapter Unlike stays unlike
@art unlike-terms
For unlike radicals there's no such rule, because there's no common factor to take out. So root two plus root three is already in simplest form. It looks unfinished, but there's no step left.

@math heading="√2 + √3 ≠ √5" items="√2 + √3 ≈ 3.1463@three point one;;√5 ≈ 2.2361@root five"
And in particular, it's not root five. Root two plus root three is about three point one four six three, while root five is about two point two three six one.

@chapter Simplify before you judge
@art revealed-alike
Root eight plus root eighteen looks like two unlike radicals. But simplify. Root eight is two root two, and root eighteen is three root two. Any decision about what combines, made before simplifying, is a guess.

@math items="√8 + √18 = 2√2 + 3√2 = 5√2@five root two;;√50 − √18 = 5√2 − 3√2 = 2√2@root fifty"
So root eight plus root eighteen is five root two, a single term. In the same way, root fifty minus root eighteen is five root two minus three root two, which is two root two. Not root thirty two. You combine coefficients, not radicands.

@chapter Multiplying
@math heading="3√2 · 4√6" items="= 12√12@twelve root twelve;;12 = 4 · 3,  so √12 = 2√3@four times three;;= 12 · 2√3 = 24√3@twenty four"
Multiplying needs nothing to match. Multiply the outside parts, multiply the radicands, then simplify. Three root two times four root six is twelve root twelve. But root twelve isn't simplified. Twelve is four times three, so root twelve is two root three, and the answer is twenty four root three. That last step is the one most often skipped.

@art difference-of-squares
With sums, you distribute just as with polynomials. And one product is special: a plus root b, times a minus root b, equals a squared minus b. It's the difference of two squares, and squaring is exactly what removes a square root.

@chapter Conjugates versus squares
@math items="(2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 4 − 3 = 1@two plus root three;;(√5 + √2)² = 5 + 2√10 + 2 = 7 + 2√10@root five plus root two"
Two products, side by side. Two plus root three, times two minus root three, is four minus three, which is one. But root five plus root two, squared, is five plus two root ten plus two, which is seven plus two root ten.

@art cross-cancel
In the conjugate pair, the cross terms are minus two root three and plus two root three. They cancel, and the root disappears. That's the whole reason rationalizing denominators works.

@art binomial-square
In the square, the cross terms are equal, and they add. Squaring a binomial never loses its middle term. And that matters later: root x plus one, squared, is x plus two root x plus one, which still has a root.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Adding the radicands@The first|Judging likeness before simplifying@The second|Losing the middle term when squaring@the third"
The first slip is adding the radicands, like turning root fifty minus root eighteen into root thirty two. The second is judging likeness before simplifying. And the third is losing the middle term when you square. Root five plus root two, squared, is seven plus two root ten, not seven.
