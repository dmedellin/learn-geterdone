@chapter Two kinds of small word
@title text="Give It Up, Not Give Up It" sub="Small Words and Comparisons" art=thumb
Find it out. Look at it. Both are a verb and a small word, and the pronoun it goes in a different place. That place tells you what kind of small word you have.

@list heading="Two kinds" items="particle  ·  up, out, off, down, away, back, over@A particle|preposition  ·  at, to, for, of, with@A preposition"
A particle is a small word such as up, out or off that joins a verb to make one action: give up, find out, put off. A preposition is a small word such as at or of that joins a noun to the verb. On the page they look the same, but they follow different rules.

@chapter The test
@art pronoun-middle
Take a pronoun, it, as the object. After a verb and up, it goes in the middle. Give it up. Find it out. Put it off.

@art pronoun-end
After a verb and at, it goes at the end. Look at it. Think of it. So the place of the pronoun tells you which small word you have.

@chapter Counting it in a novel
@words text="34 between · 8 after" sub="81.0%"
The lab runs this test on four hundred and seventy-three lines from the novel Pride and Prejudice. It finds a verb, one of seven particles, and a pronoun. Thirty-four lines have the pronoun between. Eight have it after. That gives eighty-one percent.

@art clause-break
Eighty-one percent looks like a rule with exceptions. But read the eight, and it isn't. Five of them are a verb and a particle at the end of a clause, and the next clause starts with a pronoun. Let us sit down. You are. That pronoun isn't the object at all.

@art two-jobs
The other three are get over it, twice, and glancing over it. There, over is a preposition, and the pronoun follows it, just as the rule for prepositions says. So none of the eight puts a pronoun object after a true particle.

@words text="the play: 16 between · 5 after" sub="76.2%, and again no real exception"
The play reads the same way. It has a hundred and forty-nine lines, sixteen with the pronoun between and five after. That's seventy-six point two percent. Three of the five begin a new clause, and two follow a noun. Again, none puts the object after a particle.

@chapter The preposition side
@math heading="Prepositions: the pronoun goes last" items="look at it@Look at it;;think of it@Think of it;;dance with him@Dance with him"
The lab also lists three hundred and forty-seven lines with a verb, a preposition and a pronoun. Look at it. Think of it. Dance with him. Every one has the pronoun last. Those lines were gathered that way, so the count shows how common the pattern is, not that no one could write it another way.

@chapter Nouns can move
@art noun-swap
Only the pronoun is fixed. If the object is a noun, either place is possible. Give up the idea, and give the idea up, are both fine. But give up it is the error this lesson is named for.

@math heading="Six phrases" items="find it out  ·  particle, pronoun between@find it out;;give it up  ·  particle, pronoun between@give it up;;look at it  ·  preposition, pronoun after@look at it;;get over it  ·  over as a preposition@get over it"
Name the small word, then the place of the pronoun. In find it out and give it up, the small word is a particle, and the pronoun is between. In look at it, it's a preposition, and the pronoun is after. And in get over it, over is working as a preposition.

@chapter The common ones
@art bar-ranking
The novel's lines aren't all different verbs. Go away comes thirty-two times, sit down twenty-nine, find out twenty, come back sixteen, and give up twelve. In the play, sit down leads, with eleven. And remember, the lab finds spellings. It doesn't know that give up doesn't mean give and up.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="look at it  ≠  give up it@The first|over: get it over · get over it@The second|✗ give up it   ✓ give it up@The third"
The first slip is thinking look at it proves give up it. They're different kinds of small word. The second is giving a small word only one job. Over is a particle in get it over, and a preposition in get over it. And the third is moving a pronoun the way you'd move a noun.

@chapter Try it
@words text="give up it · give it up · give it to up" sub="which is correct?"
Your turn. Which is correct: give up it, give it up, or give it to up? Pause and choose. Up is a particle, so the pronoun goes between the verb and the particle. Give it up.
