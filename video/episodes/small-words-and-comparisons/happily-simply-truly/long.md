@chapter The usual rule
@title text="Happily, Simply, Truly" sub="Small Words and Comparisons" art=thumb
Careful becomes carefully. Quick becomes quickly. You were probably told the rule: to make an adverb, add l-y. Let's score that rule, and then fix it.

@words text="careful → carefully" sub="adjective → adverb"
An adjective says what a thing is like. An adverb says how, when or how much. Many adverbs are made from an adjective by adding l-y. Careful, carefully.

@art ending-tab
The lab takes a hundred and fifty-three words from a word list that are adjectives and nothing else. It asks a dictionary for the l-y spellings of each. A hundred and twenty-six have at least one. Twenty-seven have none.

@chapter Just add -ly
@math heading="Add -ly" items="98 of 126 right  =  77.8%@Ninety-eight come out;;terrible  →  ✗ terriblely@terriblely;;economic  →  ✗ economicly@economicly"
Add l-y to the hundred and twenty-six, and compare with the dictionary. Ninety-eight come out right. That's seventy-seven point eight percent. The twenty-eight wrong ones are ordinary words. The rule makes terriblely, and economicly.

@art four-stacks
And the misses aren't random. They group by ending. Twelve end in l-e. Eight end in i-c. Seven end in y. And one is tall.

@math heading="The 28 misses, by ending" items="-le  ·  12  ·  acceptable, comfortable, horrible, terrible …@Twelve like;;-ic  ·  8  ·  democratic, economic, historic, scientific …@Eight like;;-y  ·  7  ·  angry, crazy, lazy, necessary …@Seven like;;-ll  ·  1  ·  tall@And tall"
Twelve like acceptable, comfortable, horrible and terrible. Eight like democratic, economic, historic and scientific. Seven like angry, crazy, lazy and necessary. And tall. Sort the misses this way, and each group points to its own fix.

@chapter Five changes
@list heading="Five changes" items="-y → -ily  ·  angry, angrily@angry becomes|-le → -ly  ·  capable, capably@capable becomes|-ic → -ically  ·  dramatic, dramatically@dramatic becomes|-ue → -uly  ·  true, truly@true becomes|-ll → -lly  ·  full, fully@full becomes"
Each ending has its own change. A y after a consonant turns into i: angry becomes angrily. L-e changes to l-y: capable becomes capably. I-c takes a-l-l-y: dramatic becomes dramatically. U-e loses the e: true becomes truly. And double l loses one l: full becomes fully.

@art trim-and-add
With the five changes, the rule is right on all hundred and twenty-six. That's a hundred percent. But it's an honest hundred, with limits. No word in the list ends in u-e, so that change is never tested.

@art lookalike
The only double l word is tall, and the check passes it, because tally is a word. But no one says tally for tall. A dictionary check shows that a spelling is a word. It doesn't show that it's this word. Unlike is the same: unlikely is a word, but it's an adjective.

@chapter One word on its own
@words text="public → publicly" sub="✗ publically"
One more to know. Public ends in i-c, but it takes plain l-y. The adverb is publicly. It isn't in the printed list, so the lab doesn't count it.

@chapter No -ly adverb at all
@art short-list
Twenty-seven of the adjectives have no l-y adverb in the dictionary. Three already end in l-y: elderly, ugly and unlikely. Six are about the state of a person or a thing, like afraid, alive and sorry. Eleven name a place or a kind, like eastern, rural and nuclear.

@art other-words
Five more are available, difficult, foreign, numerous and unclear. For words like these, don't make an adverb. Say it with other words. Not an adverb of difficult, but with difficulty. Not an adverb of foreign, but in a foreign way.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="✗ angryly · capablely · dramaticly@The first|elderly, ugly, friendly: adjectives@The second|hardly ≠ the adverb of hard@And the third"
The first slip is adding l-y and thinking you're done. That makes angryly, capablely and dramaticly. The second is thinking every word that ends in l-y is an adverb. Elderly, ugly and friendly are adjectives. And the third is trusting a dictionary check for the meaning. Hardly is a word, but it means almost not.

@words text="fast · hard" sub="adverbs with no ending at all"
Lately and highly are the same kind of trap. They're real words, but they don't mean late and high. And some adverbs need no ending at all. Fast and hard are adverbs just as they are, with nothing added. The lesson names them, and the lab doesn't count them.

@chapter Try it
@words text="guilty · capable · careful" sub="guiltily · capably · carefully"
Your turn. Make the adverbs of guilty, capable and careful. Look at the ending first. Guilty ends in y, so guiltily. Capable ends in l-e, so capably. And careful just adds l-y: carefully.
