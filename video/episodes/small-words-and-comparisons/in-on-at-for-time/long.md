@chapter The usual rule
@title text="In, On or At: Time" sub="Small Words and Comparisons" art=thumb
In, on and at are three small words that go in front of a time word. In July. On Tuesday. At night. Learners mix them up all the time, so let's find a rule that really works.

@words text="in · on · at" sub="a preposition joins a noun to the sentence"
These small words are called prepositions. A preposition stands in front of a noun, and joins it to the rest of the sentence.

@art length-bars
You may have been told a rule about length. In is for long times, on is for days, and at is for points. But look at Christmas. It lasts days, and it takes at. At Christmas. The afternoon lasts only hours, and it takes in. In the afternoon. So length is not what decides.

@chapter The kind of time word
@art sorting-bins
Here's a better guide. Look at the kind of time word. The lab sorts time words into eight kinds, and each kind gets one small word.

@list heading="The kind decides" items="on · a day@A day takes on|in · a month, a year, a season, a morning@takes in|at · a clock time, night, noon, midnight, a festival@takes at"
A day takes on. A month, a year, a season or a morning takes in. A clock time, night, noon, midnight or a festival takes at. That's the whole rule. It never asks how long the time is.

@math heading="Seven phrases" items="on Tuesday  ·  a day@On Tuesday;;in July  ·  a month@In July;;in 2025  ·  a year@In twenty twenty-five;;at eight o'clock  ·  a clock time@At eight o'clock;;at night  ·  night@At night, night;;in the evening  ·  part of a day@In the evening"
Name the kind first, then the small word. On Tuesday, a day. In July, a month. In twenty twenty-five, a year. At eight o'clock, a clock time. At night, night. In the evening, a part of the day.

@chapter How well it works
@art tally-row
The lab tried this rule on one hundred and nine printed lines. Most come from the novel Pride and Prejudice, from eighteen thirteen. Some come from a play from eighteen ninety-five, and seventeen come from two modern documents.

@math heading="109 printed lines" items="103 of 109 right  =  94.5%@one hundred and three;;the novel: 77 of 79@The novel gives;;the play: 9 of 13@The play gives;;modern documents: 17 of 17@the modern documents"
The rule is right on one hundred and three of them. That's ninety-four point five percent. The novel gives seventy-seven right out of seventy-nine. The play gives nine out of thirteen, and the modern documents seventeen out of seventeen. Six of the eight kinds are right on every line.

@chapter A day beats the part of the day
@art named-day
Morning, afternoon and evening take in. In the evening. Early in the morning. The lines show that twenty-two times. But seven lines use on instead. On Saturday morning. On the following morning.

@words text="on the following morning" sub="a named day beats the part of the day"
In each of the seven, a day is named or picked out, and the day decides. Morning alone takes in. But the following morning means one particular day, and a day takes on. The rule has a step for this, and it gets all seven right.

@chapter The six misses
@art clock-moon
So what are the six lines the rule gets wrong? Three of them put on before night, with a day name. On Sunday night. On Thursday night. On Wednesday night. The rule said at, because night takes at.

@list heading="Six misses, three kinds" items="on Sunday night · on Thursday night · on Wednesday night@the same step|on the evening before my going · on the morning of the day@Two more|at evening parties@the last one"
This is the same step again. If a named day beats a morning, it beats a night too. Two more pick out the evening or the morning with words after it, and the lab reads only the words before, so it can't see them. And the last one, at evening parties, isn't a time phrase at all.

@chapter No small word at all
@art empty-slot
Some time words take no small word at all. If the phrase begins with this, last, next or every, stop there. This morning. Last night. The next morning.

@words text="this morning · last night" sub="✗ in this morning"
The novel has thirteen lines of this morning and nine of last night, and every one is bare. If you add a small word, and say in this morning, you've made the error.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="✗ in Monday morning   ✓ on Monday morning@The first|✗ choosing by length   ✓ at Christmas@The second|✗ in this morning   ✓ this morning@The third"
The first slip is putting in before every part of the day. In the morning is right, so in Monday morning feels right. It isn't. The day wins: on Monday morning. The second is choosing by how long the time is. And the third is adding a small word to a phrase like this morning.

@chapter Try it
@words text="___ Friday morning" sub="in · on · at"
Your turn. Which small word goes before Friday morning? Pause and choose. Is a day named? Yes, Friday. A named day beats the part of the day, so the answer is on. On Friday morning.
