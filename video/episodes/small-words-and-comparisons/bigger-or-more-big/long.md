@chapter Two ways to compare
@title text="Bigger or More Big" sub="Small Words and Comparisons" art=thumb
Bigger, or more big? Happier, or more happy? English compares in two ways, with an ending or with more in front. Here's how to choose.

@words text="-er · more" sub="bigger · more beautiful"
A comparison of an adjective uses e-r, or more. For the top of the scale, it's e-s-t, or most. Bigger and biggest. More beautiful and most beautiful.

@art old-book
Most books say that short words take e-r and long words take more. That's a good start. But how short is short? Pleasant and handsome both have eight letters, and an old novel puts e-r on both.

@chapter Count the parts
@art beat-dots
So count parts instead of letters. A part is one beat of a word, built round a vowel sound. Big has one part. Happy has two. Beautiful has three.

@list heading="Three lines" items="one part: -er, -est · bigger@One part takes|two parts ending in -y: -ier, -iest · happier@Two parts ending|everything else: more, most · more beautiful@Everything else"
Now the rule has three lines. One part takes e-r and e-s-t: bigger, biggest. Two parts ending in y take them too, with the y changed to i: happier, happiest. Everything else takes more and most, and the adjective doesn't change.

@math heading="Four adjectives" items="big  ·  1 part  →  bigger@Big has one;;happy  ·  2 parts, -y  →  happier@Happy has two;;beautiful  ·  3 parts  →  more beautiful@Beautiful has three;;handsome  ·  2 parts  →  more handsome@handsome has two"
Let's run it. Big has one part, so bigger. Happy has two parts and ends in y, so happier. Beautiful has three, so more beautiful. And handsome has two parts with no y, so more handsome.

@chapter Scoring it on a novel
@art score-bar
The lab holds three hundred and sixty-seven comparisons from Pride and Prejudice, a novel from eighteen thirteen. The rule is right on three hundred and thirty-seven of them. That's ninety-one point eight percent.

@math heading="The novel, by parts" items="1 part:  268 with an ending · 2 with more@one part;;3 parts:  0 with an ending · 18 with more@three parts;;4 or more:  0 with an ending · 10 with more@four parts or more;;2 parts:  54 with an ending · 15 with more@two parts"
Look at the parts. Words of one part: two hundred and sixty-eight with an ending, and only two with more. Words of three parts: none with an ending, eighteen with more. Four parts or more: none, and ten. Both choices really happen in only one place, two parts: fifty-four with an ending, fifteen with more.

@chapter Where it breaks
@art two-part-fork
So words of two parts are the whole difficulty. The rule gets thirty forms wrong, and twenty-two of them are two part adjectives that the novel gives an ending. Handsomer. Handsomest. Pleasanter.

@words text="handsomer (1813) · more handsome (today)" sub="handsome · pleasant · common · gentle · minute · narrow · noble · quiet · severe · stupid"
They come from ten adjectives. Some are still good English with an ending, like quieter, narrower and gentler. But a modern writer would give handsome and pleasant more and most. The novel isn't wrong. It's from eighteen thirteen.

@math heading="Rule B" items="also -er for 2 parts ending -ow, -le, -er@also gives;;gentlest · narrowest · nobler · noblest@four forms;;341 of 367  =  92.9%@three hundred and forty-one"
A second rule, rule B, also gives an ending to two part words ending in o-w, l-e or e-r. It moves four forms: gentlest, narrowest, nobler and noblest. The score goes up to three hundred and forty-one, ninety-two point nine percent. But that's a rule built to fit the novel.

@chapter The other eight
@list heading="The other eight" items="oftener × 3  ·  an adverb@Three of the other|more angry · more likely · most likely@Five go|more strange · most sure@And more strange"
Three of the other misses are oftener. That's an adverb, so it's outside the rule. Five go the other way. More angry, more likely and most likely are two part words ending in y that the novel gives more. And more strange and most sure are one part words given more. Today, more likely is the usual form.

@chapter Other texts
@art ending-tab
The play, from eighteen ninety-five, has forty-nine forms, and rule A is right on forty-four. The two modern documents have thirty-two forms, with words like older and earlier, and the rule is right on all thirty-two. So take the rule, and check the date of an old form before you copy it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="✗ more big   ✓ bigger@The first|✗ beautifuler   ✓ more beautiful@The second|✗ copying handsomer   ✓ check the date@The third"
The first slip is thinking more is always safe. For a word of one part, it's the error: more big. The second is putting an ending on a long word. Beautifuler isn't a form anyone wrote. And the third is copying every form from an old book. Look at the date.

@chapter Try it
@art y-to-i
Your turn. What's the e-r form of happy? Pause, and count the parts first.

@words text="happy → happier" sub="✗ happyer   ✗ more happy"
It has two parts, and it ends in y. So change the y to i, and add e-r. Happier. Not happyer, and not more happy.
