@chapter A long list
@title text="Six Patterns, Not One Hundred and Eighty" sub="Irregular Verbs" art=thumb
About one hundred and eighty irregular verbs. That's a long list, and a long list is hard to hold. But the list isn't as long as it looks, because the verbs come in a few shapes.

@art three-cards
Take the three forms of a verb: the base, the past, and the form after have. Then ask one question. Which of them are the same string of letters? There are only a few answers, and each answer is a class.

@chapter Six classes
@math heading="Which forms match?" items="past = after have  ·  bring, brought, brought  ·  60@Past and the form after have;;all three the same  ·  put, put, put  ·  21@All three match;;base = after have  ·  come, came, come  ·  4@Base and the form after have;;base = past  ·  beat, beat, beaten  ·  1@Base and past"
Past and the form after have match: bring, brought, brought. That's sixty verbs, the largest class by far. All three match: put, put, put, twenty-one verbs. Base and the form after have match: come, came, come, four verbs. Base and past match: beat, beat, beaten. Just one.

@art split-by-letter
Then there are forty-six verbs where all three forms differ. They split by the last letter. Thirty-seven end in n, like known. Nine do not.

@words text="be" sub="be · am · is · are · being · was · were · been"
That's six classes. The verb be stands outside them all. It has eight forms, two of them in the past, and no rule about three strings fits it. It's the one verb handled by hand.

@art six-trays
A class tells you how many forms to hold. One for the class of twenty-one. Two for the classes of sixty, four and one. And three for the forty-six where every form differs.

@chapter Stop at the first yes
@list heading="Ask in this order" items="All three the same? → 21@all three forms the same|Past = after have? → 60@the past and the form after have|Base matches one? → 4 or 1@Does the base match|Last form ends in -n? → 37 or 9@ends in n"
Ask the questions in this order, and stop at the first yes. Are all three forms the same? Are the past and the form after have the same? Does the base match one of the other two? If not, all three differ, so look at whether the last form ends in n.

@chapter The near-miss
@math heading="Three different forms" items="know / knew / known  ·  ends in n  ·  class of 37@Know, knew, known;;go / went / gone  ·  ends in e  ·  class of 9@go, went, gone"
Look at know, knew, known, and go, went, gone. Both have three different forms, so you might expect them in one class. But known ends in n and gone ends in e, and that one letter sends them to different classes.

@art near-miss
A rule that says exactly what it covers also says exactly what it doesn't. Go and do are two of the commonest verbs in English, and they sit in the class of nine, with undergo.

@chapter A vowel pattern
@math heading="i → a → u" items="sing / sang / sung@Sing, sang, sung;;drink / drank / drunk@Drink, drank, drunk;;also: ring, sink, spring, swim@Ring, sink"
The other six in the class of nine do something else. Sing, sang, sung. Drink, drank, drunk. Ring, sink, spring and swim do it too. The vowel moves from i, to a, to u. Know that shape, and you know six verbs.

@art vowel-steps
But the rule has a limit. Begin, began, begun has the same vowels, but begun ends in n, so it goes in the class of thirty-seven. The classes follow the last letter, not the sound.

@chapter What a class does not do
@words text="bring → brought · buy → bought" sub="same class, still two forms to learn"
It would be easy to say six patterns replace one hundred and eighty verbs. That says too much. A class tells you how many forms to hold. It doesn't tell you the letters. Bring and buy are in the same class, and you still have to know brought and bought.

@art error-zone
The classes do show where the commonest error can happen. Have went, have saw, have came: these put the past where the form after have belongs. Only a verb whose two forms differ can go wrong that way. That's fifty-one verbs. For the other eighty-one, it can't happen.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking the class to be the whole answer@The first|Putting go with know@The second|Treating a class of one as a rule@the third"
The first slip is taking the class to be the whole answer. It tells you the shape, not the forms. The second is putting go with know. Gone doesn't end in n. And the third is treating a class of one, like beat, as a rule. It's just a word to learn.

@chapter Try it
@words text="cut · cut · cut" sub="which class?"
Your turn. Cut, cut, cut. Which class is it in? Ask the first question: are all three forms the same? They are, so it's in the class of twenty-one, with put and hit.
