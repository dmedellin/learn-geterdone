@chapter The quick answer
@title text="How Much of English Is Irregular" sub="Irregular Verbs" art=thumb
How much of English is irregular? There's a quick answer, and it's the wrong place to stop. Let's count it, and then take it apart.

@art page-marked
Take nine hundred and thirty-six words of Pride and Prejudice. Check every word against the list of irregular verbs, in every form: the base, the past, the form after have, the form after he or she, and the ing form.

@math heading="936 words, counted on the page" items="149 irregular forms@one hundred and forty-nine;;149 ÷ 936 = 15.9%@fifteen point nine"
The count finds one hundred and forty-nine irregular forms. That's fifteen point nine percent of the words, about one word in six. It's a large share. But look at what it's made of.

@chapter Three verbs
@math heading="Be, have and do" items="91 of the 149  =  61.1%@ninety-one;;be alone: 49, and 20 of those are was@Be alone"
Be, have and do are ninety-one of the one hundred and forty-nine forms. That's sixty-one point one percent, about three in five. Be alone is forty-nine of them, and twenty of those are was.

@art three-big
Take the three out, and the other irregular verbs come to fifty-eight forms. That's six point two percent of the words, about one in sixteen. Still a fair share, but well under half the first figure.

@words text="be · have · do" sub="main verbs, and helping words"
Why do these three stand apart? They're not only main verbs. They're also the helping words that build the tenses, so they appear whether or not the sentence is about being, having or doing anything.

@chapter What a match proves
@words text="by no means" sub="counted as a form of mean"
The count matches spellings, not meanings. In this passage, one match isn't a verb at all. By no means is counted as a form of mean. So really, one hundred and forty-eight are irregular verb forms, fifteen point eight percent. The shape doesn't change.

@chapter The whole novel
@math heading="Whole novel, quoted" items="with be, have, do: 12.39% of words@twelve point three nine;;without them: 5.01% of words@five point zero one;;be, have, do: 59.5% of the irregular forms@fifty-nine point five"
The same count was made once over the whole novel, and these figures are quoted. With the three verbs, irregular forms are twelve point three nine percent of the words, about one in eight. Without them, five point zero one percent. And be, have and do are fifty-nine point five percent of the irregular forms.

@art same-shape
The passage and the book have the same shape. More than half the irregular forms are three verbs, and the share of words falls by more than half when they leave.

@chapter The top twenty
@math heading="Top twenty verbs cover, in the novel" items="with be, have, do: 87.66%@eighty-seven point six six;;without them: 73.90%@seventy-three point nine"
Now rank the irregular verbs in the novel, and add up the top twenty. With be, have and do in the list, they cover eighty-seven point six six percent of all irregular forms. Leave the three out and rank again. The top twenty cover seventy-three point nine percent.

@art twenty-workers
That first number is the one to distrust. Three of the twenty places belong to verbs that carry fifty-nine point five percent on their own. It's not twenty equal workers. It's three large ones and seventeen small ones. If you're deciding which verbs to learn, you want the second number.

@chapter Short page, long book
@math heading="Top twenty on the passage" items="with: 142 of 149  =  95.3%@one hundred and forty-two;;without: 54 of 58  =  93.1%@fifty-four"
On the passage, the top twenty cover one hundred and forty-two of the one hundred and forty-nine forms, and without the three, fifty-four of the fifty-eight. Both are higher than the novel's figures. A short page uses only twenty-seven different verbs, so twenty of them cover nearly everything.

@art short-vs-long
A whole novel meets many more of the one hundred and thirty-three verbs, and a long tail of them only once or twice each. So the same question gets a lower answer on a longer text, and the novel's figure is the better guide.

@chapter Ask four questions
@list heading="When you hear a share" items="Share of what?@Share of what|Which items are in the count?@Which items|Count again without the biggest@Count it again|Counted here, or quoted?@counted here"
So when someone gives you a share, ask four things. Share of what: words, or irregular forms? Which items are in the count? Count it again without the biggest items. And was it counted here, where you can check it, or quoted from somewhere else?

@art four-questions
And remember, all of this is one novel, from one writer. The exact figures would change in other texts. What should hold up is the shape: a few verbs carry a great deal, and a long tail carries a little.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Quoting 87.66% as if every slot counted@The first|Treating one old novel as English@The second|Reading a share of words as a count of verbs@the third"
The first slip is quoting eighty-seven point six six percent as if every slot counted. The second is treating one novel from the eighteen tens as all of English. And the third is reading fifteen point nine percent as if sixteen percent of verbs were irregular. A few verbs are just met again and again.
