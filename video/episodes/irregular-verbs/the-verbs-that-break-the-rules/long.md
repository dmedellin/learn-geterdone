@chapter The verbs set aside
@title text="The Verbs That Break the Rules" sub="Irregular Verbs" art=thumb
Four rules build the forms of almost every regular English verb. On twelve hundred and ten regular verbs, they were right more than ninety-nine times in a hundred.

@art set-aside
But before that score was taken, a set of verbs was put to one side, because no rule builds their past at all. Bring doesn't become bringed. Those verbs are what this course is about.

@chapter What irregular means
@math heading="Three forms" items="walk  ·  walked  ·  walked@For walk;;bring  ·  brought  ·  brought@Bring, brought;;know  ·  knew  ·  known@Know, knew"
Every verb has a base form, a past, and a form used after have. For walk, the past and the form after have are both walked, and the e-d rule builds them. But listen to these. Bring, brought, brought. Know, knew, known.

@words text="the -ed rule gives the wrong answer" sub="for the past, the form after have, or both"
A verb is irregular when the e-d rule gives the wrong answer for the past, for the form after have, or for both. No rule makes brought or known, and you can't work them out from the base.

@chapter How to test a verb
@list heading="Four steps" items="Write the three forms@write the three forms|Build the past by the -ed rule@build the past|Compare with what people write@compare your guess|Check the form after have on its own@check the third form"
Here's the test. First, write the three forms. Second, build the past by the e-d rule. That's your guess. Third, compare your guess with what people write. If they match, the verb is regular. Fourth, check the third form on its own.

@math heading="Walk, show and bring" items="walk:  guess walked, walked  ·  written walked, walked  ·  regular@Walk passes;;show:  guess showed, showed  ·  written showed, shown  ·  irregular@Show passes;;bring:  guess bringed, bringed  ·  written brought, brought  ·  irregular@Bring fails"
Walk passes both steps. Show passes the past, showed, but fails the form after have, which is shown. One failure is enough, so show is irregular. Bring fails both.

@art two-spellings
Notice what the test never asks. It doesn't ask how a verb sounds, or how common it is. It just compares two spellings. That's what lets the list be printed without anyone's opinion in it.

@chapter How many there are
@words text="133 verbs · 6 classes · and be" sub="the largest class has 60"
The lab holds one hundred and thirty-three irregular verbs. One hundred and thirty-two of them fall into six classes, and the biggest class has sixty. Be stands outside them all.

@words text="about 180" sub="Pinker's figure for the whole list"
Pinker, who has written about this at length, puts the whole list at about one hundred and eighty. It's a count that depends on where you draw the line, so read it as a size, not an exact total.

@chapter They are the common words
@math heading="183 verbs checked against the 2,809 commonest words" items="132 were in it  ·  72.1%@one hundred and thirty-two;;middle verb at rank 697.5@The middle verb;;44% in the top 500@forty-four percent"
A list of one hundred and eighty-three was checked against the twenty-eight hundred and nine most common words in English. One hundred and thirty-two were in it, seventy-two point one percent. The middle verb sits at rank six hundred and ninety-seven point five, and forty-four percent are in the top five hundred.

@art frequent-end
Irregular verbs gather at the frequent end of the list. The ones that weren't in it include weep, flee, creep, shed and forgive.

@chapter Why the common ones?
@art memory-whole
Here's Pinker's idea. It's an observation more than a proof. A word you meet thousands of times is stored in memory whole. It never has to be built, so the regular rule never gets the chance to replace it.

@art rare-drift
A rare verb works the other way. You meet it too rarely to store its past, so you build it each time, and what you build is the regular one. Over many years, rare irregular verbs tend to move toward e-d. The idea is a good reason, but don't lean on it too hard.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Calling a verb regular because the past looks fine@The first|Thinking went and was are mistakes that stuck@The second|Learning the list in alphabetical order@The third"
The first slip is calling show regular because showed looks fine. Any broken form makes a verb irregular. The second is thinking went and was are mistakes that stuck. They're very old forms that people used too often to change. The third is learning the list in the order of the letters, which hides its shapes.

@chapter Try it
@art three-forms
Your turn. Pick a verb you use every day. Build its guess, the base plus e-d, for both the past and the form after have. Then compare it with what people write. If the guess is wrong, the verb is on the list.
