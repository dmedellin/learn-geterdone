@chapter A count, not a feeling
@title text="How Much of a Page You Know" sub="Vocabulary and Reading" art=thumb
How much of a page do you know? Most people answer with a feeling. This lesson answers with a count.

@art page-check
The lab takes a printed text and asks one question of every word. Is it on the list? The list is the two thousand eight hundred headwords of the New General Service List. The answer is a share, called the coverage of the list on that text.

@art word-family-root
A headword is the word the list files the others under. Walk is the headword for walks, walked and walking. So the lab takes each form back to its headword, with the rules of the earlier courses run backwards. It takes off s, e-d and i-n-g, turns the short not back into not, and knows the irregular verbs.

@chapter Three steps of the list
@art three-steps
The list comes in three bands: the commonest thousand words, the next thousand, and the rest. Each step adds less than the one before, because the list puts the commonest words first.

@math heading="Coverage on three texts" items="the passage, 936 words  ·  85.0%  →  89.2%  →  91.0%@On the passage;;the play, 1,972 words  ·  79.5%  →  83.9%  →  85.9%@On the play;;two modern documents, 1,685 words  ·  76.0%  →  84.7%  →  87.3%@On the two modern"
On the passage, from Pride and Prejudice, the first thousand words cover eighty-five percent. The first two thousand cover eighty-nine point two, and the whole list, ninety-one. On the play, it's seventy-nine point five, then eighty-three point nine, then eighty-five point nine. On the two modern documents, it ends at eighty-seven point three.

@words text="the: 26 times in the passage" sub="every repeat counts"
Coverage counts every word, each time it appears. The passage has only three hundred and sixty-one different words. If the list has the, all twenty-six times that the appears count as known. That's why a short list of common words does so much.

@chapter Names
@art capital-mark
Names are different. Nobody looks a name up, so the lab sets them apart. A word the list lacks is a name if its capital letter doesn't start a sentence, like Darcy or Meryton. The capital is the whole test, so the lab can't tell a name from a word that happens to have one, like Lord, in Oh, Lord!

@math heading="Coverage with names" items="the passage  ·  94.3%@the passage;;the play  ·  94.6%@The play;;the modern documents  ·  92.7%@the modern documents"
With names set apart, the passage is covered ninety-four point three percent. The play, ninety-four point six. And the modern documents, ninety-two point seven. This is the fairer figure to hold against what a reader needs.

@chapter What a reader needs
@art gap-to-target
So what does a reader need? Nation, in two thousand six, puts the share for an easy read of a novel at ninety-eight percent. That's one unknown word in every fifty. All three texts fall short.

@math heading="Turn the gap into words" items="100 − 94.3 = 5.7@a hundred minus;;100 ÷ 5.7 ≈ 18@divided by;;about 1 word in 18  ·  at 98%: 1 in 50@one word in eighteen"
Here's how to turn the gap into words. Take the share from a hundred: a hundred minus ninety-four point three is five point seven. Then divide a hundred by what's left. A hundred divided by five point seven is about eighteen. So on the passage, about one word in eighteen may be one you don't know.

@art one-in-eighteen
One in eighteen, against one in fifty for an easy read. And the gap isn't a feeling about difficulty. It's a list of words you can read off the page.

@chapter The words that make the gap
@words text="affection · gallantry · parsonage · sermon" sub="the passage: 41 words not covered"
On the passage, the lab finds forty-one words that aren't covered, and they come in kinds. Words of feeling and manners, like affection and gallantry, from a novel of eighteen thirteen. And words of a country house and a church, like parsonage and sermon.

@words text="the play: darling · passionately" sub="the documents: words of law and population"
Each text lacks its own words. The play lacks words of feeling, such as darling and passionately. The modern documents lack the words of two trades, law and population counting. So sort the words into kinds before you learn them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="thinking 2,800 words is enough@The first|reading a share as different words@The second|taking one text's share for all@The third"
The first slip is thinking two thousand eight hundred words is enough to read a page. On these texts, it's below ninety-eight percent every time. The second is reading the share as a count of different words. It counts every repeat. And the third is taking the share for one text as the share for all of them.

@chapter Try it
@words text="500 words  ·  25 unknown" sub="does the reader reach 98%?"
Your turn. A text has five hundred words, and the reader doesn't know twenty-five of them. Do they reach ninety-eight percent? Pause and work it out. Twenty-five of five hundred is five percent, so the reader knows ninety-five. That's below ninety-eight.
