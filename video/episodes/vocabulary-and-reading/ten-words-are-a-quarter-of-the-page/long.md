@chapter Count the page
@title text="Ten Words Are a Quarter of the Page" sub="Vocabulary and Reading" art=thumb
The last lesson counted how much of a page a list covers. This one turns the question round. Forget the list, and count the page itself. Which words appear most, and how much of the page do they fill?

@art rank-bars
The lab counts every word of the text, ranks the different ones from the commonest down, and adds up the share as it goes. The passage has nine hundred and thirty-six words, and three hundred and sixty-one different ones.

@chapter The top ten
@math heading="The passage's top ten" items="1  I  30  ·  3.2%@commonest word is I;;2  she  29   3  you  27   4  and  26   5  the  26@Then she;;6  to  25   7  of  21   8  was  20@Then to;;9  it  19   10  that  17   ·   25.6%@and that"
The commonest word is I. It appears thirty times, three point two percent of the page. Then she, you, and, and the. Then to, of and was. And then it, and that. Down to rank ten, the share so far is twenty-five point six percent.

@math heading="Add the ten counts" items="30 + 29 + 27 + 26 + 26 + 25 + 21 + 20 + 19 + 17  =  240@Add the ten;;240 ÷ 936  =  25.6%@two hundred and forty of"
Add the ten counts: thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-seven, twenty-six, twenty-six, twenty-five, twenty-one, twenty, nineteen and seventeen. They make two hundred and forty. And two hundred and forty of nine hundred and thirty-six is twenty-five point six percent.

@art quarter-page
So ten words fill a quarter of the page. And and the both appear twenty-six times. The lab puts and first only because it comes first in the alphabet.

@chapter Ten, fifty, a hundred
@art growing-share
Three numbers sum up the shape. On the passage, the ten commonest words cover twenty-five point six percent. The fifty commonest cover fifty-four point five. And the hundred commonest cover sixty-seven point seven.

@math heading="Ten · fifty · a hundred" items="the passage  ·  25.6%  ·  54.5%  ·  67.7%@Compare the passage;;the play  ·  24.9%  ·  50.5%  ·  63.3%@On the play;;the modern documents  ·  24.7%  ·  47.3%  ·  60.6%@on the modern"
Compare the passage with the others. On the play, they cover twenty-four point nine, fifty point five and sixty-three point three. And on the modern documents, twenty-four point seven, forty-seven point three and sixty point six. The shapes are close. A quarter of a page is ten words, and about two thirds is a hundred.

@chapter What kind of word
@art frame-and-fill
Now sort the ten. Not one of them names a thing or an act. Three are pronouns for people: I, she and you. It stands for a thing. And and that join. The, to and of go in front of a noun or a verb. And was is the past of be.

@words text="was proud · was roused · there was a time" sub="was: a main verb here"
Don't call was a helping verb here. In this passage, not one of the twenty is followed by an i-n-g verb. It stands before a quality, was proud, a participle, was roused, or a thing, there was a time.

@words text="persuaded that · I find that" sub="that joins 14 times · points 3 times: on that point"
And that? In fourteen of its seventeen places, it ties a verb to what follows: persuaded that, I find that. In three, it points: on that point.

@chapter Where the subject shows
@art swap-text
Switch the text, and the grammar part stays while the subject part changes. In the play, the speakers' names, Cecily and Algernon, are in the ten, mostly because the name is printed before each speech. In the modern documents, age is in the ten, because a report on an older population says it again and again.

@chapter The rest of the page
@art long-tail
After the hundred, the list falls steeply. The other two hundred and sixty-one different words share the last thirty-two point three percent. Many appear only once. These are the words that say what the page is about.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="thinking the commonest words carry the meaning@The first|reading the share as words learned@The second|taking one text's top for every text@The third"
The first slip is thinking the commonest words are the ones to learn, because they carry the meaning. They carry the grammar, and you've almost certainly met them all. The second is reading the share as a count of words learned. And the third is taking the top of one text for the top of every text.

@chapter Try it
@words text="I · you · Cecily · the · to · of · Algernon · a · it · is" sub="the play's top ten: which are its own?"
Your turn. Here's the play's top ten: I, you, Cecily, the, to, of, Algernon, a, it and is. Which ones belong to this text's own subject? Pause and sort them. Cecily and Algernon, the two names. The other eight are grammar words.
