@chapter The brown table
@title text="Colour and Lighting" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 3" art=thumb
The table in the room is brown, and stays brown, under daylight, lamplight and dusk. So what is its real colour?

@art brown-table
The empiricist says it's the colour the table has most often, in daylight and close to. Under coloured light I substitute a remembered colour. Hering called it memory colour. But a white wall in shade beside a grey paper in the light doesn't stay white. The paper is the lighter.

@words text="colour in living perception is a way into the thing"
A judgement that discounted the lighting fares no better, because the colour doesn't in fact stay identical. Both theories recognize only fixed qualities seen in a reflective attitude, whereas colour in living perception is a way into the thing.

@chapter Modes of colour
@art window-screen
David Katz showed that colour comes in modes. A coloured area is spongy and vaguely placed. A surface colour is dense, holding the gaze. Look at a dimly lit white wall through a screen with a small window, and it turns bluish grey. The screen removes the articulation of the whole field.

@art two-boxes
Adhemar Gelb looked into two boxes, one black inside and strongly lit, one white and faintly lit, sending the eye the same light. They can't be told apart. Put a white paper in the black box, and at once it appears black and strongly lit.

@chapter Gelb's disc
@quote text="If we so arrange it that the beam of an arc lamp falls exactly upon a black disc, and if the latter is set in motion to eliminate the influence of the roughness which is always present on its surface—the disc appears, like the rest of the room, faintly lighted, and the beam of light is a whitish solid with the disc as its base." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, chapter 3, p. 358"
Now the passage. A hidden arc lamp beam falls exactly on a black, spinning disc. The disc appears faintly lighted, like the rest of the room, and the beam is a whitish solid with the disc as its base.

@chapter The lab's luminance
@math heading="Luminance = reflectance × illumination" items="paper in the room: 4/5 × 1 = 4/5@white paper;;disc in the room: 1/20 × 1 = 1/20@the disc;;disc in the beam: 1/20 × 16 = 4/5@sixteen"
The lab uses the empiricist's model, luminance as reflectance times illumination, with illustrative figures. White paper reflects four fifths, so in the room its luminance is four fifths. The disc reflects one twentieth. Put it in a beam of sixteen, and one twentieth times sixteen is four fifths, exactly the paper's luminance.

@art paper-in-beam
If the light a surface sends were its colour, the disc would now have the paper's colour. Then put a scrap of white paper in the beam, in front of the disc. The disc is seen black, the paper white, and both strongly lit, as if it were a fresh disc.

@math heading="Ratio to the surround" items="disc in the beam: still 4/5@still four fifths;;paper in the beam: 4/5 × 16 = 64/5@sixty four;;disc / wall: (4/5) / (1/2) = 8/5@over a half;;disc / paper: (4/5) / (64/5) = 1/16@one sixteenth"
But the disc's luminance is still four fifths. The paper's is four fifths times sixteen, sixty four fifths. Against the wall, the disc's ratio was four fifths over a half, eight fifths. Against the paper it's one sixteenth. Only the surround changed.

@words text="Wallach, 1948: lightness follows the ratio" sub="the lesson's addition, not the chapter's"
Hans Wallach's ratio principle, from three years after the book, predicts the disc black beside the paper. Luminance can't. But the chapter goes further: the paper makes the cone of light stop looking solid, and the lighting takes on the quality of lighting. The whole is re read.

@chapter What lighting is
@art eye-reflection
Painters took centuries to see the reflection on the eye, without which a face looks dull and sightless. It went unnoticed and still did its work. It is not seen itself, but causes us to see the rest. The lighting leads my gaze, like a guide through a strange flat.

@art shady-corner
A white disc in a shady corner of a bright room is imperfectly white, and improves as you approach the shade. Yellow electric light looks yellow when you come in, then has no colour at all, so a remnant of daylight looks blue.

@words text="The lighting is not on the side of the object, it is what we assume, what we take as the norm"
The lighting is not on the side of the object. It's what we assume, what we take as the norm. And the blue of a carpet wouldn't be the same blue if it weren't a woolly blue. Colour constancy is part of the constancy of things.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The disc's colour is the light it sends@the light it sends|The ratio is the chapter's explanation@the ratio|Lighting is one more colour in the scene@one more colour"
Don't say the disc's colour is the light it sends; that stayed four fifths. Don't take the ratio for the chapter's explanation; it's Wallach's. And don't read lighting as one more colour in the scene that the eye subtracts.
