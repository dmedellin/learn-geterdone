@chapter Footprints in the sand
@title text="The Cultural World and Others" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 4" art=thumb
Chapter four begins with the things people leave behind them. A spoon, a pipe, a bell, a road. Each is moulded to the action it serves.

@art footprints
A few footprints in the sand carry a faint atmosphere of humanity. A house just abandoned, gone through room by room, carries a very definite one. Someone uses the pipe for smoking, and the bell for summoning. How can a thought be seen outside the person who thinks it?

@words text="The very first of all cultural objects … is the body of the other person"
The easy answer is analogy: I interpret what others do by my own behaviour, whose meaning I know from inside. But how can the word I be put in the plural at all? The very first of all cultural objects is the body of the other person.

@chapter No room for others
@art two-modes
For objective thought, the existence of others is a difficulty and an outrage. There are two modes of being, things in space and consciousness. Another person would have to be both at once: an object over there, and a consciousness I could reach only by being it.

@words text="If my consciousness has a body, why should other bodies not “have” consciousnesses?"
The way out is the book's account of the body, a third kind of being between pure subject and object. If my consciousness has a body, why should other bodies not have consciousnesses?

@chapter The argument from analogy
@quote text="There is nothing here resembling ‘reasoning by analogy’. As Scheler so rightly declares, reasoning by analogy presupposes what it is called on to explain. … Now the perception of others is anterior to, and the condition of, such observations" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, chapter 4, p. 410"
The argument compares the other's expressions of emotion with mine. Max Scheler objected that this reasoning presupposes what it's called on to explain, and the chapter agrees. The perception of others is anterior to, and the condition of, such observations.

@art baby-bite
Here's the example. Take a fifteen month old baby's finger between your teeth and pretend to bite, and the baby opens its mouth. It's hardly seen its own face, and its teeth aren't like yours. For it, biting has an intersubjective significance at once. There was no resemblance to reason from.

@chapter The lab's truth tables
@math heading="Sentence letters" items="p: my behaviour expresses a mind@p is;;q: that body behaves as mine does@q that;;r: that body's behaviour expresses a mind@r that"
The lab writes the argument in letters. p is: my behaviour expresses a mind. q, that body behaves as mine does. And r, that body's behaviour expresses a mind.

@math heading="Three versions" items="p, q ∴ r: 8 rows, 1 counterexample → invalid@eight rows;;p, q, q → r ∴ r: 0 counterexamples → valid@if q then r;;p, r ∴ r: 4 rows, valid, circular@circle"
From p and q, conclude r. Eight rows, one counterexample: p and q true, r false. Invalid, but Mill meant it as an induction anyway. Add the premise if q then r, and there are no counterexamples, modus ponens. Now the circle: premises p and r, conclusion r. Valid too, over four rows.

@words text="To know q, I must already perceive conduct" sub="a circular argument is perfectly valid"
That's the chapter's objection in the lab's terms. To know that the body over there behaves as mine does, I must already perceive it as someone's conduct, which is what r says. And no truth table catches that, because a circular argument is perfectly valid.

@chapter A vortex round the body
@art vortex
What takes analogy's place is an internal relation between my body as I live it and the other's body as I see it. As soon as my gaze falls on a living body in action, the things around it take on new meaning, and a kind of vortex forms, drawing my world towards it.

@art meteorites
The child finds cultural objects around it like meteorites from another planet, and learns to use them because its body schema makes what it sees done match at once what it does.

@art dialogue
Language matters most. In dialogue a common ground forms. My thoughts and the other's are woven into one fabric, and his objection draws from me thoughts I didn't know I had.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="It fails only because it's inductive@only because|The chapter calls other minds an inference after all@after all|Two modes of being is the chapter's view@two modes"
Don't say the argument fails only because it's inductive; the repair is valid and the objection survives. Don't conclude the chapter calls other minds an inference after all. And don't read the two modes of being as the chapter's view. They're objective thought's terms.
