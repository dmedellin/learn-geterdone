@chapter Down the corridor
@title text="Size and Shape Constancy" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 3" art=thumb
Someone you know walks away down a long corridor, turns at the end and waves. The angle they fill at your eye has fallen to a fraction of what it was. And they haven't, for you, become a small person.

@art corridor-wave
That's a perceptual constancy, and the chapter on the thing begins with it. We don't attribute the changing appearances to the thing, but to our relations with it. So on what basis do we judge that a size or a shape is the object's own?

@chapter Two answers
@words text="how can there be objectivity?"
The psychologist's answer: it's merely conventional to count as true the size a thing has within reach. The chapter objects that this assumes what needed explaining, a range of determinate sizes. The question is how any determinate size is given at all. How can there be objectivity?

@art fountain-pen
The second answer: the true size is the law by which the appearance varies with distance. Hold a fountain pen up to your eye so it hides the whole scene, and it stays small, because it's also a pen seen close to. Followed out, this leads with Kant to a world as a strictly related system.

@words text="the constant product of the distance multiplied by the apparent size"
For science and objective thought, then, the object is the constant product of the distance multiplied by the apparent size. And that's something the lab can compute.

@chapter The lab's two predictions
@math heading="170 cm person at 8 m, standard 2 m" items="retinal-equivalent: 170 × 2/8 = 85/2 cm@retinal image;;inferred: (85/2) × 8/2 = 170 cm@inference;;assumed 4 m: (85/2) × 4/2 = 85 cm@four metres"
A person a hundred and seventy centimetres tall, eight metres off, standard distance two. The retinal image account gives a hundred and seventy times two eighths, eighty five over two centimetres: a quarter of their height. The inference account multiplies back, eighty five over two times eight over two, a hundred and seventy. Assume four metres instead, and it gives eighty five.

@math heading="Brunswik's ratio, report 160 cm" items="(160 − 85/2) / (170 − 85/2)@a hundred and sixty minus;;= 47/51 ≈ 92%@forty seven"
The figures are illustrative, and so is a reported size of a hundred and sixty. Brunswik's ratio, which the lesson adds, places it: a hundred and sixty minus eighty five over two, over a hundred and seventy minus eighty five over two. That's forty seven over fifty one, about ninety two per cent. Neither model predicts it.

@chapter The chapter's reply
@quote text="For science and objective thought, an apparently small object seen a hundred yards away is indistinguishable from the same object seen ten yards away at a greater angle … But for me the perceiver, the object a hundred yards away is not real and present in the sense in which it is at ten yards" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, chapter 3, p. 351"
The table in my room isn't a rule governing a parade of appearances. It's because I perceive the table with its definite size that I expect each change of distance to change its appearance. For me the perceiver, the object a hundred yards away isn't present as it is at ten.

@art gallery-picture
Why not? For each object, as for each picture in a gallery, there's an optimum distance from which it requires to be seen, and a direction from which it shows most of itself. Nearer or farther, the perception blurs by excess or by lack.

@art skin-puppet
A patch of skin under a magnifying glass is as strange as a lunar landscape. Seen too far, a living body is a puppet. It appears when its detail is neither too visible nor too little, and that fixes its real size and shape.

@words text="a tension which fluctuates round a norm"
So the distance from me to the thing isn't a size that grows and shrinks. It's a tension which fluctuates round a norm.

@chapter Shape
@art tilted-coin
Shape works the same way. Tilt a coin so the cosine of the tilt is three fifths, and it projects as an ellipse with axes one and three fifths. But an oblique position isn't measured by an angle. It's felt as a lack of balance, an unequal pull on me.

@art horizon-moon
When I turn a thing in my fingers, each attitude of my body is the power to get a certain view. Koffka reported constancy is far more perfect horizontally than vertically, and the huge horizon moon is a case of it. For apes, who climb trees, vertical constancy is faultless.

@words text="I am not the spectator, I am involved"
So the chapter keeps Kant's point and moves it. A real size does refer to a system of experience. But that system isn't spread before me as if I were God. I am not the spectator, I am involved.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The real size is inferred from the projection@inferred|The retinal-equivalent size is what is seen@what's seen|The optimum distance is a convention@a convention"
Don't say the real size is inferred from the projected one. Don't read the retinal equivalent as what's seen; it's geometry. And don't take the optimum distance for a convention. It's where the thing's detail is neither too much nor too little visible.
