@chapter Not a crystal cube
@title text="The Natural World" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 3" art=thumb
The chapter turns from the thing to the world it belongs to. And it begins by saying what the world is not.

@art crystal-cube
It isn't a system of invariable relations, as Kant would mean it. The world isn't like a crystal cube, whose every side can be worked out from its geometry and whose hidden sides show through. It has its unity even where the mind hasn't fitted its facets into one projection.

@chapter A style
@words text="I experience the unity of the world as I recognize a style."
What kind of unity, then? The unity of a person I recognize before I could ever state the formula of their character, because they keep the same style in everything they do. I experience the unity of the world as I recognize a style.

@art friend-district
After ten years of friendship I seem to be dealing with a different person. After ten years in a district, it seems another. Yet only my knowledge of things has changed. The world stays the same world, because it's where I make all my corrections.

@art city-roar
I may be mistaken, and need to rearrange my certainties. But I don't for a moment doubt that things in themselves have been compatible. The world persists on the horizon of my life, as the distant roar of a great city lies behind everything we do in it.

@chapter Chartres
@art chartres-road
The world does come in outlines. And sometimes they come apart. I drive towards a town and look only now and then, and I'm faced with an object with no common measure with the one before. I finally pass the judgement: it is Chartres.

@words text="“It is Chartres”" sub="both drawn from one perception of the world"
I weld the two appearances together. But the chapter's point comes next. I can do so only because both are drawn from one and the same perception of the world.

@chapter The sceptic's model

@chapter The reply to the sceptic
@words text="disappears only to give place to another perception which rectifies it"
Descartes gave the sceptic's strongest form: the senses sometimes deceive, so never trust them wholly. The chapter grants each perception can be cancelled. But each one disappears only to give place to another perception which rectifies it. What's certain is that there's a world.

@quote text="The natural world is the horizon of all horizons, the style of all possible styles, which guarantees for my experiences a given, not a willed, unity underlying all the disruptions of my personal and historical life." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, chapter 3, p. 385"
Now the passage reads plainly. The natural world is the horizon of all horizons, the style of all possible styles. Its unity is given, not willed. I don't make it hold together by deciding to.

@art next-room
My present view isn't shut within my visual field. The next room, the land behind the hill, the inside of an object, aren't recalled or pictured. They're already there. The bright green of a Cezanne vase doesn't make me think of pottery. It presents it.

@chapter Here and elsewhere
@art white-stone
But if no view is complete, how is the world one? The contradiction holds only while we think in terms of being. Think in terms of time, and the synthesis of horizons is a process. A white, hard, cool stone seems to need no time. Such are the world and time of Descartes.

@words text="here and also elsewhere" sub="not a defect of consciousness, but its definition"
If I could survey the world from no point of view, I'd be at no time and nowhere, and nothing would exist for me. The ambiguity of being here and also elsewhere isn't a defect of consciousness. It's its definition.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Any perception may be an illusion, so all may be@so all may|The world is the largest thing@largest thing|The mystery is an unsolved problem@unsolved problem"
Don't say any perception may be an illusion, so all may be. Don't read the world as the largest thing; it's the horizon of things. And don't hear its mystery as an unsolved problem. It's not of the order where there are solutions.
