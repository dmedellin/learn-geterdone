@chapter What holds a thing together?
@title text="The Thing and the Real" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 3" art=thumb
Having read the constancies sense by sense, the chapter turns to the thing that unites them. What makes a colour, a feel, a sound and a smell one thing?

@art gust-of-wind
A thing's qualities make one thing as my senses are powers of one body in one action. Something that strikes only one sense, a reflection or a light gust, is a mere phantom. The wind comes close to real existence when it blows hard enough to be seen in the tumult of the countryside.

@art cezanne-landscape
Cezanne said a picture contains even the smell of the landscape. A thing wouldn't have this colour if it didn't also have this shape, this feel, this sound and this smell.

@chapter Not a vacant X
@quote text="The unity of the thing beyond all its fixed properties is not a substratum, a vacant X, a subject in which properties inhere, but that unique accent which is to be found in each one of them, that unique manner of existing of which they are a second order expression." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, chapter 3, p. 372"
Here's the passage. The unity of the thing isn't a substratum, a vacant X, a subject in which properties inhere. It's a unique accent found in each of them, a unique manner of existing of which they're a second order expression.

@art hot-red
The glass's brittleness, hardness, transparency and ring translate a single manner of being. Heat comes as a vibration of the thing, colour as the thing thrust outside itself. So it is a priori necessary that an extremely hot object should redden.

@chapter Two accounts
@list heading="Two accounts" items="Empiricism: a sum of associated qualities@For empiricism|Intellectualism: a unity posited by thought@For intellectualism"
The two accounts set aside are the Introduction's two. For empiricism, the thing is a sum of qualities joined by association, because they've often come together. For intellectualism, it's a unity posited by thought, the concept the appearances are brought under.

@chapter The lab's table
@list heading="Three observations" items="A very hot object must redden: n, y@must redden|The carpet's colour implies its wool: n, n@wool|The thing rests aloof in itself: y, n@aloof"
The lab codes three observations, empiricism first. A very hot object must redden: association can't carry a necessary link, but a concept can. The carpet's colour implies its wool: both fail. And the thing rests aloof in itself: empiricism's things exist without us, but a unity posited by thought loses its opacity.

@math heading="All preset" items="each: 1 absorbed, 2 failed@each account;;survivors: none@survivors;;split: the hot object and the aloof thing@split"
So each account absorbs one and fails two. Survivors: none. The split, where they part, is the hot object and the aloof thing. Show only the aloof thing, and empiricism survives. A record depends on which observations are on the table.

@chapter The woolly carpet
@art woolly-carpet
The carpet is the one both fail. It is impossible completely to describe the colour of the carpet without saying it's a carpet, made of wool. For the empiricist the colour and the woolliness are two qualities. For the intellectualist, the wool is in the concept. But the colour as seen is already woolly.

@chapter In itself, for us
@words text="a genuine in-itself-for-us"
We can't conceive anything perceived without someone to perceive it, and yet the thing presents itself as a thing in itself. The chapter calls it a genuine in itself for us, and that names the problem, not a solution.

@art silent-stone
Pay a thing disinterested attention, and it becomes hostile and alien, a resolutely silent Other. And the real differs from a picture. Tear up a picture and you hold pieces of daubed canvas. Break a stone, and break the pieces again, and they're still stone.

@art die-reductions
Then the die. Reflection reduces it: a thing for me, a shell of colour, a set of perspective views, at last a sensation in my body. But the experience of the thing doesn't go through these steps. Anything built from them is a reconstruction.

@words text="In order to perceive things, we need to live them."
Realism can't say what perceiving is. Idealism makes the thing transparent and takes away its thinghood. In order to perceive things, we need to live them. I perceive with my body, and the whole body conforms to a logic of the world.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The thing is its qualities, bound by the mind@bound by the mind|The aloof thing is beyond perception@beyond perception|The die's reductions are the order of perception@order of perception"
Don't say the thing is its qualities bound by the mind; that joins both rejected accounts. Don't read the aloof thing as beyond perception; aloofness is something it shows. And don't take the die's reductions for the order of perception.
