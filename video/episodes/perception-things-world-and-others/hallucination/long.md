@chapter The real comes apart
@title text="Hallucination" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 3" art=thumb
The chapter ends with hallucination, which takes the real apart and puts a quasi reality in its place. And in doing so, it confirms what the chapter has said about the thing and the world.

@words text="the patients, most of the time, discriminate between their hallucinations and their perceptions"
The evidence is the testimony of patients, as the psychiatrists of the time reported them. And the all important point is that the patients, most of the time, discriminate between their hallucinations and their perceptions.

@chapter Telling them apart
@art garden-window
A man said he saw someone standing in the garden under his window. A real person was placed at the spot, dressed and standing the same way. He said: yes, there is someone there, but it's somebody else.

@art two-powders
An old woman complained of powder in her bed. She found a real layer of toilet powder there, and asked what it was. This powder was damp. The other was dry. Patients often say the voices come by telephone or radio, a way of saying their world is artificial.

@chapter Not a sensation, not a belief
@words text="a perception with an inner cause?"
The empiricist says some irritation of a nerve centre produces sense data, just as a stimulus would. A hallucination is a perception with an inner cause. But then the patients couldn't tell the two apart. And they can.

@art rowing-out
So is it a false belief? The patients don't believe they see. They argue against their hallucinations. A doctor who heard voices rowed out to sea to convince himself no one was speaking. When the attack comes, the voices are still there. Either account would posit the hallucination as true, which is what the patients don't do.

@chapter No path to the rest
@quote text="The victim of hallucination enjoys no such belief: the hallucinatory phenomenon is no part of the world, that is to say, it is not accessible, there is no definite path leading from it to all the remaining experiences of the deluded subject, or to the experience of the sane." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, chapter 3, p. 395"
What I see is the only possible spectacle, for me and for others. The hallucinatory phenomenon is no part of the world. It's not accessible. There's no definite path leading from it to the rest of the patient's experience, or to anyone else's.

@words text="“Can’t you hear my voices?” … “then I must be the only one to hear them.”"
Can't you hear my voices, asks the patient. Then I must be the only one to hear them.

@art packed-detail
And the hallucinatory thing is not, as the real thing is, packed with small perceptions which sustain it in existence. It has no depth of duration. With a perceived thing, it answers me from outside. With a phantasm, the initiative comes from me.

@chapter The lab's core
@math heading="All preset" items="perceived: voice next door, person in the garden, damp powder@three perceived;;core: shared world, explorable, detail, duration, real for me@the core;;varies: heard, seen, touched@varies;;dry powder lacks: the other four@dry powder"
The lab codes three perceived things beside the dry powder. The core is five properties: in the shared world, explorable, packed with detail, thick with duration, and real for me. What varies is the sense: heard, seen, touched. The dry powder has only real for me, and lacks the other four.

@art no-path
So what every perceived thing has, on this coding, isn't a sense. It's a place in one world, and that's what the hallucination lacks. The coding is the lesson's reading, and a reader could code it differently.

@chapter The value of reality
@words text="The hallucination is not a perception, but it has the value of reality"
Yet a woman who felt a gaze fall on her at the market like a blow was unmoved by every argument. The hallucination is not a perception, but it has the value of reality. Both are modes of one primordial function by which we set up a world around us.

@art primary-faith
The normal person has the same wound through which illusion enters. We believe what we see without checking, on a primary faith Husserl called Urdoxa. So perception has no certainty of proof. But each perception is replaced only by another that corrects it, and what's certain is that there's a world.

@chapter A modern aside
@words text="perception as controlled hallucination?" sub="Anil Seth, Being You, 2021"
The neuroscientist Anil Seth describes perception as a controlled hallucination, the brain's best guess held in check by its signals. That runs the other way from the chapter, which says a hallucination lacks what perception has. Both sides can point to the page. Neither the chapter nor the lab settles it.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="A hallucination is experienced exactly like a perception@exactly like|It's a false belief@false belief|Perception is infallible@infallible"
Don't say a hallucination is experienced exactly like a perception; the patients tell them apart. Don't call it a false belief; they argue against it. And don't read the chapter as saying perception is infallible.
