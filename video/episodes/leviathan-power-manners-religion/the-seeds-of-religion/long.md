@chapter Only in man
@title text="The Natural Seeds of Religion" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 12" art=thumb
Hobbes wrote in a Europe that had spent more than a century killing over religion. So he asks where it comes from, the way you might ask where a river comes from.

@art man-and-beast
There's no sign of religion except in man, so its seed must be something peculiar to man. Hobbes finds three things. Man is curious about causes. When he sees something begin, he thinks a cause fixed the moment. And unlike beasts, he remembers how one event led to another, so he looks ahead.

@chapter Prometheus
@art prometheus
Looking ahead is the trouble. A man always guarding against the evil he fears lives in perpetual worry about the time to come. Hobbes compares him to Prometheus, the prudent man, chained to a mountain with an eagle eating his liver every day.

@words text="The Gods were at first created by humane Feare"
That fear must have some object. When nothing can be seen, there's nothing to blame for good or bad luck but some invisible power. So perhaps, as the old poets said, the gods were first created by human fear. Of the many pagan gods, Hobbes says, that's very true.

@art first-mover
The one eternal God is reached another way, by following causes back, one before another, to a first mover.

@chapter The four seeds
@quote text="And in these foure things, Opinion of Ghosts, Ignorance of second causes, Devotion towards what men fear, and Taking of things Casuall for Prognostiques, consisteth the Naturall seed of Religion" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 12"
And in these four things, opinion of ghosts, ignorance of second causes, devotion towards what men fear, and taking of things casual for prognostics, consisteth the natural seed of religion.

@art mirror-ghost
Take them in turn. The first is the thought that invisible agents are spirits, thin bodies like the image in a dream, or the face in the looking glass.

@list heading="Four seeds" items="Ghosts: thin bodies, like the face in a mirror@Ghosts|Ignorance of second causes@second causes|Devotion toward what men fear@devotion|Chance events taken for signs@chance events"
Ghosts, then. Ignorance of second causes, so luck gets pinned on a bystander or a lucky place. Devotion toward what men fear: gifts, petitions, thanks. And chance events taken for signs.

@words text="After one or two encounters, ever after"
That last one is an observation about evidence, and a sharp one. Men guess the future from the past, which is just prudence. But they take casual things, after one or two encounters, for signs of the like encounter ever after.

@chapter How far should one case move you?
@art crow-on-roof
The lab asks how far one or two cases should move a prudent man. The sign is a crow on the roof before news. Either it foretells the news, or it's unconnected. These are the lesson's numbers, not Hobbes's.

@math heading="One encounter" items="prior: 1/10@one in ten;;foretells: 1/10 · 9/10 = 9/100@nine tenths;;unconnected: 9/10 · 1/2 = 45/100@half the time;;credence: 9/54 = 1/6@one sixth;;Bayes factor: 9/5@nine fifths"
Before any case, he gives the sign one in ten. If it foretells, the news follows nine tenths of the time. If not, the news follows half the time anyway. The news follows once. His credence goes to nine fifty fourths, one sixth. The Bayes factor is nine fifths.

@math heading="Two encounters" items="credence: 9/34@nine thirty fourths;;Bayes factor: 81/25@eighty one"
It follows twice, and the credence reaches nine thirty fourths, a little over a quarter, with a factor of eighty one twenty fifths. Two coincidences make the sign a better bet than before, and still far from ever after.

@art likely-anyway
Now suppose the event follows nine times in ten whether or not there's a sign. Then the factor is one. Seeing it follow tells you nothing, and the credence stays at one in ten. The sign was there before the event, and so was everything else.

@chapter What the sum shows
@words text="A claim about men · a rule of evidence"
Keep two things apart. Hobbes's claim is about men: they leap from one or two cases to always. The lab's sum is a rule of evidence he didn't have, published after his death. It doesn't show men do otherwise, or that religion is nothing but this mistake. Hobbes names three other seeds.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking Hobbes for a Bayesian@The first|Counting the hits, forgetting the base rate@The second|Two coincidences settle it@And the third"
The first slip is taking Hobbes for a Bayesian. The sum is the lesson's. The second is counting the sign's hits and forgetting how often the event comes anyway. And the third is thinking two coincidences settle it, when they only move one in ten to nine thirty fourths.
