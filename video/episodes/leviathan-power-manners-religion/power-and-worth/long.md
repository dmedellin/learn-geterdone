@chapter What can you do?
@title text="Power and Worth" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 10" art=thumb
What are you worth? Hobbes gives an answer most people find uncomfortable.

@art interview-table
Think of a job interview. You list what you can do now, and the interviewer wonders what that will fetch. That's more or less the shape of Hobbes's definition of power.

@quote text="The POWER of a Man, (to take it Universally,) is his present means, to obtain some future apparent Good." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 10"
The power of a man, to take it universally, is his present means to obtain some future apparent good. Present means: what you have in hand today. An apparent good: one that seems good to whoever wants it. And universally: for any man, before any commonwealth exists.

@chapter Two kinds of power
@list heading="Two kinds" items="Natural: strength, form, prudence, arts, eloquence, nobility@Natural power|Instrumental: riches, reputation, friends, good luck@instrumental"
Natural power is the eminence of body or mind: strength, good looks, prudence, skill, eloquence, and nobility, where it has privileges. Instrumental powers are acquired by those, or by fortune, and are means to get more: riches, reputation, friends, and what men call good luck.

@art snowball
Power, Hobbes says, is like fame, increasing as it goes, like a falling body that speeds up. Money buys friends, friends bring protection, protection lets you earn more. Keep that image. Chapter eleven turns it into a claim that nobody stops.

@art united-strengths
Then a tour of what counts. Friends are power, for they're strengths united. Riches count only with liberality. Otherwise they just expose a man to envy, as prey. And the greatest human power is the power of most men united by consent in one person: a commonwealth, or a faction.

@chapter Worth is price
@quote text="The Value, or WORTH of a man, is as of all other things, his Price; that is to say, so much as would be given for the use of his Power:" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 10"
Now worth. The value, or worth, of a man is, as of all other things, his price: that is to say, so much as would be given for the use of his power. So it isn't absolute. It depends on the need and judgement of another.

@art general-and-judge
His own examples. An able leader of soldiers is of great price when war is present or near, but in peace not so. A learned, honest judge is worth much in peace, but not so much in war. The men didn't change. The buyer's need did.

@words text="Not the seller, but the buyer determines the price"
And the sentence that does the most work: not the seller, but the buyer determines the price. Rate yourself as high as you like. Your true value is no more than others esteem it.

@chapter Pricing four men
@art balance-price
The lab turns this into a sum. Four invented men each hold some amount of four powers. A buyer's price for a man is the buyer's need for each power times the man's amount of it, all added up.

@math heading="War buyer: 3, 2, 1, 1" items="general (8, 5, 2, 1): 3·8 + 2·5 + 1·2 + 1·1 = 37@the general;;judge (1, 8, 4, 2): 3·1 + 2·8 + 1·4 + 1·2 = 25@the judge"
A city at war needs strength three, prudence two, eloquence one, riches one. The general has eight, five, two and one, so he's worth thirty seven. The judge has one, eight, four and two, so he's worth twenty five.

@math heading="Peace buyer: 0, 3, 2, 1" items="general: 0·8 + 3·5 + 2·2 + 1·1 = 20@the general;;judge: 0·1 + 3·8 + 2·4 + 1·2 = 34@the judge"
A city at peace needs no strength, prudence three, eloquence two, riches one. Now the general is worth twenty, and the judge thirty four. War put the general twelve above the judge. Peace puts the judge fourteen above the general. Neither buyer is mistaken.

@art three-podiums
Add a faction hunting for support, and the orator tops its list at thirty. Three buyers, three different men on top. And the merchant, never best, is priced twenty four by every one. Shift the war buyer's need toward cool heads, and the general falls from thirty seven to twenty one, while the judge, at twenty three, comes out on top.

@chapter Price and dignity
@words text="A person has a dignity above all price" sub="Kant, 1785"
There's a fair objection. In seventeen eighty five, Kant wrote that whatever has a price can be replaced by an equivalent, while a person has a dignity above all price. Hobbes, for his part, defines a separate word, dignity, for the value the commonwealth sets. That's the next lesson.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Worth is a quality in the man@The first|Only strength and skill count as power@The second|A high price is a verdict on the man@And the third"
The first slip is thinking worth is a quality in the man, the same for everyone. The second is counting only strength and skill as power, when riches and friends count too. And the third is reading a high price as a verdict on the man. A price only says what a buyer needs.
