@chapter A buyer whose price binds
@title text="Honour, Dignity and Worthiness" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Last time the buyers were private: a city, a faction. Now the buyer is the commonwealth itself, and it can set a value on a man that nobody's need would give him, with a stroke of the pen.

@art title-ribbon
Think of a title or a rank. It costs the man nothing to be addressed by it, and it changes what everyone around him does. Hobbes sorts four words ordinary speech runs together: honour, dignity, worth, and worthiness.

@chapter Honour is a sign
@quote text="The manifestation of the Value we set on one another, is that which is commonly called Honouring, and Dishonouring." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 10"
First honour. The manifestation of the value we set on one another is that which is commonly called honouring and dishonouring. It's measured against the rate each man sets on himself. Praise him at exactly that rate, and you've neither honoured nor insulted him.

@list heading="Ways of honouring" items="To pray to another for aid@To pray|To obey@to obey|To give great gifts@great gifts|To give way, agree, imitate, trust@to give way"
The list has one logic. To pray to another for aid honours him, a sign you think he can help. To obey honours, because nobody obeys those who can't help or hurt. Great gifts honour, little gifts are alms. To give way, agree, imitate, trust: all honours.

@words text="Honour consists only in the opinion of power"
So honourable is whatever is a sign of power. And whether an act was just or unjust doesn't alter it. Piracy and duels were honoured. Hobbes is describing what men honour, not what they should.

@chapter The king's robe
@art kings-robe
His best scene is from the Book of Esther. The King of Persia honoured Mordecai by having him led through the streets in the king's garment, on the king's horse, a crown on his head, a prince before him crying, thus shall it be done to him that the king will honour.

@art robe-of-fool
Another king, asked by a man for leave to wear a royal robe, allowed it, but as the king's fool. And then it was dishonour. The same robe. What made the difference was what the sovereign meant by it.

@art fountain
So Hobbes draws the moral. Of civil honour, the fountain is in the person of the commonwealth, and it depends on the will of the sovereign.

@chapter Dignity
@quote text="The publique worth of a man, which is the Value set on him by the Common-wealth, is that which men commonly call DIGNITY." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 10"
The public worth of a man, which is the value set on him by the commonwealth, is that which men commonly call dignity. It's shown by offices of command, judging, public employment, and by names and titles.

@math heading="Dignity against price" items="general: 40 vs 37, 20, 16 → above all@the general;;judge: 34 vs 25, 34, 17 → equal to peace@the judge;;orator: 10 vs 19, 28, 30 → below all@the orator;;merchant: 15 vs 24 → below@merchant"
The lab sets invented dignities beside last lesson's prices. The general, made a duke, gets forty, above every buyer's price. The judge gets thirty four, equal to the peace buyer's and above the others. The orator, with no office, gets ten, below all three. And the merchant gets fifteen, below his twenty four.

@math heading="The orator raised" items="dignity 30 vs 19, 28, 30@raises the orator;;above, above, equal@above two"
Now the commonwealth raises the orator to thirty. That's above two buyers' prices, and exactly the faction's. His powers didn't change, only the sovereign's valuation. Hobbes isn't saying the commonwealth should do that. Dignity is the sovereign's act, so it can fall anywhere.

@chapter Worthiness and merit
@art fitted-key
One word is left. Worthiness is different from worth, and from merit. It's fitness: the worthiest commander is the one best fitted with what the command requires. A man may be worthy of an office and still have no claim to it.

@list heading="Four different things" items="Worth: what a buyer would pay@a buyer|Dignity: what the sovereign declares@the sovereign|Worthiness: what he's fit for@fit for|Merit: what he's owed by promise@owed"
Merit presupposes a right, the thing being due by promise, a matter for the chapters on contracts. So there are four things: what a buyer would pay, what the sovereign declares, what a man is fit for, and what he's owed. A table can only show the first two.

@art coin-and-crown
Kant used dignity almost the other way round: what a person has that no price or title can add to or take away. Hobbes's reply is that he describes how men value one another, not how they ought to.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Honour is a verdict on goodness@The first|Dignity is the same as worth@The second|Worthiness is a high price@And the third"
The first slip is treating honour as a verdict on goodness. It's an opinion of power. The second is merging dignity with worth, when one is set by buyers and the other by the sovereign. And the third is reading worthiness as a high price.
