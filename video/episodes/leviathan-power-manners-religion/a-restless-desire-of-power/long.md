@chapter Enough, until
@title text="The Difference of Manners" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 11" art=thumb
Why do people who have enough keep reaching for more? Hobbes's answer is subtler than it's usually taken to be.

@art savings-jar
You've likely felt it in miniature: a savings account that seems large enough, until you imagine something going wrong. Hobbes builds a chapter on that feeling.

@art moving-target
By manners he means the qualities that concern living together in peace, not how to greet a neighbour. And he begins with what people want from life. There's no final repose. Felicity is a continual progress of desire from one object to the next, each one only the way to the one after.

@chapter Power after power
@quote text="So that in the first place, I put for a generall inclination of all mankind, a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 11"
From that comes the famous sentence. In the first place I put for a general inclination of all mankind a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death.

@words text="Not always greed"
Now the clause most readers skip. The cause, Hobbes says, isn't always that a man hopes for more delight, or can't be content with a moderate power.

@quote text="but because he cannot assure the power and means to live well, which he hath present, without the acquisition of more." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 11"
It's because he cannot assure the power and means to live well, which he hath present, without the acquisition of more. Power is only a means, and you can't secure what you have by standing still.

@art kings-walls
Even kings, whose power is greatest, turn to securing it, at home by laws, abroad by wars. And when that's done, a new desire takes over, for fame, or ease, or admiration.

@chapter Two neighbours
@art two-houses
The lab turns that clause into a table for two neighbours. Each can hold, keeping what he has, or add, arming and extending his reach. The payoffs are the lesson's own, and higher is better.

@math heading="The greedy preset" items="both hold: 3, 3@both hold;;both add: 1, 1@both add;;add against hold: 4, 0@against one who holds;;equilibrium: both add@only equilibrium"
First, greedy neighbours. If both hold, each gets three. If both add, each gets one. Add against one who holds, and you get four, the holder nothing. Adding is the best reply to anything, so the only equilibrium is both adding, worse for each than both holding.

@math heading="The moderate preset" items="other holds: hold 3 beats add 2@other holds;;other adds: add 1 beats hold 0@other adds;;equilibria: both hold, both add@two equilibria"
Now Hobbes's clause. If the other holds, each prefers to hold, three against two. If the other adds, each prefers to add, one against nothing, because a holder loses what he has. That gives two equilibria: both hold, and both add. Fear alone, with no greed, keeps the second one stable.

@art one-greedy
Pair a moderate neighbour with a greedy one, who adds whatever happens. Now the moderate man does better to add too, and the only equilibrium is both adding. One greedy neighbour drags the moderate one along. Chapter thirteen builds on this, but these tables aren't that argument yet.

@chapter Pulled both ways
@list heading="What the desires incline to" items="Competition for riches, honour, command: war@Competition|Desire of ease and delight: obedience@ease|Fear of death and wounds: obedience@Fear of death"
The same restless creature is pulled both ways. Competition for riches, honour or command inclines to war, since one competitor wins by subduing the other. But desire of ease, and fear of death and wounds, dispose men to obey a common power.

@quote text="For benefits oblige; and obligation is thraldome; and unrequitable obligation, perpetuall thraldome; which is to ones equall, hatefull." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 11"
He has an eye for small cruelties too. For benefits oblige, and obligation is thraldom, and unrequitable obligation perpetual thraldom, which is to one's equal hateful. Help someone more than he can repay, and he may come to hate you.

@art burning-books
Ignorance of causes makes men credulous, and even makes them tell lies without malice. Disputes over right and wrong go on by pen and sword because they cross men's interests. If a triangle's angles crossed a ruler's interest, books of geometry would be burned.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Hobbes says men are greedy@The first|The tables are Hobbes's argument for war@The second|The equilibrium is what they want@And the third"
The first slip is reading Hobbes as saying men are greedy. He says not always. The second is taking these tables for his argument for war. And the third is thinking an equilibrium is what the neighbours want. Both adding pays one each, both holding three.
