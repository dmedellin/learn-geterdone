@chapter The question
@title text="The Liberal Reward of Labour" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 8" art=thumb
Smith has argued that wages in Britain sit above the bare floor. In the last third of chapter eight he asks the next question: what do high wages actually do?

@words text="18d in London · 14d or 15d a few miles off"
First, the evidence that they're above the floor. Summer wages, when a family is cheapest to keep, are highest. In London a day's wage is eighteen pence, and a few miles off fourteen or fifteen. And the Scottish day wage has risen from sixpence in summer and fivepence in winter last century, to eightpence now.

@words text="No society can surely be flourishing and happy, of which the far greater part of the members are poor and miserable."
Is that improvement good for society? Smith answers at once. No society can surely be flourishing and happy, of which the far greater part of the members are poor and miserable.

@chapter Poverty and children
@art regulator
Poverty doesn't prevent births, Smith says, but it's hard on rearing children. He reports being told of Highland mothers who had borne twenty children and didn't have two alive. Every species multiplies in proportion to its means of subsistence. In civilized society, only the lowest ranks meet that limit, and it works by destroying children.

@quote text="It is in this manner that the demand for men, like that for any other commodity, necessarily regulates the production of men, quickens it when it goes on too slowly, and stops it when it advances too fast." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 8"
A liberal reward lets the poor bring up more children, in about the proportion demand requires. Too low a reward, and the shortage of hands raises it. Too high, and the multiplication lowers it. It is in this manner that the demand for men, like that for any other commodity, necessarily regulates the production of men.

@chapter Doubling
@art doubling-tree
Smith says the North American colonies double in twenty or twenty five years, while Britain and most of Europe aren't supposed to double in less than five hundred. A population that doubles every d years is multiplied by two raised to the power t over d after t years.

@math heading="100,000 people, 100 years" items="at 25 years:  100 / 25 = 4 doublings@four doublings;;2⁴ = 16 → 1,600,000@one million six;;at 20 years:  100 / 20 = 5 doublings@five doublings;;2⁵ = 32 → 3,200,000@three million two"
Start with a hundred thousand people, the lesson's figure, over a hundred years. Doubling every twenty five years, that's four doublings, a factor of sixteen: one million six hundred thousand. Every twenty years, five doublings, a factor of thirty two: three million two hundred thousand. Five years' difference makes the population twice as large.

@art growth-curves
Hold twenty five years fixed over two centuries, and a hundred thousand becomes twenty five million six hundred thousand. Nobody takes that as a forecast, and Smith makes none. That's why he ties the rate to the demand for labour, not to nature alone. Malthus, in 1798, took the same doubling and drew a darker warning.

@chapter Free labour and industry
@words text="Free labour comes cheaper in the end" sub="disputed by Fogel and Engerman"
Smith also claims the work of freemen comes cheaper in the end than that of slaves, because a slave's upkeep is run by a negligent master, and a freeman's by himself. Robert Fogel and Stanley Engerman later argued that the American plantations don't bear that out.

@art piece-work
High wages, Smith says, encourage industry. But paid well by the piece, workmen overwork. A carpenter in London isn't supposed to last in his utmost vigour above eight years.

@art four-and-three
And the idleness of three days a week, so loudly complained of, is often caused by excessive work on the other four. The man who works moderately enough to work constantly keeps his health longest, and over a year does the most work.

@chapter Wages and prices
@art cheaper-goods
Higher wages raise many prices. But the same growth of stock makes labour more productive, through division and better machinery. So for many goods, the rise in the price of labour is more than made up by the fall in its quantity.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the doubling time as a law of nature@The first|Thinking high wages make people idle@The second|Taking his anecdotes for statistics@The third"
The first slip is reading the doubling time as a law of nature. The second is thinking Smith says high wages make people idle. He says the reverse; he warns of overwork. The third is taking his anecdotes, like the Highland mother and the London carpenter, for statistics. They're things he was told.
