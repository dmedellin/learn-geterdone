@chapter The question
@title text="The Masters and the Workmen" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 8" art=thumb
What decides a wage? Smith starts with a world where nobody has to share.

@quote text="In that original state of things which precedes both the appropriation of land and the accumulation of stock, the whole produce of labour belongs to the labourer." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 8"
In that original state of things which precedes both the appropriation of land and the accumulation of stock, the whole produce of labour belongs to the labourer. He has neither landlord nor master to share with him.

@art first-state
Had that lasted, wages would have risen with every improvement the division of labour brought. But it couldn't last beyond the first owning of land and the first gathering of stock. So Smith doesn't follow it further.

@chapter Two deductions
@art deductions
Instead come deductions. The landlord's rent is the first deduction from the produce of labour on land. The farmer who advances the labourer's keep until harvest takes his profit, the second. In manufactures, the master takes his profit from the value the workmen add. And in Europe, twenty workmen serve under a master for every one who's independent.

@chapter The bargain
@words text="As much · as little" sub="workmen · masters"
So what's left is settled by a bargain. The workmen want to get as much as possible, and the masters to give as little. Smith expects the masters to win on ordinary occasions, and his reasons are short.

@art bargaining-scale
The masters are fewer, so they combine more easily. And the law allows their combinations, or at least doesn't forbid them, while it forbids the workmen's. We have no acts of parliament against combining to lower the price of work, he says, but many against combining to raise it.

@art waiting-glasses
And the masters can wait. Many workmen couldn't live a week without work, few a month, scarce any a year. In the long run, the workman may be as needed by his master as the master by him. But the need isn't so immediate.

@words text="A tacit, but constant and uniform, combination"
Smith goes further. Masters are always and everywhere in a tacit, but constant and uniform, combination not to raise wages. We don't hear of it because it's the usual state of things. That's an argument from silence, and he offers no figures. And he's just as plain that the workmen's loud combinations usually end in the ruin of their ringleaders.

@chapter The floor
@words text="At least double his own maintenance" sub="Cantillon, as Smith reports him"
Under the bargain there's a floor. A man's wages must at least maintain him, and somewhat more, to bring up a family. Smith reports Richard Cantillon's guess that the lowest labourers must earn at least double their own maintenance, so they can raise two children. Smith won't decide the proportion himself.

@chapter A family against three wages
@art family-of-six
Smith reports that Lord Chief Justice Hales put a labourer's family of six at ten shillings a week, a hundred and twenty pence. Hales and Gregory King both supposed about twenty pence a head. Use twenty pence as one man's maintenance, the lesson's assumption, and Cantillon's minimum is forty pence a week.

@math heading="Family 120d · minimum 40d" items="Highlands:  3s = 36d · −84 · −4@three shillings;;Lowlands:  8d · 6 = 48d · −72 · +8@eight pence a day;;London:  18d · 6 = 108d · −12 · +68@eighteen pence"
Now three wages, taking six working days. In parts of the Highlands, three shillings a week, thirty six pence: eighty four short of the family, four short of the minimum. The Scottish Lowlands, eight pence a day, forty eight: seventy two short, eight to spare. London, eighteen pence a day, a hundred and eight: twelve short, and sixty eight above the minimum.

@art wage-bars
Two cautions. Hales wrote in the time of Charles the Second, so his figure is older than these wages. And the lab uses the same family's needs in all three places, without pricing each one separately.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the floor as the wage@The first|Thinking Smith takes the workmen's side@The second|Setting a family's whole needs against a man's wage@The third"
The first slip is reading the floor as the wage. It's the lowest consistent with common humanity, and Smith argues British wages are well above it. The second is thinking Smith simply takes the workmen's side. He describes both parties' faults. The third is setting the whole family's needs against the man's wage alone, when his wife and children earn too.
