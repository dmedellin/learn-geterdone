@chapter The question
@title text="High Profits Raise Prices More Than High Wages" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 9" art=thumb
When goods get dear, masters often blame high wages. On the last page of chapter nine, Smith answers them.

@quote text="In reality, high profits tend much more to raise the price of work than high wages." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 9"
In reality, high profits tend much more to raise the price of work than high wages. He's just said that in a fast growing country, low profit can make up for high wages, so it can sell as cheap as poorer neighbours.

@chapter A wage rise adds
@art linen-chain
His example is linen. Flax dressers, then spinners, then weavers. Suppose all their wages rise by twopence a day. Then the price of a piece of linen rises by a number of twopences: the people who worked on it, times the days they worked.

@art twopence-add
That's simple addition. The part of the price that is wages rises only in arithmetical proportion to the rise of wages. Each twopence is added once.

@chapter A profit rise compounds
@art stacking-profit
Now raise every employer's profit by five per cent instead. The flax dresser's employer wants five per cent more on the materials and wages he advanced. The spinner's employer wants it on the dearer flax, which already includes that extra, plus wages. And the weaver's on the dearer yarn. Each stage's rise sits on the last.

@words text="Wages: simple interest · Profit: compound interest"
So the profit part rises in geometrical proportion. Smith puts it as a debt. In raising prices, a rise of wages works like simple interest. A rise of profit works like compound interest.

@chapter A linen chain
@math heading="Profit 10% → 15% at every stage" items="grower:  (100 + 40) · 1.15 = 161@the grower;;dresser:  (161 + 30) · 1.15 = 219.65@the dresser;;spinner:  (219.65 + 60) · 1.15 = 321.60@the spinner;;weaver:  (321.60 + 90) · 1.15 = 473.34@the weaver"
The lesson builds a four stage chain. At ten per cent profit, the price after the weaver is about four hundred and sixteen fifty. Raise profit to fifteen. The grower pays a hundred in wages and forty rent, and asks a hundred and sixty one. The dresser adds thirty, two nineteen sixty five. The spinner adds sixty, three twenty one sixty. The weaver adds ninety, four seventy three thirty four.

@art simple-vs-compound
That's a rise of about fifty six eighty three, with wages unchanged. About forty eight twenty five is five points on the old advances. The other eight fifty eight or so is profit charged on earlier stages' extra profits. That's the compounding.

@words text="Wages +10%: +£28 wages · +£35.79 price"
Compare a ten per cent rise in every wage. Wages go up twenty eight pounds, and the price by about thirty five seventy nine. The extra is the employers' ordinary profit on the extra wages they advance. It belongs to the profit part. Smith's sentence is about the wages part only, and that rises twenty eight, added once.

@art both-rises
But the profit rise being larger here depends on the lesson's figures, not on Smith. Pick a short chain or a small profit rise, and the comparison can reverse. What holds is the shape: wages add, profits compound, and the longer the chain, the more it compounds.

@chapter The merchants' silence
@art merchants-silence
Smith ends the chapter on the merchants. They complain much of high wages raising prices and lessening sales. They say nothing about high profits. They're silent about the pernicious effects of their own gains, and complain only of other people's. Here Smith doesn't hide where his suspicion falls: the interest of those who live by profit isn't the public's.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Saying Smith denies wages raise prices@The first|Taking the lab's comparison as general@The second|Equating ten per cent with twopence a day@The third"
The first slip is saying Smith denies that high wages raise prices. He says they do, just less. The second is taking the lab's comparison as a general result. The third is equating the lab's ten per cent with Smith's twopence a day, which is a flat sum. Ricardo, in 1817, held that a general rise of wages falls on profit instead.
