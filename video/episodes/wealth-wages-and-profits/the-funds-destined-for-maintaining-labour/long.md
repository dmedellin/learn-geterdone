@chapter The question
@title text="The Funds Destined for Maintaining Labour" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 8" art=thumb
Which country pays its workers more, a rich one or a poor one? You'd guess the rich one. Smith says it depends on something else entirely: whether the country is growing.

@chapter Where the demand comes from
@words text="Demand for labour ∝ funds for wages"
When the demand for labour keeps rising, the masters bid against each other, and break their tacit combination. So where does that demand come from? It can only increase in proportion to the funds set aside to pay wages.

@art surplus-funds
There are two such funds. First, revenue over what its owner needs to live: a landlord with more than his family needs hires servants with the surplus. Second, stock over what its master needs: a weaver with more stock than his own materials and keep takes on journeymen. Grow the surplus, and he hires more.

@quote text="It is not the actual greatness of national wealth, but its continual increase, which occasions a rise in the wages of labour." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 8"
And then the sentence the lesson is named for. It is not the actual greatness of national wealth, but its continual increase, which occasions a rise in the wages of labour.

@chapter Three countries
@words text="New York, 1773: 3s 6d currency = 2s sterling a day"
North America is poorer than England, yet wages there are much higher. In the province of New York in 1773, common labourers earned three shillings and sixpence currency a day, equal to two shillings sterling, above the London price. And provisions were much cheaper, so the real wage was higher still.

@art three-countries
China is the stationary case. Very rich, Smith says, but long at a stand. The hands multiply beyond their employment, labourers bid against each other, and wages sink to the lowest consistent with common humanity. Bengal is the declining one: the funds decay, the lowest class fills, and want, famine and mortality follow.

@words text="Thriving · stationary · declining" sub="liberal · scanty · bare"
Smith blames the difference between North America and Bengal on their governments: the British constitution against a mercantile company that oppresses and domineers in the East Indies. So the liberal reward of labour is the natural symptom of increasing national wealth.

@chapter The lab's quotient
@art still-and-thriving
The lab turns the rule into a sum, and the sum is the lab's: wage equals fund divided by workers. A rich country with ten thousand pounds and a thousand workers, neither growing, pays ten pounds every year. A poorer one with six hundred pounds and a hundred workers pays six.

@art growing-pile
But its fund grows five per cent a year, its workers three. After thirty years, the wage is about ten pounds sixty eight. It's overtaken the rich country.

@math heading="Crowded: fund £1,000, workers +2% a year" items="year 0:  1000 / 100 = 10@year nought;;year 1:  1000 / (100 · 1.02) = 9.80@year one;;year 2:  1000 / (100 · 1.02²) = 9.61@year two;;year 10:  1000 / (100 · 1.02¹⁰) = 8.20@year ten"
Now a crowded country. The fund stays at a thousand pounds, and a hundred workers grow two per cent a year. In year nought, the wage is ten. In year one, nine eighty. In year two, nine sixty one. By year ten, eight twenty. The fund never changed, and the wage fell by about a fifth.

@art crowded-share
And in a declining country, with the fund falling four per cent a year while workers still grow one per cent, the wage after ten years is just over six pounds. What matters is the gap between the growth rates, not the size of the fund.

@chapter What the sum leaves out
@words text="Wages fund: Mill 1848 · given up 1869"
Smith's wage isn't really a quotient. It's settled by masters and labourers competing, and it has a floor. The lab has none, so read its falling wages as a direction, not a forecast. John Stuart Mill made the quotient a doctrine, the wages fund, in 1848. After attacks by Longe and Thornton, he gave it up in 1869.

@art travellers-reports
His evidence has critics too. His China comes from travellers' reports, and historians like Kenneth Pomeranz argue it wasn't stationary in important ways. His Bengal is a few years after the famine of 1770, which he doesn't name.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking a rich country to pay high wages@The first|Reading fund over workers as Smith's formula@The second|Reading China and Bengal as measured facts@The third"
The first slip is taking a rich country to pay high wages. The second is reading fund over workers as Smith's own formula. And the third is reading China and Bengal as measured facts, when they rest on reports he didn't test.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Fund £1,000 · 100 workers · +2% a year" sub="wage in year 2?"
Your turn. Multiply the workers by one point oh two, twice, and divide the fund by the result. You should get about nine sixty one. Then let the fund grow two per cent too, and watch the wage stay at ten.
