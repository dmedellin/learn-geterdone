@chapter The question
@title text="The Profits of Stock" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 9" art=thumb
Chapter eight asked what moves wages. Chapter nine asks the same of profit, and Smith says the same causes act on it, but the other way. The growth of stock that raises wages tends to lower profit.

@art fluctuating-profit
There's a problem, though. Profit is so very fluctuating that a man in a trade can't always tell you his own average. The average across a kingdom is harder still, and for a remote period, Smith says, it's altogether impossible.

@chapter Interest as the index
@quote text="It may be laid down as a maxim, that wherever a great deal can be made by the use of money, a great deal will commonly be given for the use of it" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 9"
So he uses something that can be known, the rate of interest. It may be laid down as a maxim, that wherever a great deal can be made by the use of money, a great deal will commonly be given for the use of it. As interest moves, profit moves with it.

@art interest-ladder
Then he traces the legal rate. Under Henry the Eighth, interest above ten per cent was made unlawful. Under James the First, the limit fell to eight. Soon after the Restoration, six. Under Queen Anne, five. And these laws, he says, followed the market rate, not the other way round.

@chapter Competing stocks
@art competing-stocks
Over that same period, the country's wealth grew faster and faster, wages rose, and profits in most trades fell. The reason is competition. When many rich merchants turn their stock into the same trade, they lower its profit. A great town needs more stock than a village, and pays higher wages and lower profit.

@art dutch-canal
Holland is far along that road. The Dutch trade on lower profits than any people in Europe. Their government borrows at two per cent. When their merchants complain trade is decaying, Smith says falling profit is the natural effect of prosperity. Money, says the proverb, makes money.

@chapter The exceptions
@art new-colony
High wages and high profits scarcely ever go together, except in new colonies, where land is cheap and stock scarce. Interest there runs from six to eight per cent. And where stock is falling, wages fall and profits rise. In Bengal, money is often lent to farmers at forty, fifty and sixty per cent. In China, twelve per cent is said to be common.

@chapter Double interest
@words text="Double interest" sub="a good, moderate, reasonable profit"
Smith separates clear profit, the surplus after occasional losses, from gross. Interest can only come out of the clear. And in Britain, double interest is what merchants call a good, moderate, reasonable profit. Where clear profit is eight or ten per cent, half may reasonably go to interest.

@math heading="Double the legal rates" items="Henry VIII:  2 · 10 = 20%@Henry the Eighth;;James I:  2 · 8 = 16%@James the First;;Restoration:  2 · 6 = 12%@Restoration;;Anne:  2 · 5 = 10%@Anne;;fall:  20 − 10 = 10 points@a fall of"
The lab doubles each legal rate. That multiplication is the lab's, not Smith's. Under Henry the Eighth, twenty per cent. James the First, sixteen. The Restoration, twelve. Anne, ten. That's a fall of ten points, half the first.

@art half-to-lender
Smith limits the rule himself. With profit at ten and interest at five, the borrower keeps five. At eight and four, he keeps four. But the proportion might not hold where profit is much lower or much higher. Bengal, at forty per cent interest, is no case of double interest.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating double interest as a law@The first|Reading falling profit as decay@The second|Thinking the statutes set the rate@The third"
The first slip is treating double interest as a law. It's a merchants' rule, applied where profit is eight or ten per cent. The second is reading falling profit as decay. Smith says it's a sign of prosperity. The third is thinking the statutes set the rate. They followed the market.

@words text="Smith: competition · Ricardo: dearer food"
Later writers agreed profit falls as stock grows, but not on why. David Hume had argued in 1752 that interest depends on profit, not on the quantity of money, and Smith builds on that. Ricardo blamed the rising cost of food as worse land is farmed. Smith's reason is competition among stocks.
