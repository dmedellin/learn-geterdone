@chapter The idea
@title text="Slope-Intercept Form" sub="Lines, Functions and Graphs" art=thumb
What if you could look at an equation and see its line, with no table and no working? Solve it for y, and the two numbers you need are sitting right there.

@words text="y = mx + b" sub="m = slope · (0, b) = y-intercept"
That shape is y equals m x plus b. m is the slope, and the point zero, b is the y intercept. It works for every line except a vertical one.

@math heading="Why m and b?" items="x = 0:  y = b@put x equal to zero;;x = 1:  y = m + b@at x equals one;;run 1  →  rise m@a run of one"
You don't have to memorise why. The y intercept is where x is zero, and if you put x equal to zero, you get y equals b. For the slope, at x equals one the height is m plus b. So a run of one gives a rise of m.

@chapter Getting into the form
@math heading="2x + 3y = 12" items="3y = −2x + 12@subtract two x;;y = (−2x + 12)/3@divide by three;;y = −(2/3)x + 4@every term;;m = −2/3   b = 4@slope is"
Here's the move. Isolate y. From two x plus three y equals twelve, subtract two x from both sides. Then divide by three, and divide every term. That gives y equals minus two thirds x, plus four. The slope is minus two thirds, and b is four.

@art divide-each-term
That last step is where a term gets lost. Minus two x plus twelve, all over three, is minus two thirds x plus four, not plus twelve. The intercepts from the last lesson confirm it: zero, four is there, and putting in six gives zero.

@chapter Dividing by a negative
@math heading="4x − 2y = 10" items="−2y = −4x + 10@subtract four x;;y = 2x − 5@divide every term;;(0, −5):  0 + 10 = 10  ✓@zero, minus five;;x = 3:  y = 1;  12 − 2 = 10  ✓@x equals three"
Try four x minus two y equals ten. Subtract four x. Then divide every term by minus two, and both signs change: y equals two x minus five. Check zero, minus five in the original: zero plus ten is ten. And at x equals three, the new form gives one, and the original gives twelve minus two, ten.

@art parallel-shift
Dividing by a negative is where this lesson's errors come from. Change only one sign and you get y equals two x plus five, a plausible line that passes no check at all. Substituting takes a few seconds and catches it.

@chapter Graphing from the form
@art intercept-start
Graphing is now two moves. Plot zero, b. Then treat the slope as instructions: write it as rise over run, go that run to the right, go that rise up, or down if it's negative, and mark the point.

@math heading="y = −(3/4)x + 2" items="start at (0, 2)@Start at zero, two;;run 4 right, rise −3 (3 down)@run four;;land on (4, −1)@lands on;;−(3/4)(4) + 2 = −3 + 2 = −1  ✓@check"
Take y equals minus three quarters x plus two. Start at zero, two. The slope says run four right and rise minus three, which means three down. That lands on four, minus one. And check: minus three quarters times four, plus two, is minus three plus two, minus one.

@art steps-from-intercept
Two points, and the line is drawn. Notice the direction of that step. A negative slope sends you right and down, so a falling line is exactly what you should expect to see. If the picture rises, a sign went missing somewhere.

@art slope-as-fraction
A whole number slope is a fraction too. A slope of two is two over one, so one right and two up. Forget that invisible one, step two right and two up, and you've drawn a line of slope one.

@art same-slope-lines
And the form makes comparing lines easy. Two lines written this way can be checked for being parallel just by looking at two numbers. No working at all. That's what Parallel and Perpendicular Lines will use.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Dividing only the first term@The first|Losing a sign when dividing by a negative@The second|Reading m before solving for y@The third"
The first slip is dividing only the first term. The second is losing a sign when you divide by a negative. And the third is reading m off before y stands alone. In two x plus three y equals twelve, the slope isn't two. It's minus two thirds.

@chapter Try it
@words text="−3x + 6y = 18" sub="first step given: 6y = 3x + 18"
Now you. Start with minus three x plus six y equals eighteen. The first step is given: six y equals three x plus eighteen. Divide every term, name m and b, take one slope step from the intercept, and check that new point in the original equation.
