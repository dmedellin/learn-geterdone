@chapter The question
@title text="Slope" sub="Lines, Functions and Graphs" art=thumb
How steep is a line? You might reach for a protractor and measure the angle.

@art stretched-axes
But stretch one of the axes and the line looks flatter, even though nothing about it has changed. So steepness needs a number that survives any drawing. That number is slope.

@art rise-run-triangle
Pick two points on a line. Going from one to the other, you move up by some amount, the rise, and across by some amount, the run. Slope is the rise divided by the run.

@math heading="Slope" items="m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)@m equals;;x₁ ≠ x₂@as long as"
In symbols, m equals y two minus y one, over x two minus x one, as long as the two x values are different.

@words text="m = 3/2" sub="2 right → 3 up · 1 right → 3/2 up"
Read it as a rate. m is how much y changes for every one step to the right. A slope of three over two means going two to the right takes you three up. Equally, going one to the right takes you three halves up. Same statement.

@chapter Any two points
@art similar-triangles
Here's something that has to be true for this to work. Any two points on the line give the same slope. Two different choices make two right triangles with the same angles, and similar triangles have proportional sides. So the ratio agrees.

@words text="the slope of the line"
Without that fact, the phrase the slope of the line would mean nothing, because different people would get different numbers for the same line. It's why one number can stand for a whole line's steepness.

@chapter The ordering rule
@math heading="(−2, 1) and (4, 10)" items="(10 − 1) / (4 − (−2)) = 9/6 = 3/2@ten minus one;;(1 − 10) / (−2 − 4) = −9/−6 = 3/2@one minus ten;;✗  (10 − 1) / (−2 − 4) = 9/−6 = −3/2@But mix them"
Take minus two, one and four, ten. Ten minus one, over four minus minus two, is nine over six, or three halves. From the other point, one minus ten, over minus two minus four, is minus nine over minus six, still three halves. But mix them, ten minus one over minus two minus four, and you get minus three halves.

@art sign-flip
That last answer isn't a small slip. It describes a line that falls where the real one rises. The rule is simple: whichever point you call first, use it first on top and on the bottom. And watch four minus minus two. Subtracting a negative adds, so the run is six, not two.

@chapter Sign, zero and undefined
@art four-slopes
The sign tells you direction. A positive slope rises from left to right. A negative slope falls. A horizontal line has a rise of zero, so its slope is zero. And a vertical line has a run of zero, and you can't divide by zero.

@math heading="Four slopes" items="(−2, 1) → (4, 10):  9/6 = 3/2  rising@nine over six;;(1, 7) → (3, 1):  −6/2 = −3  falling@minus six over two;;(−3, 4) → (6, 4):  0/9 = 0  horizontal@zero over nine;;(5, 2) → (5, 9):  run 0, undefined@the run is zero"
Here are four. Minus two, one to four, ten gives nine over six, three halves, rising. One, seven to three, one gives minus six over two, minus three, falling. Minus three, four to six, four gives zero over nine, zero, horizontal. And five, two to five, nine: the run is zero, so the slope is undefined.

@art horizontal-vs-vertical
Keep those last two apart. Saying no slope is ambiguous, so avoid it. Say m equals zero for horizontal, and slope undefined for vertical. They're the flattest and the steepest lines, opposites.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Mixing the order of subtraction@The first|Writing run over rise@The second|Calling a vertical line's slope zero@And the third"
The first slip is mixing the order of subtraction, which flips the sign. The second is writing run over rise, which turns a slope of three into one third, a different line. And the third is calling a vertical line's slope zero. A quick glance catches the first. One, seven to three, one moves right and down, so minus three is plausible.

@chapter Try it
@words text="(−5, 4) and (1, −8)" sub="numerator: −8 − 4 = −12"
Your turn. Use minus five, four and one, minus eight. The top is given: minus eight minus four, which is minus twelve. Write the bottom in the matching order, simplify, and check the sign against the right and down movement. Then reverse both orders and confirm you get the same slope.
