@chapter The question
@title text="Parallel and Perpendicular Lines" sub="Lines, Functions and Graphs" art=thumb
Two lines on a graph. Will they ever meet? And if they do, is it at a right angle? You can answer both just from their slopes. Almost always.

@chapter Parallel
@art parallel-gap
Two different lines are parallel when they never meet. For lines that aren't vertical, that happens exactly when their slopes are equal. Equal slopes mean the same rise for every step across, so the vertical gap between the lines is the same at every x. If the gap isn't zero, they never touch.

@math heading="Same m, different b" items="y = 2x + 1@two x plus one;;y = 2x − 5@two x minus five;;4x − 2y = 10  →  y = 2x − 5@in disguise"
In slope intercept form it's just looking. y equals two x plus one and y equals two x minus five have the same slope and different intercepts, so they're parallel. And four x minus two y equals ten is that second line in disguise. Staring at the standard form would never show you that.

@art coincident-lines
Check b as well as m. y equals two x plus one and two y equals four x plus two have the same slope because they're the same line. Call those coincident, not parallel.

@chapter Perpendicular
@art right-angle-cross
Two lines are perpendicular when they meet at a right angle. For lines with slopes, that happens exactly when the two slopes multiply to minus one. Equivalently, the second slope is minus one over the first.

@art quarter-turn
That's the negative reciprocal, and its two parts do separate jobs. The reciprocal swaps rise and run, because a quarter turn swaps the two legs of the slope triangle. The minus sign reverses direction, because a quarter turn sends a rising line to a falling one.

@math heading="Flip, then negate" items="2  →  −1/2@two goes to;;−3  →  1/3@minus three;;3/4  →  −4/3@three quarters;;1  →  −1@one goes to"
So two goes to minus one half. Minus three goes to one third. Three quarters goes to minus four thirds. And one goes to minus one. Every product is minus one. That last one is the picture to keep: y equals x and y equals minus x cross at a right angle.

@chapter The vertical exception
@art vertical-horizontal-cross
Now a vertical line and a horizontal line. You can see they're perpendicular. But the horizontal one has slope zero and the vertical one has no slope. There's nothing to multiply, and zero times anything is zero, never minus one.

@words text="Recognise vertical lines first"
That isn't a flaw in the rule. The rule is about lines that have slopes. So spot the vertical lines first. Perpendicular to vertical means horizontal, and perpendicular to horizontal means vertical. No arithmetic needed.

@chapter A worked example
@math heading="Through (1, 2), parallel to y = −3x + 7" items="m = −3@slope is minus three;;y − 2 = −3(x − 1)@y minus two;;y = −3x + 5@five"
Through one, two, parallel to y equals minus three x plus seven. The parallel slope is minus three. Point slope gives y minus two equals minus three times x minus one, which simplifies to y equals minus three x plus five.

@math heading="Through (1, 2), perpendicular" items="m = −1/(−3) = 1/3@one third;;y − 2 = (1/3)(x − 1)@one third times;;y = (1/3)x + 5/3@five thirds;;check:  1/3 + 5/3 = 6/3 = 2  ✓@six thirds;;−3 · 1/3 = −1  ✓@product"
For the perpendicular, the slope is one third. So y minus two equals one third times x minus one, and y equals one third x plus five thirds. Check the point: one third plus five thirds is six thirds, which is two. And the product, minus three times one third, is minus one.

@art through-point
Keep five thirds as a fraction. Round it and you lose the exact check. Do both checks, too. The point check catches arithmetic slips. The slope check catches flipping without negating, which the point check would happily pass.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Flipping without negating, or the reverse@The first|Calling identical lines parallel@The second|Forcing the product rule on a vertical line@The third"
The first slip is doing only half the operation. Perpendicular to three quarters is minus four thirds, not four thirds or minus three quarters. The second is calling identical lines parallel. The third is forcing the product rule on a vertical line.
