@chapter The idea
@title text="The Coordinate Plane" sub="Lines, Functions and Graphs" art=thumb
Two number lines at right angles. That's all a coordinate plane is. But it turns a pair of numbers into a point, and an equation into a picture. And the order inside the pair turns out to be the whole idea.

@art two-axes
One number line runs across. That's the x axis. The other runs up and down. That's the y axis. They cross at the origin, the point zero, zero.

@words text="One pair ↔ one point" sub="in both directions"
Here's the sentence that does the real work. Every point of the plane is named by exactly one pair of numbers, and every pair names exactly one point. Because that matching runs both ways, you can answer a question about points with algebra, and a question about algebra with a picture.

@chapter Reading a pair
@art point-plotted
In the pair x, y, the first number is how far you go across from the y axis. Right is positive. The second number is how far you go up from the x axis. Up is positive. Across first, then up. Always start from the origin, not from the last point you plotted.

@art swapped-points
So three, five is three right and five up. And five, three is five right and three up. They're two different points. Writing a pair backwards is the most common way a correct calculation ends up drawn in the wrong place.

@chapter Six points
@math heading="Across, then up" items="(3, 5): right 3, up 5@three, five;;(−4, 2): left 4, up 2@minus four, two;;(−3, −1): left 3, down 1@minus three, minus one;;(2, −4): right 2, down 4@two, minus four"
Let's place some. Three, five: right three, up five. Minus four, two: left four, up two. Minus three, minus one: left three, down one. And two, minus four: right two, down four.

@math heading="On an axis" items="(0, −3): on the y-axis, 3 below the origin@zero, minus three;;(−5, 0): on the x-axis, 5 to the left@minus five, zero"
Two more. Zero, minus three sits on the y axis, three below the origin. And minus five, zero sits on the x axis, five to the left.

@chapter Quadrants
@art quadrants
The two axes cut the plane into four regions, called quadrants. They're numbered anticlockwise, starting from the upper right. And you don't need to memorise pictures. The signs of the two coordinates decide everything.

@list heading="Read the signs" items="QI  (+, +)  upper right@Quadrant one|QII  (−, +)  upper left@Quadrant two|QIII  (−, −)  lower left@Quadrant three|QIV  (+, −)  lower right@quadrant four"
Quadrant one has both coordinates positive, upper right. Quadrant two has x negative and y positive, upper left. Quadrant three has both negative, lower left. And quadrant four has x positive and y negative, lower right.

@art axis-points
Every one of those conditions is strict. So a point with a zero coordinate lies on an axis, and in no quadrant at all. Zero, minus three is in none. So is minus five, zero. The origin lies on both axes.

@chapter The graph of an equation
@words text="On the graph ⇔ the equation is true"
Now the payoff. The graph of an equation in x and y is the set of all points whose coordinates make it true. A point is on the graph if and only if substituting it gives a true statement.

@math heading="2x + 3y = 12" items="(3, 2):  6 + 6 = 12  ✓ on@three, two;;(1, 4):  2 + 12 = 14  ✗ off@one, four;;(6, 0):  12 + 0 = 12  ✓ on@six, zero;;(0, 4):  0 + 12 = 12  ✓ on@zero, four"
Take two x plus three y equals twelve. Try three, two: six plus six is twelve. True, so it's on the graph. Try one, four: two plus twelve is fourteen. False, so it's off. Six, zero gives twelve, on. And zero, four gives twelve, on.

@art line-membership
So membership is decided by arithmetic, never by how the picture looks. A point that looks close to the line might not be on it. Substitute. The picture is a summary of the arithmetic, not a replacement for it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the pair as up, then across@The first|Putting an axis point in a quadrant@The second|Deciding membership by eye@And the third"
The first slip is reading the pair as up, then across. Two, seven is two across and seven up, and the swapped point often still looks plausible. The second is putting an axis point in the nearest quadrant. There's no such thing. And the third is deciding membership by eye, instead of substituting.
