@chapter The pressed eye again
@title text="Demonology and the Relics of the Gentiles" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 45" art=thumb
Press your eye hard and a light appears outside it, which nobody else can see. There's no such thing out there, Hobbes says, only a motion in the inner organs. That's sight in miniature.

@art seeming-image
Light from bodies reaches the eye and makes an image that seems not an imagination, but the body itself out there. When the object goes, the motion goes on, as memory, or as a dream. The ancients never worked this out, so they took those images for things really outside them.

@chapter How demons were made
@list heading="Two schools, one name" items="Incorporeal forms, putting on airy bodies@incorporeal|Bodies of air or subtler matter@bodies|Both called them demons@demons"
Two schools arose. Some said the images are incorporeal forms that put on airy bodies to be seen. Others said they're living bodies, made of air or something subtler. Both called them demons.

@art ghost-in-glass
It's as if the dead they dreamed of weren't inhabitants of their own brain, but of the air, or heaven, or hell. Like seeing your own ghost in a looking glass. And the heathen rulers, seeing how useful the fear was, let the poets regulate it: good demons as a spur to obedience, evil ones as reins against breaking the laws.

@words text="Angels and spirits, good and evil · but not incorporeal"
The Greeks carried this to the Jews, and anyone mad or epileptic was said to have a devil. Why did Christ not correct it? Hobbes says that question is more curious than necessary for salvation. I find in Scripture, he says, that there be angels and spirits, good and evil, but not that they are incorporeal.

@chapter No image of God
@quote text="And therefore there can bee no Image of God; nor of the Soule of Man; nor of Spirits; but onely of Bodies Visible" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 45"
Every image is figured, and figure is a quantity every way determined. So there can be no image of a thing infinite. And therefore there can be no image of God, nor of the soul of man, nor of spirits, but only of bodies visible.

@chapter The lab's names
@math heading="Definitions" items="body = dimension@body;;phantasm = seeming, brain@phantasm;;spirit = body, subtle, invisible@spirit;;cannot add: dimension + incorporeal@cannot be added"
The lab defines the names as sums. A body has dimension. A phantasm is a seeming in the brain. A spirit, as Scripture speaks of it, is a body, subtle and invisible. And dimension and incorporeal cannot be added.

@math heading="A demon is a body?" items="Gentiles: demon = seeming, brain, incorporeal@Gentiles;;sum has dimension and incorporeal: absurd@absurd;;Hobbes: demon = seeming, brain: no clash: false@Hobbes's;;Scripture: demon = spirit: true@Scripture"
Now take the claim, a demon is a body. On the Gentiles' definition, a demon is a seeming taken to be incorporeal, so adding dimension is absurd. On Hobbes's, a demon is just a phantasm, so there's no clash, only a missing part: false. And if a demon is a spirit, as Scripture speaks, it's true.

@art three-definitions
Across the eight claims, the Gentiles' preset gives four true, one false, three absurd. Hobbes's gives three true, three false, two absurd. Scripture's gives three true, two false, three absurd. Define the demon as incorporeal, then say anything bodily of it, and your own names contradict each other.

@words text="The verdicts are the lab's sorting"
A caution. Those four verdicts are the lab's sorting, not Hobbes's. For him, a false universal claim is itself absurd. So the lab's false means only not borne out by these definitions.

@chapter Worship and idolatry
@art hat-before-throne
The second half is about worship: words and actions that betoken fear to offend, or desire to please. It's civil or divine according to the worshipper's intention, not the words. Take your hat off before a throne, and you honour the prince, not the stool. Suppose his soul is in the stool, and petition it, and that's idolatry.

@art broken-serpent
A man forced by terror of death to worship gives no worship, so no idolatry. And drawing a picture from fancy is no sin. Holding it for a representation of God is. Hobbes asks Christian sovereigns to do as Hezekiah did to the brazen serpent, and break down the images their subjects worship.

@art empty-bottles
The chapter ends with relics of the Gentiles in the church: canonising saints, the title Pontifex Maximus, processions, holy water, maypoles. Old empty bottles of Gentilism, he calls them, filled with new wine. The church's defenders answered that it had baptised those customs, not been taken over by them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The lab's verdicts are Hobbes's@The first|Hobbes denies angels and spirits@The second|He condemns all pictures@And the third"
The first slip is taking the lab's verdicts for Hobbes's. The second is thinking Hobbes denies there are angels or spirits. He finds them in Scripture, as subtle bodies. And the third is thinking he condemns pictures. He condemns worshipping them as if God were in them.
