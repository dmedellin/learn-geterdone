@chapter The judge's question
@title text="Of the Benefit That Proceedeth from Such Darkness" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 47" art=thumb
In any detective story, the question isn't only who could have done it, but who's better off. Hobbes has spent three chapters on doctrines he thinks false. Now he asks the judge's question of them.

@quote text="For amongst Praesumptions, there is none that so evidently declareth the Author, as doth the BENEFIT of the Action." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 47"
A severe Roman judge, Cassius, used to ask accusers, cui bono: what profit did the accused get? For amongst presumptions, there is none that so evidently declareth the author, as doth the benefit of the action. Notice the word. It's a presumption, the best there is, not a proof.

@chapter The root doctrine
@art crown-and-keys
The first doctrine is the root one: that the church now on earth is the kingdom of God. It gives the pastors a right to govern the church, and so the commonwealth. By it the pope made subjects believe that to disobey him was to disobey Christ, which is in effect a universal monarchy over Christendom.

@words text="The Romane, and the Presbyterian Clergy"
And not only Rome. Where the Presbytery took over, it kept the doctrine that Christ's kingdom began at the Resurrection, and claimed the power to excommunicate a lawful king. So the authors of this darkness in religion, Hobbes concludes, are the Roman and the Presbyterian clergy.

@chapter The dozen
@list heading="Twelve doctrines, among them" items="The pope cannot err@cannot err|Bishops hold their right from the pope@bishops|Clergy exempt from civil law@exemption|Priests may not marry@marriage|Confession@confession|Purgatory and indulgences@purgatory|The schoolmen's Aristotle@Aristotle"
Then a dozen more. That the pope cannot err. That bishops hold their right from him, not their sovereign. The clergy's exemption from the civil laws. Denying priests marriage. Confession, which gives intelligence of princes' designs. Purgatory and indulgences, which enrich the clergy. And Aristotle in the universities, which keeps the errors from being found out.

@chapter Counting the table
@math heading="All fourteen doctrines" items="pope: 7@pope;;Roman clergy: 9@Roman clergy;;Presbytery: 1@Presbytery;;sovereigns: 0@sovereigns"
The lab codes each doctrine with the parties it benefits, as the lesson reads Hobbes's sentences, and counts. With all fourteen, the pope is served by seven, the Roman clergy by nine, the Presbytery by one, and the sovereigns by none. The most served is the Roman clergy.

@math heading="Change the list" items="held, the twelve: pope 6, clergy 8, Presbytery 0@twelve;;root, the two: pope 1, clergy 1, Presbytery 1@two root"
Count only the twelve, and it's pope six, Roman clergy eight, Presbytery none. Count only the two root doctrines, and the three parties tie at one each, which is Hobbes's own pairing of the two clergies.

@art scales-of-benefit
But the count has limits. Every doctrine weighs one, however large its benefit, and the answer depends on the coding. And a doctrine can profit someone without being invented for that. The church's defenders said councils and theologians developed these doctrines because they believed them. You can even put the question to Hobbes's own book: sovereign supremacy in religion benefits sovereigns.

@chapter Three knots
@art three-knots
Hobbes then tells how the web was woven, in three knots on Christian liberty. First, presbyters agreed what to teach, and shunned the disobedient. Second, the presbyters of the chief cities took the name of bishops. Third, the bishop of Rome took authority over all the others.

@words text="The way is the same, but the order is inverted"
In England they came undone in reverse. Elizabeth dissolved the pope's power. The Presbyterians put down the bishops. Then their power went too, leaving the independency of the first Christians. That, without contention, he says, is perhaps the best. And the sovereigns who let it all happen were accessaries to their own damage.

@chapter The kingdom of fairies
@art ghost-on-grave
Then the chapter's famous joke. The papacy is no other than the ghost of the deceased Roman Empire, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof.

@art fairy-ring
The hierarchy may be compared, not unfitly, to the kingdom of fairies. The fairies have one king, Oberon, and the clergy one, the pope. Both inhabit darkness and graveyards, can't be brought to answer in court, and take the cream, or the tithes.

@art swept-house
He means it as an argument: the pope's power outside his own dominion consists only in the fear seduced people have of excommunication. And he ends with a warning. The spirit cast out may return, or a worse assembly of spirits may enter this clean swept house.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Cui bono proves authorship@The first|Hobbes spares the Presbytery@The second|The count measures guilt@And the third"
The first slip is treating cui bono as proof. It's a presumption. The second is thinking Hobbes spares the Presbytery. He names them with Rome. And the third is reading the count as a measure of guilt. Every doctrine weighs one.
