@chapter Can one man do it all?
@title text="A Review, and Conclusion" sub="Leviathan" art=thumb
Hobbes ends the book with an objection. Civil duty seems to need contrary things in one man: judgment and quick fancy, reason and eloquence, courage and fear of the laws. Surely no one has them all.

@art bricks-and-straw
They're great difficulties, he answers, but not impossibilities. Education and discipline reconcile them, in turns. Like the Israelites in Egypt, some days fixed to making bricks, and other days ranging about to gather straw.

@words text="Sidney Godolphin" sub="hating no man, nor hated of any"
And he names an instance, his friend Sidney Godolphin, who had clear judgment and wide fancy, strong reason and graceful speech, courage for war and fear for the laws. Hating no man, nor hated of any, he was killed at the beginning of the civil war.

@chapter The added law
@quote text="every man is bound by Nature, as much as in him lieth, to protect in Warre, the Authority, by which he is himself protected in time of Peace." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, A Review, and Conclusion"
Now the law. To the laws of nature, Hobbes would add this: every man is bound by nature, as much as in him lies, to protect in war the authority by which he is himself protected in time of peace.

@art shield-and-crown
His reason is a contradiction. You can't claim a right of nature to preserve your own body, and a right to destroy the one by whose strength it's preserved. It follows from the other laws, he says, but the times require it to be remembered.

@chapter The covenant, in numbers
@words text="Protection 10 · obeying costs 3 · penalty 6, half the time" sub="sovereign: costs 4, gets 6"
The lab treats it as a covenant where the sovereign protects first, with numbers the lesson invents. Protection is worth ten to the subject, obeying costs him three, and the penalty is six, applied half the time.

@math heading="Protected preset" items="keep: 10 − 3 = 7@keeping;;break: 10 − (1/2)(6) = 7: level, keeps@breaking;;least penalty: 3 ÷ 1/2 = 6@least penalty;;sovereign: 6 − 4 = 2@two ahead"
Keeping is ten minus three, seven. Breaking is ten minus half of six, also seven. Level, so he keeps. The least penalty is three over a half, six. And the sovereign, paying four and getting six, is two ahead.

@art weak-sword
Weaken the sword to four. Breaking is now eight against seven, so the subject does better to break, and the sovereign who protected first is four behind. That's Chapter seventeen again: without a power that makes breach cost more, covenants are but words.

@math heading="Failed preset: protection gone" items="keep: 0 − 3 = −3@minus three;;break: 0 − (1/10)(6) = −3/5@three fifths;;least penalty: 3 ÷ 1/10 = 30@thirty"
Now let protection fail. It's worth nothing, and the sword reaches him one time in ten. Keeping is minus three. Breaking is minus three fifths. The least penalty that would hold him is thirty. Obeying has become a bare cost. Hobbes's word is that he then has the liberty to submit.

@chapter When to submit
@art garrison
That was the question of the day. In sixteen fifty, the new republic asked men to promise to be faithful to it. Hobbes's answer: you become the conqueror's subject when, having liberty to submit, you consent by words or other sufficient sign. You have that liberty when the means of your life are within the enemy's guards and garrisons.

@words text="Conquest is not the victory itself"
Conquest isn't the victory itself, but the right over persons acquired by it. The slain are overcome, not conquered, and a man in chains is still an enemy. Conquest is made in submission, promising obedience for life and liberty. And a contract lawfully made cannot lawfully be broken.

@chapter Hobbes judges his book
@art new-wine
He thinks the principles true and the reasoning solid. The doctrines of Part Three are new, he admits, but now, when men call for truth as well as peace, they're new wine in new cask. And he skips the fashion for quoting the ancients. If we reverence age, the present is the oldest.

@words text="Without other designe, than to set before mens eyes the mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience"
His last paragraph says the book was written without partiality, and without other design than to set before men's eyes the mutual relation between protection and obedience. Then he goes back to natural bodies. For truth that opposes no man's profit or pleasure is welcome to all.

@art closing-book
That's where Hobbes stops, and the read along stops with him. Every lab figure showed what follows from a definition, a payoff or a count. None said whether people are as Hobbes says. Whether protection and obedience stand together is for each reader to weigh against the commonwealths he knows.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Obey whoever wins@The first|The added law makes obedience unconditional@The second|The numbers are Hobbes's@And the third"
The first slip is thinking you owe obedience to whoever wins. Conquest is made in submission. The second is reading the added law as unconditional. It binds you to an authority that protects you. And the third is reading the lab's numbers as Hobbes's.
