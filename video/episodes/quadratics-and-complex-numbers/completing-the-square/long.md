@chapter The idea
@title text="Completing the Square" sub="Quadratics and Complex Numbers" art=thumb
The square root property solves x minus h, squared, equals k in two lines. Completing the square is how you get any quadratic into that shape, so those two lines are always available.

@art square-gap
Picture x squared plus six x as a square with two strips along its sides. It's almost a bigger square, but one corner is missing. Fill that corner and you've completed the square.

@math heading="The constant" items="(x + p)² = x² + 2px + p²@x plus p;;b = 2p  →  p = b/2@then p is;;constant = (b/2)²@halve"
Here's the rule in symbols. x plus p, squared, is x squared plus two p x plus p squared. So if the coefficient of x is b, then p is b over two. In words: halve the coefficient of x, then square it.

@art square-filled
For x squared plus six x, half of six is three, and three squared is nine. So x squared plus six x plus nine is a perfect square, x plus three, squared.

@words text="(b/2)² = b²/4" sub="not b²/2"
Do it in that order: half, then square. That gives b squared over four. Square first and halve afterwards, and you get b squared over two, which is a different number for every b except zero.

@words text="x² − 5x + 25/4 = (x − 5/2)²" sub="the added constant is never negative"
The sign looks after itself. For x squared minus five x, half of minus five is minus five halves. Its square is twenty five quarters, which is positive, and the minus survives inside the bracket.

@chapter Solving an equation
@math heading="x² + 6x − 7 = 0" items="x² + 6x = 7@move the constant;;x² + 6x + 9 = 7 + 9@add nine;;(x + 3)² = 16@sixteen;;x + 3 = ±4@plus or minus four;;x = 1  or  x = −7@one or minus seven"
Solve x squared plus six x minus seven equals zero. Move the constant to the right. Then add nine to both sides. The left side is now x plus three, squared, and the right is sixteen. So x plus three is plus or minus four, and x is one or minus seven.

@art balance-scale
That nine on the right is what keeps the equation true. Add nine to the left alone, and you've made a different equation, with different solutions. A nicer left side is no consolation.

@chapter Divide by a first
@math heading="2x² + 8x − 10 = 0" items="x² + 4x − 5 = 0@divide every term;;x² + 4x + 4 = 5 + 4@half of four;;(x + 2)² = 9@squared equals nine;;x + 2 = ±3@plus or minus three;;x = 1  or  x = −5@one or minus five"
The rule assumes the x squared has coefficient one, so a goes first. For two x squared plus eight x minus ten, divide every term by two. Half of four is two, and two squared is four, so add four to both sides. x plus two, squared equals nine. So x plus two is plus or minus three, and x is one or minus five.

@art divide-first
Dividing by a is always legal, because a isn't zero. That's part of what makes it a quadratic.

@chapter Fractions are normal
@math heading="x² − 5x + 3 = 0" items="x² − 5x + 25/4 = −3 + 25/4@twenty five quarters;;(x − 5/2)² = 13/4@thirteen quarters;;x − 5/2 = ±√13 / 2@root thirteen over two;;x = (5 ± √13) / 2@five plus or minus"
Nothing says b is even. For x squared minus five x plus three, move the three across, then add twenty five quarters to both sides. That gives x minus five halves, squared, equals thirteen quarters. Take roots: root thirteen over two. So x is five plus or minus root thirteen, all over two.

@words text="(x − 1)² = −2/3" sub="no real solution, and that's finished"
Fractions here aren't a sign that something's gone wrong. And if a square completes to a negative number, like minus two thirds, there's no real solution. That's a finished answer too.

@chapter An expression has no sides
@math heading="x² + 6x + 1" items="(x² + 6x + 9) − 9 + 1@add nine and subtract nine;;(x + 3)² − 8@minus eight;;x = 0:  1  and  9 − 8 = 1@at zero"
With no equals sign, there's only one side. So add nine and subtract nine in the same expression. x squared plus six x plus one becomes x plus three, squared, minus eight. Check at zero: the original is one, and nine minus eight is one.

@art add-subtract
Adding to both sides, and adding and subtracting in one place, look different on the page. They're the same idea: change nothing overall.

@chapter Where people slip
@art tipped-scale
The commonest slip is adding the constant to one side only. x squared plus six x equals seven becomes x squared plus six x plus nine equals sixteen, not seven. The version with seven is a different equation, with solutions minus three plus or minus root seven, and those aren't the ones you wanted.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Adding the constant to one side only@That's the first|Completing before dividing by a@The second|Losing the subtracted constant@The third"
That's the first slip. The second is completing before dividing by a. For three x squared plus twelve x minus two, the number to halve is four, not twelve. The third is losing the subtracted constant. x squared plus six x plus one is x plus three, squared, minus eight, not plus one.
