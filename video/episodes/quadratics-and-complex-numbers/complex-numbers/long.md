@chapter The missing number
@title text="Complex Numbers" sub="Quadratics and Complex Numbers" art=thumb
The discriminant lesson ended with no real solution. The word doing the work there is real. x squared plus one equals zero needs a number whose square is minus one.

@art squares-above
No point on the number line has one, because the square of a real number is always zero or positive. So if you want that equation solved, you need a new number.

@chapter This has happened before
@list heading="Each system was extended" items="x + 3 = 1 → negative integers@x plus three|3x = 1 → fractions@three x equals one|x² = 2 → irrationals@x squared equals two|x² = −1 → i@minus one"
Every number system so far was extended for the same reason. x plus three equals one has no counting number answer, so negatives were joined on. Three x equals one needed fractions. x squared equals two needed the irrationals. And now x squared equals minus one needs one more.

@art nested-systems
Each time, the new numbers got called names: unnatural, absurd, irrational, imaginary. The names stuck. The objections didn't. Imaginary is a seventeenth century insult that hardened into a technical term. It doesn't make these numbers any less legitimate.

@chapter The number i
@math heading="The imaginary unit" items="i² = −1@i squared;;r² ≥ 0 for every real r@every real number"
The imaginary unit i is defined by exactly one property: i squared equals minus one. It isn't a real number, and that's a consequence, not an assumption. Every real number squares to zero or more, and minus one is less than zero.

@math heading="Complex number" items="a + bi,  a and b real@a plus b i;;3 − 7i:  real part 3,  imaginary part −7@three minus seven i"
A complex number is one you can write as a plus b i, with a and b real. a is the real part, and b is the imaginary part. So three minus seven i has real part three, and imaginary part minus seven. Not minus seven i. Both parts are real numbers.

@art reals-inside
Every real number is complex too. Five is five plus zero i. The reals aren't replaced. They sit inside, as the complex numbers with imaginary part zero, and everything you've proved about them still holds. A number with real part zero and a non-zero imaginary part is called pure imaginary.

@chapter Equal means both parts
@math heading="Equality" items="a + bi = c + di  ⟺  a = c and b = d@exactly when;;a − c = (d − b)i@rearranging;;i = (a − c)/(d − b) would be real  ✗@divide"
Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts match and their imaginary parts match. Why? Rearranging gives a minus c equals d minus b, times i. If d minus b weren't zero, you could divide, and i would be a quotient of real numbers, so real. It isn't.

@art matching-points
So d equals b, and then a equals c. The other direction is easy: if both parts match, the two expressions are identical. Matching both parts is exactly what equal means here.

@art complex-plane
So one equation between complex numbers is two equations between real numbers. That's why a plus b i behaves like the point a, b in a plane, with the reals along the horizontal axis and the pure imaginary numbers up the vertical.

@art no-order
That plane picture explains something missing too. Points in a plane don't come in an order. So positive, greater than, and between aren't available for complex numbers. Those words only apply to real numbers.

@chapter Roots of negatives
@math heading="√(−k) = i√k,  k > 0" items="√(−9) = 3i@nine;;√(−25) = 5i@twenty five;;√(−12) = 2i√3@twelve"
For positive k, the root of minus k is defined as i root k. Square it and you get minus k. So root minus nine is three i, root minus twenty five is five i, and root minus twelve is two i root three. Convert to i before you do anything else.

@math heading="(−6 + √(−8)) / 2" items="√(−8) = 2i√2@convert the root;;(−6 + 2i√2) / 2 = −3 + i√2@divide both terms"
This is the shape a quadratic hands you. Minus six plus root minus eight, over two. Convert the root first: two i root two. Then divide both terms by two to get minus three plus i root two. That's standard form, with real part minus three and imaginary part root two.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Writing √(−9) = −3@The first|Calling the imaginary part bi@The second|Treating reals and complex as rivals@The third"
The first slip is writing root minus nine as minus three. Minus three squared is nine, not minus nine. The second is calling the imaginary part b i, when it's b. The third is treating the reals and the complex numbers as rival systems. x squared minus four equals zero still has exactly two and minus two.
