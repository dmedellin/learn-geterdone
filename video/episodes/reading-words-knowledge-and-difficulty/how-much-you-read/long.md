@chapter The question
@title text="How Much You Read" sub="Reading Faster · Words, Knowledge and Difficulty" art=thumb
If you want to read more this year, should you read faster, or read for longer? You can settle it with one multiplication.

@art product-boxes
A year of reading is minutes a day, times your rate, times days. The lab takes the year as three hundred and sixty five days, and a book as ninety thousand words.

@art year-grid
Put in the minutes you actually read, not the minutes you mean to, and your rate from your log. For a week, note the minutes you really spend, and take the average.

@chapter Three readers
@math heading="Twenty minutes at 250" items="20 × 250 × 365 = 1,825,000 words@twenty times two fifty;;≈ 20.3 books@twenty point three books"
Start with a reader who reads twenty minutes a day at two hundred and fifty words a minute. Twenty times two fifty times three sixty five is one million, eight hundred and twenty five thousand words a year. That's about twenty point three books.

@math heading="Faster, or longer?" items="faster: 20 × 300 × 365 = 2,190,000 ≈ 24.3 books@three hundred;;longer: 30 × 250 × 365 = 2,737,500 ≈ 30.4 books@thirty minutes"
Now raise the rate to three hundred and keep the twenty minutes: two million, one hundred and ninety thousand words, about twenty four point three books. Or keep the rate at two fifty and read for thirty minutes: two million, seven hundred and thirty seven thousand, five hundred words, about thirty point four books.

@art book-stacks
Ten more minutes a day adds nine hundred and twelve thousand, five hundred words a year, about ten point one books. Reading at three hundred adds three hundred and sixty five thousand, about four point one books.

@chapter The rate that would match
@math heading="Matching ten minutes by rate" items="+10 on 20 minutes = +50% time@fifty percent more time;;250 × 30 / 20 = 375 wpm@three seventy five;;20 × 375 × 365 = 2,737,500@the same"
Why so lopsided? Ten more minutes on twenty is fifty percent more time, so it adds fifty percent more words. To get the same from rate alone, two fifty would have to become two fifty times three halves: three seventy five. Twenty minutes at three seventy five gives the same two million, seven hundred and thirty seven thousand, five hundred.

@art two-levers
A rate gain of half is far beyond what any technique in this Subject has reliably shown. Even a twenty percent gain, from two fifty to three hundred, adds less than half of what the ten minutes add. And ten minutes is a choice you can make tomorrow.

@chapter Volume and growth
@art diary
There's a second reason the minutes matter. Anderson, Wilson and Fielding, in nineteen eighty eight, had a hundred and fifty five fifth graders keep diaries of their time out of school. Time spent reading books was the best predictor of their growth in reading.

@art growing-words
Nagy and Herman, in nineteen eighty seven, argued from estimates of how many words children learn each year that most of that vocabulary is picked up from context while reading, rather than taught.

@words text="Correlational" sub="readers who read more grow more; the studies can't fully separate the two"
Both lines of evidence are correlational, or rest on estimates. Readers who read more grow more, and the studies can't fully separate the reading from the readers. For an adult, they suggest a direction, not a size.

@chapter The course, together
@list heading="Putting it together" items="A rate is a rate on a kind of text@a kind of text|It falls on hard text, unknown words, unfamiliar subjects@It falls|What you know comes largely from what you've read@largely from what you've read"
Put the course together. A rate is a rate on a kind of text. It falls on hard text, on text full of unknown words, and on unfamiliar subjects. And the words and subjects you know come, on the best estimates, largely from what you've read.

@words text="Reading more is the larger lever"
Reading faster is worth training, and this Subject trains it every day. But reading more is the larger lever on how much you read. And if the correlational studies point the right way, it's also the one that makes the next text easier.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="To read more, read faster@The first|Counting intended minutes@The second|Reading the correlation as proof@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the way to read more is to read faster. The second is counting intended minutes: a week of noted minutes is a measurement, and a resolution is not. And the third is reading the correlation as proof.

@chapter Try it
@words text="15 minutes a day · 240 wpm · 365 days" sub="words a year?"
Your turn. A reader reads fifteen minutes a day at two hundred and forty words a minute. How many words is that in a year? Fifteen times two forty is three thousand six hundred a day, and times three sixty five, one million, three hundred and fourteen thousand.
