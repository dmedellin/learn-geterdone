@chapter The question
@title text="Unknown Words and Coverage" sub="Reading Faster · Words, Knowledge and Difficulty" art=thumb
How many words on a page can you not know before the page stops making sense? To answer that, you need one measurement: coverage.

@words text="coverage = known tokens / all tokens"
Coverage is the share of a text's running words, its tokens, that you know. Known tokens over all tokens. And from the same count, you can say how often an unknown word turns up: the tokens over the unknown ones.

@chapter The drill
@art shield
The drill gives you The Grammar of Heraldry, a hard passage of three hundred and eighty words. Read it once at your own pace, and mark every word you don't know as you meet it. Mark a word each time it appears, then count your marks.

@art marked-page
Why each time? Because coverage counts tokens, not dictionary words. A term you don't know that appears six times interrupts the reading six times, so it counts six times. That's how often you meet the gap.

@chapter Three coverages
@math heading="A 500-word page" items="10 unknown: 490 / 500 = 98%, one in 50@ten unknown;;25 unknown: 475 / 500 = 95%, one in 20@twenty five;;50 unknown: 450 / 500 = 90%, one in 10@fifty unknown"
Take a five hundred word page. With ten unknown tokens, that's four ninety over five hundred, ninety eight percent, one unknown in every fifty. With twenty five, ninety five percent, one in every twenty. With fifty unknown, ninety percent, one in every ten.

@art density-rows
Ninety five percent sounds close to ninety eight. But on the page, it's an unknown word in nearly every sentence, instead of one in every two or three. As shares, they're three points apart. As densities, they're very different reading.

@chapter What the studies found
@words text="Hu and Nation (2000)" sub="learners of English · one story · different coverages"
The best known study is Hu and Nation's, from two thousand. They gave learners of English in New Zealand one story, in versions at different coverages, and tested their comprehension.

@words text="95%: some understood, most did not" sub="≈ 98% estimated for unassisted reading"
At ninety five percent, some of the readers understood the story adequately, but most did not. Hu and Nation estimated that about ninety eight percent was needed for most such readers to read a text of that kind unassisted.

@art line-not-threshold
That figure has been argued over since. Laufer and Ravenhorst Kalovski, in twenty ten, proposed ninety five as a minimum and ninety eight as optimal. Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe, in twenty eleven, found comprehension rising roughly in a line with coverage, with no threshold where it jumped.

@words text="Learners of English" sub="for a fluent native reader: an extrapolation"
All of these concern learners of English. For a fluent native reader, the figures are an extrapolation, and a reasonable one only as a rough guide.

@chapter Reading your result
@art held-definitions
If your coverage of the heraldry passage was in the low nineties, you met an unknown word in almost every sentence. The passage defines many of its terms, but each definition has to be held while the next term arrives. Near ninety eight or above, look instead at the long sentences.

@art learn-words-first
The drill isn't timed, because marking interrupts the reading. What it gives you is the count. And a reader who regularly meets one unknown word in every twenty is likely to gain more from learning the field's words than from a change of pace.

@words text="Same few terms? Look them up first"
If the same few terms account for most of your marks, look them up first, and read the page again later.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Context will supply one in twenty@The first|Counting each unknown word once@The second|Reading 95% and 98% as nearly the same@And the third"
The first slip is assuming one unknown word in twenty is fine because context will supply it. None of the studies found that context reliably fills the gaps at ninety five percent. The second is counting each unknown word once. And the third is reading ninety five and ninety eight percent as nearly the same.

@chapter Try it
@words text="19 unknown tokens in 380 words" sub="coverage? one in every how many?"
Your turn. A reader marks nineteen unknown tokens in a passage of three hundred and eighty words. What's their coverage, and how often does an unknown word turn up? Three sixty one over three eighty is nineteen twentieths, ninety five percent. And three eighty over nineteen: one in every twenty.
