@chapter The question
@title text="What You Already Know" sub="Reading Faster · Words, Knowledge and Difficulty" art=thumb
Two texts, the same length, the same readability grade. One is about something you know, and one isn't. Will they read the same? The formula says yes. Let's test it.

@art suitcase-and-slide
The drill gives you two procedures. Packing for a Week Away, and Performing a Gram Stain, from a laboratory. Read each once at your natural pace and answer six questions. The packing passage is logged as a test, and the Gram stain as a condition, so a reading slowed by an unfamiliar topic stays out of your test medians.

@chapter Matched by the formula
@math heading="By the counts" items="Packing: 385 words · 16 sentences · grade ≈ 9.4@three eighty five;;Gram stain: 375 words · 18 sentences · grade ≈ 8.9@three seventy five"
By the counts, they're as close as two texts on different subjects get. Packing for a Week Away has three eighty five words in sixteen sentences, a grade of about nine point four. Performing a Gram Stain has three seventy five words in eighteen sentences, about eight point nine.

@art same-grade
So if the formula measured difficulty, the Gram stain would be the slightly easier read. Whether it was easier for you is what the drill measures.

@chapter A typical reader
@math heading="Typical preset" items="familiar: 385 × 60 / 96.25 = 240, × 6/6 = 240@ninety six point two five;;unfamiliar: 375 × 60 / 100 = 225, × 4/6 = 150@a hundred seconds;;150 / 240 = 5/8 = 62.5%@five eighths"
Here's a typical reader. The packing passage took ninety six point two five seconds, two forty words a minute, six of six. The Gram stain took a hundred seconds, two twenty five a minute, with four of six. Effective rates of two forty and one fifty, and a ratio of five eighths, sixty two point five percent.

@art steps-held
The rate fell a little, and the questions fell more. The steps of the stain had to be taken in one by one, and two of them didn't stay. That's why you compare effective rates: an unfamiliar topic can cost time, understanding, or both.

@chapter An expert reader
@math heading="Expert preset" items="familiar: 240 × 6/6 = 240@the packing passage;;Gram stain: 375 × 60 / 90 = 250, × 6/6 = 250@ninety seconds;;250 / 240 = 25/24 ≈ 104.2%@twenty five twenty fourths"
Now a reader who has worked in a microbiology laboratory. The packing passage is the same, two forty and six of six. The Gram stain takes them ninety seconds, two fifty a minute, six of six. That's a ratio of twenty five twenty fourths, about a hundred and four point two percent. For them, the specialist passage is the familiar one.

@art puzzle-fit
What you know tells you what a sentence is likely to say before you finish it, and where each new fact fits. Without it, every step has to be held in mind on its own. No change of technique gave the expert their extra hundred effective words a minute. What they knew did.

@chapter The studies
@art baseball
The best known demonstration is Recht and Leslie's, from nineteen eighty eight. Sixty four junior high students, sorted by reading ability and by how much they knew about baseball, read a passage about part of a game. Knowledge of baseball mattered more than reading ability for what they recalled.

@words text="Recall, not speed" sub="young readers · Rayner et al. (2016): vocabulary at the heart of speed"
But they measured recall, not speed, and their readers were young. Rayner and colleagues' twenty sixteen review puts language skill, vocabulary above all, at the heart of reading speed. What you know of the topic is the part this drill isolates.

@chapter What follows
@art intro-first
Here's the least glamorous finding in the Subject, and one of the most useful. In a field you read often, the gain most within reach is often to know more of the field: an introductory text first, then the hard one. That's an inference from studies of recall, not something they timed.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Speed is independent of what you know@The first|Trusting the grade to predict your reading@The second|Comparing rates, not effective rates@And the third"
The first slip is believing speed is a skill independent of what you know. The second is trusting the grade to predict your reading. And the third is comparing the rates and not the effective rates. And remember, one question is about sixteen point seven points, so a one question difference is not a result.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Familiar: 260 wpm, 6/6 · Unfamiliar: 225 wpm, 4/6" sub="ratio of the effective rates?"
Your turn. A familiar passage at two sixty words a minute with six of six, and an unfamiliar one at two twenty five with four of six. What's the ratio of the effective rates? One fifty over two sixty: fifteen twenty sixths, about fifty seven point seven percent.
