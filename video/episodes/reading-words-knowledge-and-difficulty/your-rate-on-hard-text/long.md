@chapter The question
@title text="Your Rate on Hard Text" sub="Reading Faster · Words, Knowledge and Difficulty" art=thumb
When a text gets harder, does your reading rate drop? Or does your rate hold, and your understanding give way instead? You can find out with two readings.

@art easy-hard-pages
Read an easy passage, The Octopus, at the pace you'd use if no one were timing you, and answer six questions. Then do the same with a hard one, From Drifting Continents to Moving Plates. The lab alternates which comes first. Both go in your log as tests, each with its band, easy and hard.

@words text="395 words · 391 words" sub="grade ≈ 6.8 · grade ≈ 17.3"
The passages are close in length, three ninety five words and three ninety one, and far apart in difficulty. By the formula the next lesson teaches, The Octopus is about grade six point eight, and the continents passage about grade seventeen point three.

@chapter Slow down and keep it
@math heading="Big-drop preset" items="easy: 395 × 60 / 79 = 300 wpm, 6/6@three ninety five;;hard: 391 × 60 / 138 = 170 wpm, 5/6@three ninety one"
Some readers slow down a long way and keep their score. Three ninety five words in seventy nine seconds is three hundred a minute, with six of six. Three ninety one in a hundred and thirty eight seconds is one seventy, with five of six.

@math heading="Big-drop preset" items="170 / 300 = 17/30 ≈ 56.7%@seventeen thirtieths;;170 × 5/6 ≈ 141.7 effective@one forty one point seven"
Divide the hard rate by the easy rate. One seventy over three hundred is seventeen thirtieths, about fifty six point seven percent. And the hard effective rate is about one forty one point seven.

@art ratio-bars
A ratio near one says the pace held. A ratio well below one says it fell. This reader slowed to a little over half their easy rate, and kept most of the meaning.

@chapter Hold the pace and lose it
@art two-patterns
Other readers barely change pace, and pay for it in the questions. The held rate preset read the easy passage at two fifty, six of six, and the hard one at two forty, with three of six.

@math heading="Held-rate preset" items="240 / 250 = 24/25 = 96%@ninety six percent;;easy effective: 250@two fifty on the easy;;hard effective: 240 × 3/6 = 120@one twenty"
The rate ratio is twenty four twenty fifths, ninety six percent. That looks like a reader untroubled by difficulty, until you see the effective rates: two fifty on the easy passage, and one twenty on the hard one. Half the hard passage went by without being understood, at nearly full speed.

@chapter What the research says
@art gear-down
That second pattern is the one Ronald Carver described in his nineteen ninety review. Readers tend to keep a nearly constant rate, the rauding rate, across texts within their level, and when the text gets harder it's their comprehension that falls.

@words text="Learning gear ≈ 200 wpm" sub="Carver (1990), college students"
On text well above their level, they drop into a slower gear, learning, at about two hundred standard length words a minute for the college students he studied.

@art fixations-longer
The eye movement studies show the same thing from the inside. Rayner's nineteen ninety eight review reports that on difficult text, fixations get longer and regressions more frequent. Each glance costs more time, and more of them go backwards.

@chapter One pair, one day
@words text="1 question ≈ 16.7 points" sub="a one-question difference is not a result"
Two readings differ in more than difficulty. The passages differ in topic too, and whichever you read second, you read a little more practised, or more tired. One question is about sixteen point seven points, so a one question difference is not a result.

@words text="Carver describes readers on average"
And if your readings go the other way, you haven't refuted Carver's review, and it hasn't refuted you. It describes readers on average. Yours is one pair, on one day.

@chapter A rate on a kind of text
@art kind-of-text
What this settles is narrower and more useful: a rate is a rate on a kind of text. A figure measured on easy text says little about how fast you can read a contract, a paper or a manual with the sense intact.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating your speed as one number@The first|The ratio without the scores@The second|One pair as the verdict@And the third"
The first slip is treating your reading speed as one number. The second is reading the rate ratio without the scores, because ninety six percent can hide a halved effective rate. And the third is taking one pair of readings as the verdict. Repeat it on other texts first.

@chapter Try it
@words text="“Graduates read 600 wpm”" sub="short magazine pieces · three easy questions"
Your turn. A course says its graduates read six hundred words a minute, measured on short magazine pieces with three easy questions. What does that tell you about reading a technical report? Little. It's a rate on easy text, with questions too easy to catch a held pace and a lost meaning.
