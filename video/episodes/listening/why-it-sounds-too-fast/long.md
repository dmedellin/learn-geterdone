@chapter Too fast?
@title text="Why It Sounds Too Fast" sub="Listening" art=thumb
Ask people learning English what's hardest, and a great many say the same thing. People speak too fast. They can read the page, and they know the words. But said out loud, it goes right past them.

@art fast-stream
Usually, speed isn't the problem. The problem is a short list of small words. They make up about two words in every five you hear, and most of them change shape when they're said.

@chapter Squashed words
@art squash
English has a group of very common words that change shape when they aren't stressed. This course calls them squashed, or says they take a weak form. To becomes a short, flat sound. And loses most of itself. Of, was, have, for and can all do the same.

@art small-list
There are fewer than fifty of them, and that's the whole list. Everything else keeps its shape. Even some short, common words aren't on it: it, in, on, by, with, my, did, they and who. They have no squashed form.

@chapter Counting a real page
@words text="387 of 936 words  =  41.3%" sub="from only 43 different words"
The lab counts them on a real page, nine hundred and thirty-six words from the novel Pride and Prejudice. Three hundred and eighty-seven of those words are on the list. That's forty-one point three percent. And just forty-three different words account for all of it.

@art two-in-five
So two words in every five reaching your ear come from that list. Most of them arrive in a shape you may never have been shown. The words carrying the meaning are still said clearly. What's between them has been flattened.

@chapter What happens to them
@math heading="Written → said, when not stressed" items="and → ən   ·   the → ðə@And becomes;;to → tə   ·   of → əv@To becomes;;was → wəz   ·   that → ðət@Was becomes;;for → fə   ·   have → həv@For becomes;;can → kən   ·   you → jə@And can becomes"
Here are ten, written and then said. And becomes un. The becomes thuh. To becomes tuh, and of becomes uv. Was becomes wuz, and that becomes thut. For becomes fuh, and have becomes huv. And can becomes kun, and you becomes yuh.

@art same-shape
Notice the vowel. Most of these squashed forms use the same one: a short, flat sound made in the middle of the mouth. So they're not only short. They also sound like each other. That's why a run of small words can arrive as a single noise.

@words text="he · she · we · his · him  →  a short i" sub="is · will · not  →  it's · I'll · don't"
A few pronouns take a short i sound instead: he, she, we, his and him. And three words lose their vowel altogether and lean on the word before: is, will and not. That's how it's, I'll and don't come about.

@chapter When they stay full
@art end-of-line
The count of three hundred and eighty-seven is the most it could be. A word on the list keeps its full shape when it's stressed. Most also keep it when they're left at the end of a sentence. Who are you looking at? says at in full. Yes, I can says can in full.

@words text="I saw him" sub="him, her and you stay squashed at the end"
The words for people are different. I saw him ends on the squashed him, and her and you behave the same way. The lab counts twenty-four listed words that stand last before a mark, and half of those are him, her, he or you.

@words text="the book that I read  ·  that book  ·  have tea" sub="that, have, has, had: squashed only for grammar"
And four words, that, have, has and had, squash only when they do grammar work. The book that I read: squashed. That book, pointing at something: full. Have tea: full. The lab counts those four separately: forty-two of the three hundred and eighty-seven.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="thinking the words are being dropped@The first|learning only the full forms@The second|squashing every word every time@The third"
The first slip is thinking the words are being dropped. They aren't missing. They're short and flat, and the word was there. The second is learning only the full forms. In ordinary speech, the squashed one is normal. And the third is squashing every word on the list, every time.

@chapter Try it
@list heading="Four steps" items="mark the words from the list@Mark every|say the rest normally@Say the rest|squash the marked ones@Squash the|say it all again@Then say"
Try this on any sentence. Mark every word from the list. Say the rest normally. Squash the marked ones, short and flat. Then say the whole thing again. It'll be quicker, and closer to what you hear. Nothing was said faster. Fewer parts got full weight.
