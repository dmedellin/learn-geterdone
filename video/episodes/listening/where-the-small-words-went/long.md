@chapter Two words as one
@title text="Where the Small Words Went" sub="Listening" art=thumb
The first lesson of this course ended on three words that lose their vowel when they're said: is, will and not. They lean on the word before, and two words come out as one sound. Writing has a way to show that.

@art two-into-one
It's called a contraction. Two words are written as one, with an apostrophe where letters were left out. Do not becomes don't. I will becomes I'll. It is becomes it's. The part after the apostrophe is the small word, with its vowel gone.

@list heading="Seven endings" items="n't  =  not@for not|'ll  =  will@will, am|'m  =  am@am, are|'re  =  are@are and have|'ve  =  have@and have|'d  =  would or had@would or had|'s  =  is, has, or belonging@is, has"
Seven endings to know. They stand for not, will, am, are and have. The ending d stands for would or had. And the ending s can stand for is, has, or belonging.

@chapter Counting not
@art stage-vs-book
The lab counts every short not against every full not, in three texts. A play from eighteen ninety-five. A passage from a novel from eighteen thirteen. And two modern documents, a court opinion and a news story.

@math heading="How much of not is written n't" items="the play, 1895  ·  22 of 36  =  61.1%@In the play;;the novel, 1813  ·  1 of 13  =  7.7%@In the novel;;two documents, now  ·  0 of 10@in the two modern"
In the play, twenty-two of the thirty-six are short. That's sixty-one point one percent. In the novel, it's one of thirteen. And in the two modern documents, it's none of ten.

@art share-bars
Counted over each whole text, the play has a hundred and sixty-eight short ones against a hundred and forty-three full ones. That's fifty-four percent. The whole novel has twelve against one thousand three hundred and ninety-seven, under one percent. The samples point the same way as the whole texts.

@chapter Which texts contract
@words text="made to be said  ·  made to be read" sub="the play  ·  the novel and the documents"
So which kind of text contracts? Text that's made to be said. The play was written for people on a stage to talk to each other. Careful text made to be read contracts little, and that's as true of a court opinion from twenty twenty-five as of a novel from eighteen thirteen.

@words text="reading: the full form" sub="listening: the short form"
That's why the first lesson's list matters more for listening than for reading. The reader's text has the full form. The speaker's doesn't.

@chapter The seven commonest
@math heading="The play's seven commonest" items="don't  =  do not@Don't is;;isn't  =  is not@isn't;;it's  =  it is, or it has@it's;;I'm  =  I am@I'm;;won't  =  will not@won't;;can't  =  cannot@can't;;I'll  =  I will@I'll"
Here are the seven commonest in the whole play. Don't is do not. Then isn't, it's, I'm, won't, can't and I'll. Most are a whole word and a short ending. Put the word back, and the full form is there.

@art broken-piece
But won't is different. Cut the short not off, and what's left, w-o, isn't a word. The full form is will not. The two words were said as one for so long that the spelling changed. Shan't did the same: it's shall not. And can't is cannot.

@chapter Endings with two answers
@art fork-three
The d and s endings stand for more than one word, and the lab doesn't guess. You sort them by the word that follows. After d, a base verb means would. I'd give anything is I would give anything. A past form after it means had.

@words text="he's gone  =  he has gone" sub="he's going  =  he is going   ·   Mr. Worthing's ward  =  belonging"
After s, a past form means has, and an i-n-g form means is. He's gone is he has gone. He's going is he is going. And in Mr. Worthing's ward, the s shows that the ward belongs to him.

@chapter Listen hardest for n't
@art flip-arrow
So when you're listening, expect the short forms to be normal in talk. And listen hardest for the short not, because it's the one that turns the sentence round. I can go, and I can't go, say opposite things.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="taking a contraction for careless English@The first|reading every 's as is@The second|letting the short not slip by@And the third"
The first slip is taking a contraction for careless English. The play is carefully written, and most of its nots are short. The second is reading every s after an apostrophe as is. It can be has, or belonging. And the third is letting the short not slip by.

@chapter Try it
@words text="I'd give anything" sub="'d  =  would or had?"
Your turn. In I'd give anything, does the d stand for would or had? Try both. I had give anything isn't English, because had needs a past form after it. So it's I would give anything.
