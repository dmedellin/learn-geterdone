@chapter No spaces
@title text="Where a Word Begins" sub="Listening" art=thumb
Write a sentence down, and it comes with spaces in it. Say the same sentence, and the spaces are gone. The sound arrives as one run, and the person listening has to decide where each word stops.

@art no-gaps
You do this in your own language without noticing. In a new language, it's most of the difficulty, and almost nobody mentions it. So how does a listener find where a word begins?

@chapter The help English gives
@art strong-front
English gives a strong clue. Words that carry meaning are usually said with their first part hardest. Father, answer, carriage and happiness all lead with their strong part.

@words text="FA·ther  ·  AN·swer  ·  CAR·riage  ·  HAP·pi·ness" sub="S = said hardest  ·  . = said lightly"
So when you hear a strong part, a good guess is that a new word has just started. In the lab, every word outside the list of small words gets a mark for where it's said hardest.

@chapter How often the guess is right
@art four-of-five
On the page in the lab, four hundred and seventy-four of these words carry a mark for their strong part. Three hundred and ninety of them start on it. That's eighty-two point three percent, about four words in five.

@words text="390 of 474  =  82.3%" sub="marks from CMUdict, a list of American pronunciations"
The marks come from a list of American pronunciations, but British and American speakers put the strong part in the same place in nearly every word. A few names, rare words, and short grammar words like it, in and with carry no mark, so they aren't counted either way.

@art cut-points
Four times in five isn't sure, and it doesn't need to be. A listener uses this help many times a second, alongside everything else they know. A guess that's right four times in five is very strong help.

@words text="about 9 in 10 in conversation" sub="quoted from Cutler and Carter (1987), with a looser test"
Work on recorded conversation puts the figure closer to nine in ten. That's quoted from Cutler and Carter, in nineteen eighty-seven, and not counted on this page. Their test was also looser than the lab's.

@chapter It fits the last lesson
@words text="2 in 5: small words, said short" sub="most of the rest: strong at the front"
It also fits with the last lesson. About two words in five of what you hear are small words, said short. Most of the rest announce themselves by being said strongly at the front. Between them, the run of sound has a shape.

@chapter Where it fails
@art front-piece-light
Nearly one word in five starts on a light part instead. Believe. Again. Return. Another. Look at them, and a pattern appears. They begin with a small piece stuck on the front, and those pieces are almost never the strong part.

@math heading="Start strong  ·  start light" items="FAther  S.    ·    beLIEVE  .S@Father starts;;ANswer  S.    ·    aGAIN  .S@Answer;;MARket  S.    ·    reTURN  .S@Market;;HAPpiness  S..    ·    aNOTHer  .S.@Happiness"
Father starts strong, and believe starts light. Answer, and again. Market, and return. Happiness, and another.

@words text="be- · a- · re- · un- · de- · con-" sub="almost never the strong part"
So the failures aren't random. They start with a short set of front pieces: b-e, a, r-e, u-n, d-e and c-o-n. Once you know them, you know when to expect the strong part to come second.

@chapter What to do with this
@art let-go
So stop trying to catch everything. Listen for the strong parts, and take the meaning from the words carrying them. Let the small words go by. You can often work them out afterwards, without having heard them clearly. That isn't a trick for beginners. It's what a listener who grew up with the language is already doing.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="trying to hear every word equally@The first|reading the strong part off the spelling@The second|taking 82.3% as a rule@the third"
The first slip is trying to hear every word equally. The second is reading the strong part off the spelling. Record is two different words, depending on where it falls. And the third is taking eighty-two percent as a rule, not a guess. It's useful because it's high, not because it's certain.

@chapter Try it
@words text="father · market · again · answer" sub="which one starts light?"
Your turn. Which of these starts on a light part: father, market, again or answer? Pause and say them. Again begins with a small piece, a, and its strong part is second. The other three lead with their strong part.
