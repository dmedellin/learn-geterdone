@chapter What fills the space?
@title text="Why Words Run Together" sub="Listening" art=thumb
The last lesson said that speech has no spaces, and that a listener cuts the sound into words with the help of the strong parts. That leaves a question. What's in the place where the space would be?

@art gap-vanish
A written space is a rule of print. A voice doesn't stop for it. In take it, the k and the i are said straight after each other, and the k goes with it. In proud of, it's the d. The word is whole on the page, but not whole in the ear.

@chapter Three meetings
@art hop-consonant
The lab marks three kinds of meeting between words. The first: a consonant meets a vowel. Proud of. In a. Take it. The consonant is said with the next word.

@art vowel-blend
The second: a vowel meets a vowel. We are. I am. The army. There's nothing to move, so the two run on into one another, often with a faint w or y between them.

@art one-held
The third: the same consonant ends one word and starts the next. Not to. Ought to. It's said once, and held a little longer than one sound would be.

@chapter Counting them
@art three-counts
The lab marks all three on a passage of nine hundred and thirty-six words from Pride and Prejudice. It has seven hundred and fifty-six places where one space comes between two words. In sixteen, a word isn't in the dictionary, so the lab scores seven hundred and forty.

@math heading="740 scored places" items="consonant → vowel  ·  124  ·  16.8%@A hundred and twenty-four;;vowel → vowel  ·  53  ·  7.2%@Fifty-three;;the same consonant  ·  16  ·  2.2%@Sixteen have;;not marked  ·  547@five hundred and forty-seven"
A hundred and twenty-four are a consonant meeting a vowel, sixteen point eight percent. Fifty-three are a vowel meeting a vowel. Sixteen have the same consonant on both sides. The other five hundred and forty-seven aren't marked.

@words text="547 not marked" sub="not counted is not the same as nothing happens"
Those are a consonant meeting a consonant, or a vowel meeting a consonant. The page doesn't say what a speaker does there, and it doesn't say that nothing happens. So sixteen point eight percent isn't how often words run together.

@chapter Why it matters
@words text="proud of  →  proudov" sub="a · of · and · at · are"
Here's why this matters. Many small words from the first lesson come straight after a consonant: a, of, and, at, are. The word before has just taken their front, and they were already short and flat. A learner who knows only the written shape hears proud of as proudov, and doesn't find two words.

@words text="an apple  →  a napple" sub="an hour  →  a nower"
In a, the n doesn't stay behind. You hear something close to i na, and you have to know that the n belongs to in. The same thing turns an apple into something close to a napple, and an hour into a nower.

@art strong-anchor
The last lesson gives the way out. The strong part of proud is the front of the word. Listen for that, and what comes after it is of, whatever it sounds like.

@chapter What the counts don't say
@words text="poor Reynolds · your resolution · for writing" sub="3 of the 16 pairs are r"
The counts come from a text from eighteen thirteen, with sounds from an American dictionary. Of the sixteen same consonant pairs, ten are t, and three are r. A speaker from the south of England doesn't say that r before a consonant, so for them, the pair isn't the same sound twice.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="expecting a gap at every space@The first|reading 16.8% as how often words join@The second|counting every same letter pair as certain@And the third"
The first slip is expecting a gap wherever the page shows a space. A learner who waits for the break will always be a word behind. The second is reading sixteen point eight percent as how often words run together. And the third is counting every pair of the same letter as certain.

@chapter Try it
@words text="take it · in a · we are · not to" sub="say each pair as one word"
Your turn. Say each pair as if it were one word. Take it, like takit. In a, like i na. We are, running on. And not to, with one t. That's close to what arrives. You're listening for one run, with no gap in it.
