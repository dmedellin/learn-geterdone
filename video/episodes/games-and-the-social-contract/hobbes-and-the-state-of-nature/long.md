@chapter The state of nature
@title text="Hobbes and the State of Nature" sub="Games and the Social Contract" art=thumb
Without a sovereign, two rational and uncertain people attack each other. Put a penalty on attacking, and the equilibrium changes.

@list heading="Three causes of quarrel" items="Competition: invade for gain@competition|Diffidence: invade for safety@diffidence|Glory: invade for reputation@glory"
Hobbes finds three causes of quarrel in human nature: competition, diffidence and glory. Competition makes men invade for gain, diffidence for safety, and glory for reputation.

@quote text="solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" cite="Hobbes"
Without a common power to keep them all in awe, he writes, they're in a condition of war, of every man against every man. And life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

@art two-figures-apart
Competition and diffidence can be put in a table. Glory can't, so the lesson leaves it out rather than invent a payoff for it.

@chapter Competition
@math heading="Anarchy" items="peace, peace:  3, 3@each gets three;;attack on peace:  5, 0@takes five;;attack, attack:  1, 1@each gets one"
Take competition first. Two people each choose peace or attack. If both keep the peace, each gets three. An attacker who finds the other at peace takes five, and the victim gets zero. If both attack, each gets one.

@art attack-dominates
This is the prisoner's dilemma with new labels. Attack pays five against peace and one against attack, while peace pays three and zero. So attack dominates.

@words text="Only equilibrium: (attack, attack), 1 each"
Both attack, and each collects one, where both would have collected three at peace. The model needs no wicked people to get there: only that each prefers more to less and can't count on the other.

@chapter The sovereign
@art sword
Now let a sovereign fine whoever attacks, by four, whatever the target does. Subtract four from every payoff in which a person attacks, and leave the victim's payoffs alone.

@math heading="Penalty of 4" items="attack on peace:  5 − 4 = 1@now gets one;;attack, attack:  1 − 4 = −3@minus three;;vs peace: peace 3, attack 1 → peace@peace pays three;;vs attack: peace 0, attack −3 → peace@peace pays zero"
An attacker who finds the other at peace now gets one, and two attackers each get minus three. Against peace, peace pays three and attack pays one. Against attack, peace pays zero and attack pays minus three.

@words text="Only equilibrium: (peace, peace), 3 each, no fine paid"
So peace dominates, and the only equilibrium is peace with peace, three each. Nobody is fined there, so the penalty is never collected. A threat that works costs nothing to carry out.

@chapter Covenants without the sword
@math heading="How big a penalty?" items="penalty 1:  attack on peace pays 4 > 3@pays four;;penalty 2:  4 − 1 = 3, a tie@the two tie;;penalty above 2:  peace dominates@more than two"
Hobbes remarked that covenants without the sword are but words. In the table, a promise changes no payoff. Only the sword does, and it has to change them by enough: at a penalty of one, attacking a peaceful neighbour still pays four against three, at two the two tie, and it takes more than two.

@art scale-tip
Nothing in either table says the people have changed. The sovereign has changed what an attack costs, and with it what each person does in reply to the other.

@chapter Fear without greed
@art standoff
The third table has no gain from attacking at all. Peace with peace pays four each. Attacking pays three whatever the other does, because all it gets is the safety of not being caught unprepared, and a peaceful victim gets zero.

@math heading="Diffidence" items="peace, peace:  4, 4@four each;;attack:  3 whatever the other does@that pays three;;two equilibria@two equilibria"
With four each at peace and attack that pays three, this is the stag hunt, and it has two equilibria: peace with peace, and attack with attack. War can be reached by people who want nothing the other has. Peace is worth keeping only if the neighbour keeps it with probability at least three quarters.

@art assurance
What the sovereign supplies here is assurance: a common power that makes each person's expectation of the other safe. The model doesn't say who watches the sovereign or why he'd fine anyone. That penalty is an assumption, and Hobbes's argument for a trustworthy authority is the part the table leaves out.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Three slips" items="Hobbes took people to be wicked@wicked|The sovereign is the cause of peace@cause of peace|The model is a history of states@a history"
First, thinking Hobbes took people to be wicked; the model needs only that they're rational and unsure of each other. Second, expecting the sovereign to be the cause of peace. He changes payoffs, peace follows from the new table, and a fine of one leaves the war going on under a ruler. Third, reading the model as a history of how states began.
