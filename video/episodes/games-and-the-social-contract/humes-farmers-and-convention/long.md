@chapter Two neighbours
@title text="Hume's Farmers and Convention" sub="Games and the Social Contract" art=thumb
Two farmers would each gain from the other's help. In one season they refuse. Over many seasons they help, with no promise between them.

@art two-fields
Hume imagines two neighbours whose corn is ripe on different days. Your corn is ripe today, and mine will be ripe tomorrow. It would profit us both if I worked with you today and you helped me tomorrow.

@quote text="both of us lose our harvests for want of mutual confidence and security" cite="Hume"
But I have no kindness for you, and I know you have as little for me. So I won't take pains on your account, and you reason the same way. In his words, both of us lose our harvests for want of mutual confidence and security.

@chapter One harvest
@math heading="Each season as a game" items="help, help:  3, 3@three each;;help, refuse:  0, 5@zero and five;;refuse, help:  5, 0@five and zero;;refuse, refuse:  1, 1@one each"
Write each season as a game where both farmers choose: help or refuse. Help each other, and it's three each; help alone, and it's zero and five. Refuse a helper, and it's five and zero; both refuse, and it's one each. These are the course's standard numbers, so only their order should be read as Hume's.

@art refuse-wins
Played once, this is the prisoner's dilemma. Refusing pays five against a farmer who helps, and one against a farmer who doesn't, so it beats helping either way. Both refuse, and each collects one.

@words text="Saints with the same payoffs: the same table"
Hume's conclusion is right for one harvest, and it has nothing to do with the farmers being unkind. Turn them into saints with the same payoffs, and the table is the same.

@chapter The second farmer
@art next-season
The tempted farmer is whichever one has just been helped. Refusing to return the help pays five this season instead of three, a gain of two. But he'll want help again, and his neighbour knows it.

@art next-season-loop
Suppose the neighbour follows tit for tat, which helps first and afterwards does whatever the other did last. Then a refusal is answered by a refusal, and the gain of two is followed by seasons at one where there would have been three.

@words text="Stable when δ ≥ 2/3"
That trade is worth making only when the future counts for little. With these payoffs, tit for tat is stable against itself when the chance of another season is at least two thirds. At nine tenths, it sustains cooperation.

@chapter Twelve seasons
@math heading="Tit for tat against itself" items="every season:  3 each@all help;;12 × 3 = 36@thirty six"
Twelve seasons of tit for tat against tit for tat are all help, and each farmer collects twelve times three, which is thirty six. Nobody gave a word, and nobody is kind.

@art rowers
That's a convention: a regularity of behaviour that each member keeps because the others keep it, so that no one gains by departing from it alone. Hume's example is two men rowing a boat, who pull together without ever having promised to.

@chapter A refusal that begins the season
@math heading="One farmer refuses first" items="season one:  5 and 0@he collects five;;then the roles reverse@roles reverse;;6 × 5 + 6 × 0 = 30@each collects thirty"
Now make one farmer suspicious. He refuses in the first season, then copies his neighbour, who helps first. In the first season he collects five and the neighbour zero. Then the roles reverse, and over twelve seasons each collects thirty, six short of what the two trusting farmers made.

@art alternating
Neither can leave that pattern by following his own rule. The gap between one and thirty six is the cost of playing once. The gap between thirty six and thirty is the cost of a single refusal to go first.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Three slips" items="They fail because they are selfish@because they are selfish|A convention needs a promise@needs a promise|Twelve seasons prove they must cooperate@must cooperate"
First, thinking the farmers fail because they are selfish, when what changed is that there's a next season. Second, thinking a convention needs a promise, when what keeps it going is that each does best by keeping it. Third, thinking twelve seasons prove they must cooperate.

@words text="Always refuse is an equilibrium too"
But two farmers who always refuse are also in equilibrium. And farmers who know which harvest is the last will refuse in it, and so on backwards.
