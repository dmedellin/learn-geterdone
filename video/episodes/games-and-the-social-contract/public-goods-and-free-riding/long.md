@chapter The pool
@title text="Public Goods and Free-Riding" sub="Games and the Social Contract" art=thumb
A contribution that benefits everyone returns little to the person who gives it. So each does better by letting the others pay.

@art pool
Ten people are each given ten. Each may put it into a common pool, or keep it. The pool is tripled and divided equally among all ten, whether they contributed or not.

@math heading="One contribution" items="10 into the pool → 30@grows to thirty;;30 ÷ 10 = 3 back to the giver@three;;and 3 to each of the nine others@nine others"
So a contribution of ten grows to thirty in the pool. The contributor gets back one tenth of that, three, as does every one of the nine others.

@art split-ways
That is the key fact about a public good. The thirty does not come back to the person who paid for it. It spreads across the whole group, and the giver keeps one share in ten, the same share as each person who gave nothing at all.

@chapter Counting the others
@words text="k = how many of the other nine contribute"
As in the commons, count the others. Let k be how many of the other nine contribute. Either way, I receive a tenth of three times the pool.

@math heading="Ten people, tripled" items="C(k) = 3·(k + 1) − 10 = 3·k − 7@three k minus seven;;D(k) = 3·k@three k;;D(k) − C(k) = 7@seven"
If I contribute, the pool holds k plus one contributions and I keep none of my ten, so I get three k minus seven. If I don't, I keep my ten and get three k. The difference is seven, for every k.

@art keep-wins
Not contributing pays seven more than contributing, whatever the others do. So not contributing dominates. Each contribution is worth thirty to the group and three to the giver, who has paid ten for it.

@chapter The free-rider
@words text="Free-rider: takes the benefit, bears none of the cost"
A free-rider receives the benefit of a good without bearing a share of its cost. Here, it's a person who doesn't contribute and still takes a share of the pool.

@math heading="Nine others contribute" items="I contribute:  C(9) = 20@twenty;;I keep mine:  D(9) = 27@twenty seven;;A lone free-rider gains 7@gains seven"
If nine people contribute and I don't, I get twenty seven. Had I contributed too, I'd have had twenty. A lone free-rider gains seven.

@chapter When everyone does it
@art empty-pool
Now let everyone do as I did. Nobody contributes, the pool is empty, and each gets zero, where universal contribution would have given twenty. Each person loses twenty.

@math heading="The group" items="All contribute:  20 each, 200 in all@two hundred;;No one contributes:  0 each@zero"
The best total is all ten contributing: twenty each, two hundred in all. Against that, the only equilibrium has no one contributing, and the sum is zero. The good would benefit everyone, and that isn't enough to get anyone to pay for it.

@words text="One gains 7 · all lose 20 each"
Don't confuse the two numbers. A person may reason correctly from the free-rider's gain of seven, and the group ends up with the loss of twenty a head.

@chapter Where the dilemma stops
@math heading="Two people, tripled" items="10 → 30, half back:  15@fifteen;;contributing pays 5 more@five more"
The dilemma stops when the share is large enough. Two people with the same tripling have a share of one half, so a contribution of ten returns fifteen to its giver. Now contributing pays five more whatever the other does, and contributing dominates.

@math heading="Ten people, multiplier ten" items="10 → 100, a tenth back:  10@exactly what it cost;;neither choice dominates@neither choice"
With ten people and a multiplier of exactly ten, a contribution of ten becomes a hundred, and a tenth of that comes back: exactly what it cost. Neither choice dominates, and every number of contributors is an equilibrium.

@art ratio
So the rule compares the multiplier with the group. Below the group size the dilemma holds, and the larger the group, the smaller each person's share. Public goods are hard to supply for large groups, and easy for small ones.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Three slips" items="Everyone benefits, so everyone pays@everyone will pay|The free-rider's gain is the group's loss@the free-riders gain|Any shared good is a dilemma@any shared good"
First, thinking that if everyone benefits, everyone will pay; the benefit doesn't reach the person who decides. Second, confusing the free-rider's gain with the group's loss. Third, thinking any shared good is a dilemma, when it depends on the multiplier being below the group size.

@art giver
And the table doesn't say real people behave like it. Experiments where people give something aren't refuted by it, because a person who values giving has a different table.
