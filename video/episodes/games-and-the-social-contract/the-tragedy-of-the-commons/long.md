@chapter One pasture
@title text="The Tragedy of the Commons" sub="Games and the Social Contract" art=thumb
Each herder gains from one more animal, while the cost falls on everyone. So every herder adds, and all are left worse off.

@art pasture
Ten herders graze one pasture. Each keeps one animal and may add a second. Every animal yields twenty two, less the number of animals grazing there. So each animal added makes every animal, the herder's own included, worth one less.

@chapter Counting the others
@words text="k = others who restrain, 0 to 9"
Fix one herder, and count the others who restrain. Call that number k, from zero to nine. It's everything the herder needs to know about the others.

@math heading="Ten herders" items="C(k) = 22 − (2·n − 1 − k) = 3 + k@three plus k;;D(k) = 2·(22 − (2·n − k)) = 4 + 2·k@four plus two k;;D(k) − C(k) = 1 + k@one plus k"
If I restrain, my one animal is worth three plus k. If I add, my two animals together are worth four plus two k. The difference is one plus k, which is positive for every k.

@art adding-dominates
So adding pays more than restraining whatever the others do. Adding dominates, and the one equilibrium has no herder restraining. A lone restrainer among nine adders would get three where adding gets four, so no herder can improve alone.

@chapter The equilibrium and the best
@math heading="Totals" items="Everyone adds:  10 × 4 = 40@forty;;All restrain:  10 × 12 = 120@a hundred and twenty;;Nine restrain, one adds:  121@a hundred and twenty one"
At the equilibrium each herder gets four, forty in all. If all ten restrain, each gets twelve, and the total is a hundred and twenty. The best total of all is nine restraining and one adding: a hundred and twenty one, because the first extra animal does little harm to a lightly stocked pasture.

@art gap
Either way, the equilibrium is far below the optimum. Each step that led there was individually sensible.

@chapter Private gain, shared loss
@math heading="What one herder gains" items="Lone adder among nine:  22 vs 12@twenty two;;Gain:  10@a gain of ten;;Everyone adds:  4 vs 12@four;;Loss:  8 each@a loss of eight"
A herder who adds while the other nine restrain gets twenty two instead of twelve, a gain of ten. But if every herder does the same, each gets four, where all restraining would give twelve: a loss of eight each.

@art private-shared
The gain is private, and the loss is shared. That asymmetry is the whole of the tragedy. The herder who adds collects the whole value of the extra animal, while the grass it eats is lost a little to each of the ten.

@chapter Make the adder pay
@art fee
Put a fee of eleven on every animal added. The most a herder ever gains by adding is ten, when the other nine restrain. So eleven beats that gain at every k, and the only equilibrium is all ten restraining.

@words text="Fee 10: the border"
A fee of exactly ten is the border: a herder facing nine restrainers is then indifferent. The herders' wishes haven't changed. The cost of the extra animal has been moved onto the herder who adds it, and giving each herder his own pasture would do the same.

@chapter Not every commons
@art three-herders
Now shrink the group. The pasture and its yield stay exactly the same, and only the number of herders changes.

@math heading="Three herders" items="Equilibrium total:  96, also the best@ninety six;;All add vs all restrain:  13 more each@thirteen more"
Three herders on the same pasture don't reach a tragedy. Adding is still best for each, but the equilibrium total, ninety six, is also the best total. Each herder even gets thirteen more if all three add than if all three restrain.

@words text="5 herders: no gap · 6 herders: a gap"
At five herders the equilibrium is still the best total, and at six it isn't. It's a fact about how large the cost to others is, compared with the gain.

@words text="Herders who can talk and make rules"
Two limits. These herders can't talk, and real commons are often managed by their users with rules that change the payoffs. Elinor Ostrom's work on such arrangements is why the lesson claims no more than a conditional.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Three slips" items="The tragedy requires greed@requires greed|The equilibrium is the best they can do@the best they can do|Every shared resource is a tragedy@every shared resource"
First, thinking the tragedy requires greed: it needs only a change in who pays. Second, mistaking the equilibrium for the best they can do; stability isn't merit. Third, thinking every shared resource is a tragedy, when three herders on this pasture have no gap at all.
