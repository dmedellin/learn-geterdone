@chapter Born, not chosen
@title text="The Evolution of Cooperation" sub="Games and the Social Contract" art=thumb
In a population, a type spreads when it out-earns the other type. And what spreads need not be good for the group.

@art population
Everything so far has had players who choose. Here each individual is born a cooperator or a defector, and plays that type against one other individual drawn at random. The payoffs are fitness: the number of offspring, who inherit the type.

@words text="Share x cooperates: meet a cooperator with chance x"
A share x of the population cooperates. So a cooperator meets a cooperator with chance x, and a defector with chance one minus x.

@chapter The replicator step
@math heading="Replicator step" items="x′ = x·fC / (x·fC + (1 − x)·fD)@the old share times its payoff"
With cooperators' average payoff f C and defectors' f D, the next generation's cooperating share is the old share times its payoff, over the population's mean payoff. If cooperators earn more than the mean, their share rises.

@chapter The dilemma
@math heading="R = 3, S = 0, T = 5, P = 1" items="fC = 3·x@three x;;fD = 1 + 4·x@one plus four x;;fD − fC = 1 + x@one plus x"
Take the prisoner's dilemma payoffs. A cooperator earns three against a cooperator and zero against a defector, so on average three x. A defector earns five and one, so on average one plus four x. The defectors out-earn the cooperators by one plus x, at every share.

@math heading="From x = 1/2" items="fC = 3/2@three halves;;fD = 3@three;;mean = 9/4@nine quarters;;x′ = (3/4) / (9/4) = 1/3@a third"
Start with half the population cooperating. Cooperators earn three halves, defectors earn three, and the mean is nine quarters. The cooperators' part of the mean over the whole mean is one third, so after one generation a third of the population cooperates.

@art shrinking-share
The cooperating share falls each generation. Five generations from the same start, it ends close to zero.

@art shares-line
Here is the same run as a line. Each generation the share is lower than the one before, because at every share the defectors earn one plus x more than the cooperators do. Nothing in the step ever turns it round.

@chapter Good for the group?
@art two-populations
Compare what whole populations earn. A population of cooperators averages three each, and a population of defectors averages one. Selection still moves toward the defectors.

@words text="Selection compares types, not populations"
Selection compares types inside a population, one against the other. The mean of the whole is nothing it looks at. So a type that's bad for the group but better for the individual spreads.

@chapter The stag hunt
@math heading="Stag hunt" items="stag:  4·x@four x;;hare:  3@hare hunter earns three;;tie at x = 3/4@three quarters"
In the stag hunt, stag pays four against stag and zero against hare, and hare pays three whatever the other does. So a stag hunter earns four x and a hare hunter earns three. They tie at three quarters, the very number that was the belief threshold for hunting stag.

@math heading="Two starts" items="from 4/5:  rises to 64/79@sixty four seventy ninths;;from 1/2:  falls to 2/5@two fifths"
From four fifths, the stag hunters' share rises, to sixty four seventy ninths in one generation. From one half it falls, to two fifths.

@art two-ends
Both ends are in equilibrium, all stag and all hare, and which end is reached depends on the start. Where the population began settles a stag hunt in a way it can't settle the dilemma.

@chapter Meeting your own kind
@math heading="Half of meetings with your own kind" items="cooperator:  3/2 + (3/2)·x@three halves plus;;defector:  1 + 2·x@one plus two x;;at x = 1/2:  9/4 vs 2@nine quarters against two;;x′ = 9/17@nine seventeenths"
Now keep the dilemma's payoffs, but let half of every individual's meetings be with its own kind. A cooperator's average becomes three halves plus three halves x, and a defector's one plus two x. At one half that's nine quarters against two, and the cooperating share rises, to nine seventeenths.

@art clusters
Nothing in the payoffs has become kinder. The change is in who meets whom. That's why repeated games and the structured populations of biology can sustain cooperation that a well-mixed population can't. What the step doesn't show is how any of this is inherited, or what happens when a rare new type appears.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Three slips" items="Selection favours what is good for the group@good for the group|A rising share means the type is good@the type is good|The step is a model of choice@a model of choice"
First, thinking selection favours what is good for the group. Second, reading a rising share as a verdict that the type is good: stag hunters grow from four fifths and shrink from one half, under the same payoffs. Third, treating the step as a model of choice, when no one in the population decides anything.
