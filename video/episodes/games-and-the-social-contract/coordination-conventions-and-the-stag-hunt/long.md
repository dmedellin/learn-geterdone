@chapter Matching
@title text="Coordination, Conventions and the Stag Hunt" sub="Games and the Social Contract" art=thumb
Some games aren't about conflict at all. Both players just want to match. The trouble is there's more than one way to match, and something has to settle which.

@art driving-sides
The driving game is the plainest case. Two drivers meet on a road, and each keeps to the left or the right. Same side, they pass safely, and each gets one. Different sides, each gets zero. There are two equilibria, left with left and right with right, with identical payoffs.

@chapter Conventions
@words text="Convention" sub="everyone follows it, expects others to, and prefers to if they do"
A convention is a way of behaving, in a coordination game that keeps coming up, such that everyone follows it, everyone expects the others to, and everyone prefers to follow it provided the others do. And some other way of behaving would have served as well.

@art no-signature
Driving on the right is a convention in every country that does it, and so is driving on the left. Nothing in the table prefers either. What keeps a country on one side is each driver's expectation of the others. No one needs to have promised anything. An agreement could start a convention, but a custom with no origin in any agreement can be one too.

@chapter The stag hunt
@art stag-and-hare
The stag hunt has two matched cells whose payoffs differ. Two hunters each go for a stag or a hare. A stag takes both of them to bring down, and is worth four to each. A hare can be taken alone, and is worth three. A hunter who goes for a stag alone gets nothing.

@math heading="The stag hunt" items="Stag, Stag:  4, 4@stag with stag;;Stag, Hare:  0, 3@A lone stag hunter;;Hare, Hare:  3, 3@hare with hare"
Stag with stag pays four each, so it's payoff dominant. Hare with hare pays three each. A lone stag hunter gets zero while the partner gets three. Both matched cells are equilibria.

@art lone-hunter
So the stag is the better match, but it's the riskier one. Going for it means betting on your partner. If they go for the hare instead, you're left with nothing at all.

@math heading="Risk dominance" items="leave Stag, Stag:  lose 1,  1 · 1 = 1@from stag;;leave Hare, Hare:  lose 3,  3 · 3 = 9@from hare"
But hare guarantees three whatever the other does, and stag risks zero. One standard measure of that risk compares what each player loses by deviating. Deviate from stag, stag, and you lose one. Deviate from hare, hare, and you lose three. The larger product, nine against one, belongs to hare, hare. It's risk dominant.

@chapter The belief that stag needs
@math heading="q = chance the other hunts stag" items="hunt stag:  4 · q + 0 · (1 − q) = 4q@Hunt stag;;hunt hare:  3@hunt hare;;stag at least as good when 4q ≥ 3,  so q ≥ 3/4@three quarters"
Let q be the probability you give to the other hunting stag. Hunt stag, and you get four q, since it only works when the other joins. Hunt hare, and you get three whatever happens. So stag is at least as good exactly when four q is at least three, that is, when q is at least three quarters.

@art trust-threshold
A hunter who's only fifty fifty about the partner should go for the hare. Stag would expect two, against the hare's certain three. A pair who both give stag nine in ten can sustain it. That's why trust is hard to start, and easy to lose.

@words text="The table doesn't say which belief you'll have."
And the table doesn't say which belief the pair will have. That depends on what each has seen the other do, which is the part of the problem a convention answers and a table can't.

@chapter Battle of the sexes
@art opera-football
One more game adds disagreement. One player prefers the opera, the other the football, and both prefer going together to going apart. With the favourite worth three and the other worth two, both matches are equilibria. In the mix, each expects only six fifths, less than the two either gets at the match they like less. Even a convention now favours someone.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a convention is an agreement@The first|Thinking rational players pick the best equilibrium@The second|Hunting stag on an even chance@the third"
The first slip is thinking a convention is an agreement. The second is taking the payoff dominant equilibrium to be the one rational players choose. Rational hunters still end up at hare when each doubts the other. Rationality alone doesn't pick between equilibria. Expectation does. And the third is hunting stag on an even chance.
