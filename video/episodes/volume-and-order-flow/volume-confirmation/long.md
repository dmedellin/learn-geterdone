@chapter The question
@title text="Volume Confirmation" sub="Volume and Order Flow" art=thumb
Price pushes through a level everyone was watching, and the volume bar is huge. Is that a breakout? Maybe. The lesson's answer is that you need something more than a big candle.

@words text="A breakout needs price acceptance" sub="not only a high-volume candle"
A breakout needs price acceptance, not only a high volume candle. Buyers control an upside break when price closes above the level and keeps accepting higher prices. Sellers control a downside break when price stays below support.

@chapter Three words
@art level-break
The lesson uses three words. A break is when price crosses a previously defended level. Acceptance is when price closes and keeps trading outside the prior area. That's the one you want.

@art failed-break
And failure is when price crosses the level but comes back inside. That shows the aggressive side couldn't maintain progress. The same three words work for a downside break through support, just upside down.

@chapter The strongest evidence
@art wick-above
So which is the strongest evidence of an upside breakout? Not a wick above resistance. A wick is a visit that the close rejected. And not high volume below resistance. That's participation with no decision attached, because the level hasn't been broken.

@words text="Close above + follow-through + expanding volume"
The strongest evidence is a close above resistance, with follow through, and expanding volume. Price has to establish acceptance above the level. Volume adds participation evidence on top.

@chapter Measuring breakout volume
@art break-volume
How do you know breakout volume is expanding? Compare the break bar with normal volume. The lab takes the average of the bars before the break as the baseline.

@math heading="Break-bar ratio" items="baseline = 1,500 (18 bars)@eighteen bars;;break bar = 2,700@twenty seven hundred;;2,700 ÷ 1,500 = 1.8×@one point eight"
Say the eighteen bars before the break average fifteen hundred contracts, and the break bar trades twenty seven hundred. Twenty seven hundred divided by fifteen hundred is one point eight times. That's the same arithmetic as relative volume, with the baseline drawn from the bars before the break.

@math heading="Not these" items="✗  1,500 ÷ 2,700 = 0.56×@inverts;;✗  2.7×  is the volume in thousands@in thousands"
Fifteen hundred over twenty seven hundred inverts the comparison. And two point seven is just the bar's volume in thousands, not its ratio to the baseline.

@chapter When volume isn't enough
@words text="High volume, then back inside" sub="failed breakout"
Now a trap. Price breaks resistance on high volume, then closes back inside the range. What happened? That's a failed breakout. Buyers were active, but they couldn't maintain higher prices.

@words text="A failed high-volume break can say more"
And here's the subtle point. The break carried high volume, and the level held anyway. That's informative. A failed high volume break can be more informative than a weak break.

@art thin-acceptance
What about the opposite? Does low volume automatically invalidate a breakout? No. It lowers confirmation, and it means you need more price evidence. Price can still build acceptance above the level. The lab's weak volume case holds all five recent closes above it.

@words text="Low volume: less evidence, not none"
But don't swing to the other extreme and say volume never matters. Participation is one of the three parts of confirmation, along with closes and follow through. Without it, you need more price evidence before you call the break accepted.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Buying only because volume is high@The first|Moving the level after the result@The second|Ignoring a close back inside@the third"
Three mistakes. The first is buying only because volume is high. The second is moving the breakout level after seeing the result. And the third is ignoring a close back inside the range.

@art moved-level
That second one is easy to do without noticing. So define the level before the breakout. Volume supports a break. Acceptance validates it.

@chapter Try it
@list heading="Reading steps" items="Mark the level@mark the level|Check participation@check participation|Check acceptance@check acceptance"
The lab's three reading steps put that in order. First, mark the level. Resistance, support, or the range boundary, before the break. Second, check participation, comparing breakout volume with normal volume. Third, check acceptance. Require closes, follow through, or a successful retest outside the level.
