@chapter The question
@title text="Volume Fundamentals" sub="Volume and Order Flow" art=thumb
Under almost every price chart sits a row of bars. That's volume. It's one of the most quoted numbers in trading, and one of the most misread. So let's pin down what it counts, and what it simply can't say.

@art volume-bars
Volume is the number of shares or contracts traded during a period. That's all. A tall bar means a lot changed hands. A short bar means little did. In one word, volume measures participation.

@words text="Volume shows activity, not direction"
Here's the idea the whole lesson rests on. Volume shows activity, not direction. To see why, look at a single trade.

@chapter One trade, two sides
@art one-trade-two-sides
A completed trade always has a buyer and a seller. One contract changes hands, so it was bought by one party and sold by another, at the same moment. Volume counts that contract once.

@math heading="A bar prints 5,000 contracts" items="bought = 5,000@five thousand were bought;;sold = 5,000@five thousand were sold;;✗  2,500 bought + 2,500 sold@two and a half thousand"
So if a bar prints five thousand contracts, how many were bought and how many sold? Five thousand were bought, and five thousand were sold. Not two and a half thousand each. Every one of the five thousand had both sides.

@words text="Volume never means net buying"
And the candle's colour doesn't change that. Colour only tells you where the close sat against the open. So volume of five thousand means five thousand changed hands. It never means net buying.

@chapter Direction comes from price
@art rising-expanding
If volume carries no direction, where does direction come from? From price. Control is inferred from which side accepts worse prices, and whether price makes progress. Price moving up shows buyers successfully pushing the auction. Price moving down shows sellers doing it.

@words text="Price up + volume expanding" sub="buyers accepting higher prices, with more participation"
So when price rises on expanding volume, the simplest reading is this. Buyers are accepting higher prices, with stronger participation. The sellers in those trades are supplying, not controlling.

@words text="Not a forecast" sub="continuation is still not guaranteed"
But notice what that reading doesn't say. A tall bar is a record of what traded, not a forecast. Large moves often come with expanding volume, but the count doesn't predict the next bar. Continuation is still not guaranteed.

@chapter Heavy volume, no progress
@art flat-heavy
Now the interesting case. Price is flat, but volume is very high. That's not no trading. It's the opposite. The puzzle is why so much trade moved price so little.

@words text="Absorption or two-way balance"
One answer is absorption, or two way balance. Resting liquidity is taking the other side of the aggressive orders. Heavy volume without progress says nothing about which way, or whether, price leaves. It's not a guaranteed breakout.

@chapter Context
@art baseline
Is a given bar high or low? That depends. Volume matters relative to the instrument, the timeframe, and the recent baseline. A bar that's huge for one market can be ordinary for another.

@list heading="Read it in order" items="Start with price@start with price|Compare volume@compare volume|State the behaviour@state what buyers"
So the lesson gives you three reading steps. First, start with price. Is it rising, falling, or staying in a range? Then compare volume. Is participation expanding, normal, or contracting? Finally, state what buyers and sellers are doing, without predicting the next candle.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Calling every high-volume bar bullish@The first|Comparing raw volume across instruments@The second|Ignoring the timeframe and session@And the third"
The first mistake is calling every high volume bar bullish. Volume alone is neither bullish nor bearish. The second is comparing raw volume across unrelated instruments. And the third is ignoring the timeframe and the session.

@art two-markets
The fix for all three is the same. Compare volume with a recent baseline for that market, and require price progress before you call one side in control.
