@chapter The question
@title text="Price and Volume Relationships" sub="Volume and Order Flow" art=thumb
You've got two things on the chart, price and volume. Each one moves. So how do you read them together without fooling yourself? The lesson's answer fits in one line.

@words text="Direction comes from price" sub="conviction comes from participation"
Direction comes from price. Conviction comes from participation. Rising price means buyers are making progress. Falling price means sellers are. Volume tells you whether participation is expanding or contracting.

@words text="Which variable sets direction?" sub="price movement"
So which variable establishes direction? Price movement. Not the colour of the most recent candle, which is one bar's close against its open. Not volume, because volume has no sign. Every contract was both bought and sold. Direction is what price did while it traded.

@chapter Up on expanding volume
@art up-expanding
Start with the clearest case. Price is going up, and volume is going up. Buyers keep accepting higher prices while participation expands. That's directional progress with more and more people taking part. It's the strongest of the four readings, because price and participation agree.

@words text="Confirms activity" sub="not guaranteed continuation"
Even here, keep the claim small. Strong participation confirms what is happening now. Nothing in the count guarantees the next bar.

@chapter Down on expanding volume
@art down-expanding
Flip it. Price falls while volume expands. Sellers keep accepting lower prices, and participation grows as they do. That's active selling pressure and downward progress.

@words text="Not buyers, not balance"
It isn't buyer control. The buyers in those trades are the passive side, not the side making progress. And it isn't balance either. Balance means price ends near where it began. A falling price on rising participation is a decided move, not a contest.

@chapter A move on fading volume
@art up-contracting
Now the subtle one. Price rises, but volume contracts. The best description is upward progress with weakening participation. Buyers are still moving price higher. There are just fewer participants supporting the move.

@list heading="Fading volume is not…" items="Strong confirmed demand@strong confirmed demand|A guaranteed reversal@a guaranteed reversal"
So it's not strong confirmed demand. Confirmation is expanding participation, and this is the weaker case. But it's not a guaranteed reversal either. Thin participation lowers the evidence for the trend. It doesn't reverse it, and trends on falling volume can persist for a long time.

@chapter Measuring the trend in volume
@art two-halves
How does the lab decide whether participation is expanding? It compares two averages. The average volume of the last fourteen bars, and the average of the first fourteen.

@math heading="Volume trend" items="recent average = 1,250@twelve hundred and fifty;;earlier average = 1,000@one thousand;;1,250 ÷ 1,000 = 1.25@divided by;;trend = +25%@up twenty five percent"
Say the recent average is twelve hundred and fifty contracts, and the earlier one was one thousand. Divide the recent by the earlier: twelve fifty divided by one thousand is one point two five. So participation expanded, up twenty five percent.

@math heading="Two easy slips" items="✗  1,000 ÷ 1,250 = 0.8 → −20%@backwards;;✗  difference 250 → +250%@two hundred and fifty contracts"
Two slips to avoid. Dividing one thousand by twelve fifty gives point eight, minus twenty percent, which measures the change backwards. And the difference is two hundred and fifty contracts, not two hundred and fifty percent.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Green candles = buyer volume@The first|Low-volume trends must reverse@The second|Reading one bar, not a sequence@The third"
The first mistake is equating green candles with buyer initiated volume. One candle's colour is just its close against its open. The second is assuming low volume trends must immediately reverse. And the third is using one bar instead of a sequence.

@art one-bar-vs-sequence
Direction is read from the sequence of closes, not from a single bar. One red candle in a rising run doesn't make sellers the winners. So describe price first, and volume second, over a run of bars.

@art mixed
And sometimes the run tells you nothing clear. Price chops up and down, and volume jumps around with it. When the evidence is mixed, treat it as mixed, not as a forced signal. Saying there's no clear reading yet is a perfectly good reading.
