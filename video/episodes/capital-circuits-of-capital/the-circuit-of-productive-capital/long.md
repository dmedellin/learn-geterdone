@chapter Same capital, new start
@title text="The Circuit of Productive Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 2" art=thumb
Same spinner, same four hundred and twenty two pounds, same five hundred pounds of yarn. This time, begin the story in the mill.

@words text="P … C′ – M′ – C … P" sub="produce · sell · buy back · produce"
Now the sale and the purchase back sit in the middle. Their only job is to turn this week's yarn into next week's cotton and labour-power.

@art mill-loop
Money becomes a form the capital takes for a moment and drops. In the money circuit, money is where capital starts and ends. Here, Marx says, the capital is just as anxious to take the money form as it is to throw it off.

@quote text="The rotation of productive capital has the general formula P...C'-M'-C...P." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 2"
It stands for the regular renewal of production: production as a process of reproduction.

@chapter Simple reproduction
@math heading="The yarn sells for £500" items="£500 = £422 (M) + £78 (m)@five hundred;;£422 → cotton, coal, wear, wages@goes back;;£78 → the household@leaves the circuit"
Start with simple reproduction. The yarn sells for five hundred pounds. Four hundred and twenty two goes back into cotton, coal, wear and wages, and next week starts as this one did. The other seventy eight leaves the circuit altogether, spent on the capitalist's household.

@art revenue-leaves
Of that money Marx says it is not advanced, but spent. So the capital at the end is four hundred and twenty two, exactly the capital at the start.

@chapter The look of the thing
@words text="No overproduction?" sub="every sale is also a purchase"
That equality makes the circuit easy to misread. It looks like producing in order to consume. Say and James Mill argued that every sale is also a purchase, so there can never be more goods in general than buyers. Marx doesn't name them here. He replies that replacing commodities to produce surplus value is a different matter from swapping products through money.

@art warehouse-glut
And the circuit can hide a glut. Once the yarn is sold to a merchant, the spinner carries on, whether or not anyone has used the yarn. Much of the product can lie unsold in dealers' warehouses. Then new goods arrive, old bills fall due, and dealers sell at any price. Then a crisis comes.

@chapter Accumulation
@art bigger-mill
Now let part of the surplus stay in. Marx's own figure: the capital may continue as four hundred and eighty pounds, or five hundred.

@math heading="Lay out £58 of the £78" items="£422 + £58 = £480@plus fifty eight;;P … P′@P prime"
Add fifty eight of the seventy eight, and next week's productive capital is four hundred and twenty two plus fifty eight, four hundred and eighty pounds. The circuit becomes P to P prime, reproduction on an enlarged scale.

@chapter The hoard
@words text="A spindle can't be bought by halves"
But you can't invest any amount you like. Marx notes that new installations generally assume one pound per spindle, so the surplus must reach a considerable figure first. Until then, the cycle repeats.

@art hoard-safe
The lesson takes an illustrative minimum, two hundred pounds for the smallest useful addition, and has the spinner save all seventy eight each week.

@math heading="Saving £78 a week, minimum £200" items="week 1:  £78 < £200@after one week;;week 2:  £156 < £200@after two;;week 3:  £234 ≥ £200@after three;;week 4 starts at £422 + £234 = £656@six hundred and fifty six"
After one week, seventy eight pounds sits in the safe. After two, one hundred and fifty six. After three, two hundred and thirty four, which clears two hundred, so all of it goes in. The fourth week starts at six hundred and fifty six pounds.

@words text="Latent capital" sub="it adds nothing while it waits"
Until then, the money is latent capital, Marx's word. It produces nothing. It grows only because more money is thrown into the same safe.

@art reserve-fund
A hoard has a second use. If yarn is slow to sell, or cotton has risen, the capitalist can draw on it to keep the mill running. Then it's a reserve fund.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Just the money circuit from another point@The first|A hoard counts as capital at work@The second|A sale to a merchant means it's consumed@And the third"
The first slip is treating this as the money circuit started somewhere else. The movements match, but this form hides the increase. The second is counting a hoard as capital at work. And the third is thinking a sale to a merchant means the product has been used, when sales between capitals can run far ahead of final use.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Lay out £58 of £78" sub="start: £422"
Your turn. The spinner lays out fifty eight pounds of his seventy eight as capital, and spends the rest. What's next week's productive capital? Then try saving everything, with nothing under two hundred pounds investable, and find the week it first goes in.
