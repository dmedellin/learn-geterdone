@chapter Two kinds of time
@title text="The Time of Circulation" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 5" art=thumb
A capital spends part of its life producing, and part of it being sold and bought back. Marx's claim in this chapter is that the second part produces nothing at all.

@quote text="The time of circulation and time of production mutually exclude one another. During its time of circulation, capital does not perform the functions of productive capital and therefore produces neither commodities nor surplus-value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 5"
That's the whole chapter in two sentences.

@art two-bars
The chapter gives no figures, so the lesson supplies some. A spinner's yarn takes six weeks from raw cotton to finished stock. Selling it and buying back cotton and labour-power takes another six.

@math heading="Turnover = production + circulation" items="turnover:  6 + 6 = 12 weeks@twelve weeks;;circulating:  6/12 = 1/2@half;;turnovers a year:  52/12 = 13/3@fifty two"
The capital's turnover, the time until the value advanced is back in its starting form, is six plus six, twelve weeks. For half of those weeks, the capital isn't in production at all. And a fifty two week year divided by twelve gives thirteen thirds turnovers.

@chapter What counts as production time
@art mill-at-night
Production time is longer than labour time. The mill stands at night with its machines still in it. Cotton waits in store so spinning needn't depend on each day's delivery.

@art cellar-wine
Grain lies in the ground, and wine in the cellar, with no one working on them. All of that is time in the sphere of production. Only the part where labour is applied creates value, which is why capitalists try to close the gap. Here, production time is taken whole.

@chapter Circulation as a limit
@words text="Divided capital, smaller productive part"
If the whole capital moves in one lump, production simply stops during circulation. If it's divided, production can continue, but the longer the other parts spend on the market, the smaller the part in production must be.

@quote text="And this limitation is proportional to the duration of the time of circulation." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 5"
Circulation time limits production time and the making of surplus value. And this limitation is proportional to the duration of the time of circulation.

@art shrinking-share
Shorten circulation to two weeks, and the turnover is eight weeks, with only a quarter of it circulating.

@chapter Selling matters more
@art sale-and-purchase
Of the two parts of circulation, selling is usually the longer, and always the more important. Buying cotton just converts value from one form to another. Selling the yarn does that, and also realises the surplus value. The sale is the moment the seventy eight pounds becomes money.

@art distant-ship
The sale also has a limit in the commodity's body. Things that spoil must be consumed soon, so they have local markets, like the products of big breweries and dairies near towns. A product that keeps can go far.

@math heading="A distant market" items="turnover:  6 + 20 = 26 weeks@twenty six;;circulating:  20/26 = 10/13@ten thirteenths;;turnovers a year:  52/26 = 2@twice"
Ship it to a market twenty weeks away, and the turnover is six plus twenty, twenty six weeks. Ten thirteenths of it is circulation. And the capital turns over just twice a year.

@chapter Why it looks productive
@words text="A negative effect taken for a positive one"
So why does waiting look productive? Marx says political economy takes this negative effect for a positive one, because its results look positive. When profits are equalised, slow circulating capitals do end up charging more. Volume three explains that as a share of surplus value made elsewhere.

@words text="Is time itself productive?" sub="Böhm-Bawerk, 1880s"
The strongest objection came later. Eugen von Bohm-Bawerk argued that roundabout methods yield more, and that interest is the premium on present goods over future ones. He wrote after Marx's death, so the chapter answers the view, not the man: in circulation, capital produces neither commodities nor surplus value.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Tied up means at work@The first|The wait earns the profit@The second|Production time equals labour time@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a capital produces value for as long as it's tied up. The second is crediting a long circulation time with the profit it seems to bring. And the third is equating production time with labour time, when it includes nights, stocks and natural processes too.
