@chapter The question
@title text="The Circuit of Money Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 1" art=thumb
Volume one followed capital into the factory, and took the rest for granted. It assumed the capitalist could find cotton and workers for sale, and that the yarn would sell. Volume two takes those assumptions one at a time, starting with the shape of the whole movement.

@words text="M – C … P … C′ – M′" sub="money · commodities · production · more commodities · more money"
Here it is. Money buys commodities. Production happens. More commodities come out, and they're sold for more money. Read it as money, commodities, production, more commodities, more money.

@quote text="Hence the formula for the circulation process of money capital is: M-C...P...C'-M', the dots indicating the points where the process of circulation was interrupted, and C' and M' designating C and M increased by surplus value." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 1"
The dots matter. They mark where circulation stops and production takes over. And the primes mean the same things, increased by surplus value.

@art three-stages
One word on translation. The old English edition calls this the circulation of money capital. Later translations say circuit, and that's the word these lessons use.

@chapter Stage one: buying
@art two-purchases
Marx's spinner starts with four hundred and twenty two pounds. Three hundred and seventy two buys means of production: cotton, coal, wear on the spindles. Fifty buys labour-power.

@words text="Pm must absorb all the labour bought" sub="surplus labour included"
That split isn't arbitrary. The cotton and the rest must be enough to soak up all the labour bought, surplus labour included. Too little, and some labour sits idle. Too much, and some cotton goes unspun.

@words text="The class relation is set up in M – L"
And buying labour-power is different in kind from buying cotton. Money can buy it only where people have nothing else to sell. After the Russian serfs were freed in eighteen sixty one, landowners with money still couldn't find hands, because peasants who held village land weren't yet free wage workers.

@chapter Stage two: production
@art mill-adds-value
In the middle stage, circulation stops. The spinners carry the three hundred and seventy two pounds of means of production into the yarn, and add one hundred and twenty eight pounds of new value.

@math heading="One week in the mill" items="£372 + £128 = £500@five hundred;;£128 = £50 (v) + £78 (s)@fifty replaces;;78 / 50 = 156 / 100@one hundred and fifty six"
So ten thousand pounds weight of yarn are worth five hundred pounds. Of the new value, fifty replaces the wages and seventy eight is surplus value. Seventy eight over fifty is one hundred and fifty six per cent, Marx's rate of exploitation. All of the increase happens here, and nothing has been sold.

@chapter Stage three: selling
@quote text="has not experienced any change of magnitude, but only of form" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 1"
So what makes the final sale a function of capital? Nothing that happens inside it. The yarn's value has not experienced any change of magnitude, but only of form. What makes it commodity capital is where it came from.

@chapter A partial sale
@art partial-sale
Now sell less than all of it, at its value. The money back is the product's value times the share sold.

@math heading="Selling part of the yarn" items="7,440 lb → £372;  M′ − M = −£50@seven thousand four hundred and forty;;8,440 lb → £422;  M′ − M = £0@eight thousand four hundred and forty;;10,000 lb → £500;  M′ − M = £78@all ten thousand"
Sell seven thousand four hundred and forty pounds weight, and he gets three hundred and seventy two back, only his constant capital. Sell eight thousand four hundred and forty, and he recovers four hundred and twenty two, his whole capital, with nothing over. Only all ten thousand bring the seventy eight pounds home.

@art warehouse-stock
A negative figure here isn't lost value. Sell nothing, and the money back is zero, but the yarn is still worth five hundred pounds. It's capital still sitting in the warehouse as yarn.

@chapter Money hides the source
@art mixed-coins
Once sold, the result looks different from how it was made. In five hundred pounds of coins, the capital and the surplus are just four hundred and twenty two and seventy eight side by side. Nothing in the coins says which is which.

@quote text="Buying in order to sell dearer is its slogan." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 1"
That's why Marx calls this circuit one-sided and typical. It starts and ends in money, so it shows the motive most plainly, the making of money. Buying in order to sell dearer is its slogan.

@words text="Gain made by selling dear?" sub="the monetary system's reading"
It also invites a misreading. The early mercantilists' monetary system read the gain as made in circulation, by selling above value. Marx's answer is the yarn: it was worth five hundred pounds before it left the mill.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The surplus is made in the sale@The first|Three kinds of business@The second|M – L as only a money deal@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the surplus is made at the end, in the sale. The second is treating money, productive and commodity capital as three kinds of business, when they're forms one capital takes in turn. And the third is reading the purchase of labour-power as only a money deal, when it rests on workers being separated from the means of production.
