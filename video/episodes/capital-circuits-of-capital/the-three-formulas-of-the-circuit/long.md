@chapter One lump or three parts
@title text="The Three Formulas of the Circuit" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 4" art=thumb
So far the spinner's four hundred and twenty two pounds moved in one piece. All money, then all at work, then all yarn.

@art one-lump
Take that literally, and the mill stands idle while the yarn is on the market and while the money buys cotton.

@art artisan-waits
Marx's example of a business that really works that way is a Chinese artisan who works only for private customers, and waits between orders for the next one.

@chapter Three circuits at once
@words text="M…M′ · P…P · C′…C′"
A factory doesn't wait. While this week's yarn is being sold, last week's money is buying cotton, and the cotton bought before that is being spun. The capital has been divided, and the division is what makes the work continuous.

@quote text="One part of capital, continually changing, continually reproduced, exists as a commodity-capital which is converted into money; another as money-capital converted into productive capital; and a third as productive capital converted into commodity-capital." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 4"
So a continuous capital runs all three circuits at once, through different parts of itself.

@art staggered-parts
Each part goes through the stages one after another. But because the parts are staggered, the capital as a whole is in every stage at the same time. Succession becomes simultaneity.

@chapter How big is each part?
@words text="share in a form = its weeks / all weeks"
How much of the capital is in each form? If production runs evenly, each part's share of the capital is the share of the circuit's time spent in that form.

@math heading="Even: 4 + 4 + 4 weeks" items="circuit = 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 weeks@twelve week;;money:  4/12 = 1/3@buying;;productive:  4/12 = 1/3@producing;;commodity:  4/12 = 1/3@selling"
Say four weeks buying, four producing and four selling. That's a twelve week circuit. So four twelfths, a third, is in each form: a third buying, a third producing, a third selling.

@art slow-sale
Now let selling slow down to eight weeks.

@math heading="Slow sale: 4 + 4 + 8 weeks" items="circuit = 16 weeks@sixteen;;commodity:  8/16 = 1/2@half;;productive:  4/16 = 1/4@a quarter"
The circuit becomes sixteen weeks. Eight sixteenths, half the capital, is now unsold product, and only a quarter is at work. To keep the same production going, the business would need a larger capital.

@chapter When one part stops
@art blocked-batches
The price of continuity is that every interruption spreads. If one batch of yarn can't be sold, the money to replace its cotton never comes. The batches behind it find their next step blocked by the ones ahead. Stop the succession, and the whole capital stops.

@chapter Value in motion
@art value-in-motion
Marx draws a large conclusion. Capital, as value creating more value, can be understood only as a thing in motion, not as a thing at rest. It's value staying itself through money, means of production and yarn, and comparing its present size with its starting size.

@words text="Value: just a ratio between commodities now?" sub="Samuel Bailey, 1825"
That's his answer to Samuel Bailey, Ricardo's sharpest critic, who argued that value is only a relation between commodities exchanged at the same time. Marx's reply: a capital makes exactly that comparison across time on every circuit. Four hundred and twenty two laid out in March is measured against five hundred back in June.

@words text="He sells dearer" sub="because his commodity contains a greater value"
And one more line belongs with the first lesson. The capitalist does sell dearer than he bought, Marx concedes. Not because he gets more than the value, but because his commodity contains a greater value than its elements did.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A capital is in one form at a time@The first|Three circuits are three kinds of capitalist@The second|The division is the capitalist's choice@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a capital is in one form at a time. The second is taking the three formulas for a banker, a manufacturer and a merchant, when all three belong to one capital. And the third is thinking the division is a choice. If selling takes eight weeks, half in commodity form is forced on the business.

@words text="2 weeks buying · 6 producing · 2 selling"
Your turn. A business spends two weeks buying, six producing and two selling, and runs evenly without stopping. What share of its capital is at work at any moment? And what share is in money or commodity form together?
