@chapter Start from the product
@title text="The Circuit of Commodity Capital" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 3" art=thumb
The third circuit starts where the second one ended, with the finished yarn. And that turns out to show something the other two hide: the whole product of a capital, and how little of it is anyone's income.

@words text="C′ – M′ – C … P … C′"
The yarn isn't the capital that was advanced. It's that capital plus what production added. Marx says this circuit opens with augmented capital value in the form of commodities.

@art swollen-product
So from the first step it carries two movements at once, the capital value's and the surplus value's, and they part company at the sale. That's why the prime is there at both ends. The money circuit restarts as plain M. This one restarts as a product with surplus value already in it.

@chapter Splitting the yarn
@art yarn-in-three
Marx does the sum this circuit invites, and he does it in pounds of yarn. Ten thousand pounds weight, at a shilling a pound, are worth five hundred pounds.

@math heading="10,000 lb at 1s a pound" items="£372 + £50 + £78 = £500@three hundred and seventy two;;c:  10,000 · 372/500 = 7,440 lb@carry the constant;;v:  10,000 · 50/500 = 1,000 lb@one thousand;;s:  10,000 · 78/500 = 1,560 lb@fifteen hundred and sixty"
That's three hundred and seventy two of constant capital, fifty of variable and seventy eight of surplus value. So seven thousand four hundred and forty pounds of yarn carry the constant capital, one thousand carry the wages, and fifteen hundred and sixty carry the surplus value.

@chapter Every lot divides the same
@art three-lots
Sell it in lots, and each lot divides the same way. Marx's first lot is the seven thousand four hundred and forty pounds whose value equals the constant capital.

@math heading="Lot 1: 7,440 lb" items="c:  5,535.36 lb@five thousand five hundred;;v:  744 lb@seven hundred and forty four;;s:  1,160.64 lb@eleven hundred and sixty"
Of those, five thousand five hundred and thirty five point three six pounds replace the constant capital used up in them, seven hundred and forty four pay their wages, and eleven hundred and sixty point six four are surplus product.

@math heading="Adding the three lots" items="c:  5,535.36 + 744 + 1,160.64 = 7,440 lb@add the constant;;s:  1,160.64 + 156 + 243.36 = 1,560 lb@the surplus"
The other two lots divide the same way. Add the constant parts of all three and you get seven thousand four hundred and forty again. Add the surplus parts and you get fifteen hundred and sixty. Three sales give the same result as one.

@art weaver-buys
None of this is the buyer's business. A weaver cares about the price and how much yarn his mill needs. If he analyses the seller's costs, Marx says, it's only to prove the yarn could be sold cheaper and still leave a fair profit.

@chapter A society's product
@art many-capitals
Here's why this circuit matters. The spinner's money needs coal, cotton and machinery on the market when it comes back, and those are other capitals' products. His workers need bread and clothing, which are others' products again.

@quote text="It is a movement of the aggregate capital of the capitalist class, in which every individual capital appears only as a part whose movements intermingle with those of the others and are conditioned on them." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 3"
Follow every capital's product at once, and you're following a whole society's annual product.

@chapter Smith's objection
@words text="Price = wages + profit + rent?" sub="Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations, 1776"
Adam Smith held that every price finally resolves into wages, profit and rent. The cotton was grown with paid labour, the spindle made with paid labour. Trace back far enough and you find only incomes.

@math heading="This year's £500 of yarn" items="£372 must buy back means of production@must buy back;;£50 + £78 = £128 new value@only fifty"
Marx's reply fills a later chapter. Each of those inputs divides the same way, so the regress never ends in pure income. In this year's five hundred pounds, three hundred and seventy two must buy back means of production. Only fifty plus seventy eight, one hundred and twenty eight, is new value, anyone's income.

@art harvest-table
Marx credits one economist with choosing this circuit: Quesnay, whose Tableau Economique of seventeen fifty eight follows a year's harvest between farmers, landlords and artisans. Marx calls the choice great discrimination on his part.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The whole product is revenue@The first|Only a divisible product splits@The second|The buyer cares about c, v and s@And the third"
The first slip is treating the whole product as revenue to be consumed. The second is thinking only a divisible product can be split. A house can't be sold in pieces, but its value still divides in the same proportions. And the third is reading the seller's composition into the buyer's purchase.
