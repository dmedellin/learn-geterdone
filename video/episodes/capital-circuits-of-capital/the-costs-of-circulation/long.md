@chapter Every price hides costs
@title text="The Costs of Circulation" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 6" art=thumb
Think of the price of almost anything. Some of it paid for making the thing. Some paid for moving it to the shop. And some paid for advertising, sales staff, accounts and the shop's own margin.

@words text="Does it do something to the product?"
Ordinary accounting treats all of those as costs the price must cover. Marx draws a line through them. On one side, costs that do something to the product: move it, or keep it from spoiling. On the other, costs that only change who holds the value, and in what form.

@quote text="The general law is that all expenses of circulation, which arise only from changes of form, do not add any value to the commodities." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 6"
That's his general law. Those expenses, he says, must be made up out of the surplus product.

@chapter Buying and selling
@art haggling
Start with buying and selling. When Greek meets Greek, then comes the tug of war. Each side tries to get the better of the other, the haggling takes time, and none of it adds value, any more than a lawsuit increases the value of the thing fought over.

@art burning-coal
Marx's image is combustion. Breaking up the coal is necessary for it to burn, and produces no heat.

@words text="10 hours worked · paid for 8"
Hand the job to a specialist, and nothing changes. A hired salesman is paid the value of eight hours, and works ten. His two unpaid hours produce no surplus value, because his ten hours produce no value at all. They just make the employer's selling cost smaller.

@art ledger-desk
Book-keeping is the same kind of cost. So is money itself: the gold kept in circulation, and replaced as it wears, is social labour tied up in a machine for exchanging.

@chapter Storage and transport
@art good-and-bad-stock
Storage is in between. Trade needs some stock to flow at all: cotton in the mill's store, bread in the shop. Keeping a necessary stock preserves the product, and its cost enters the value. But a stock that's there because it won't sell is a loss.

@art carter-route
Transport is the exception. A product's use is where it's consumed. Yarn in Manchester isn't yet yarn on a loom in Bombay, so carriage is production continued after the product leaves the mill.

@quote text="The productive capital invested in this industry adds value to the transported products, partly by transferring value from the means of transportation, partly by adding value through the labor-power used in transportation." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 6"
It passes on the wear of the carts or trains, and adds the labour of the people moving the goods.

@chapter The line in figures
@math heading="£400 laid out, goods worth £500" items="s:  £500 − £400 = £100@a hundred;;book-keeper, pure:  £100 − £10 = £90@ninety"
The lab puts the line in figures. A capital of four hundred pounds makes goods worth five hundred, a surplus value of a hundred. Pay a book-keeper ten pounds, a pure cost, and ninety is left.

@math heading="Carriage and storage" items="carter, adds value:  £510 − £410 = £100@still a hundred;;stockpile:  £100 − £5 = £95@ninety five"
Pay a carter ten instead, and the goods are worth five hundred and ten against four hundred and ten laid out, so the surplus is still a hundred. Store a normal stock for five, plus five more because it won't sell, and ninety five is left.

@art surplus-shrinks
The two ten pound payments look the same in the cash book. One takes ten pounds from the surplus value. The other comes back in the value of goods now where they'll be used.

@chapter An argued line
@words text="Is every paid service production?"
This line is among the most argued over in the book. Modern national accounts count a shop's margin and a bank's fees as value added, and from Say onward, economists have held that any service people pay for is production. The lesson computes the line as the chapter draws it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Every necessary cost adds value@The first|A specialist makes it productive@The second|All storage is alike@And the third"
The first slip is thinking everything a business must pay for adds value. Necessary isn't the same as productive of value. The second is thinking a cost becomes productive when a specialist takes it over. And the third is treating all storage alike, when only a stock that trade needs adds value.
