@chapter The question
@title text="The Extent of the Market" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 3" art=thumb
If exchange is what makes people divide their work, then there's a catch. The division can only go as far as exchange can. Chapter three is about that limit.

@words text="Limited by the extent of the market"
Smith's first sentence is the thesis. The division of labour must always be limited by the extent of the power of exchanging, or, in other words, by the extent of the market.

@chapter A small market
@art highland-houses
When the market is very small, nobody has a reason to give himself entirely to one job, because he can't trade away his surplus. In the scattered houses of the Highlands, every farmer must be butcher, baker and brewer for his own family. And a porter can find work only in a great town. A village is far too narrow for him.

@chapter The nailer
@math heading="One nailer, one Highland district" items="1000 · 300 = 300000 nails a year@working days;;1000 / 300000 = 1/300 of his year@one three hundredth"
Now Smith's figure. A nailer making a thousand nails a day, for three hundred working days, makes three hundred thousand nails a year. But in a remote Highland district it would be impossible to sell one thousand, one day's work in the year. Even at that bound, the place takes one three hundredth of his year.

@art nail-heap
So a nailer who tried to live there would be idle on all but one day in three hundred. The place keeps no whole nailer at all. Someone still makes the nails, but in a few days of the year, alongside other work.

@chapter Let the market grow
@math heading="300,000 nails a year each" items="village:  30000 → 1/10 of a nailer@a village;;market town:  150000 → 1/2@a market town;;port:  900000 → 3 nailers@a port;;city:  3000000 → 10 nailers@a city"
Let the demand grow, with figures the lesson wrote. A village takes thirty thousand nails, a tenth of a nailer. A market town, a hundred and fifty thousand, half a nailer. A port, nine hundred thousand, three nailers. And a city, three million, ten nailers.

@art market-ladder
Only in the port does the trade first stand on its own. Below that, the man who can't live by nails alone makes everything iron. Smith says the country smith's trade is even more various than the carpenter's.

@art river-village
Now add a river. An inland village that reaches only its neighbours takes twenty thousand nails, a fifteenth of a nailer's year. A village on a navigable river takes six hundred thousand, and keeps two nailers. Nothing about the villagers changed, only how far their trade can go.

@chapter Wagon against ship
@art wagon-and-ship
What widens a market most is water. A broad wheeled wagon, with two men and eight horses, takes about six weeks to carry near four tons between London and Edinburgh and back. In about the same time, a ship with six or eight men sailing between London and Leith often carries two hundred tons.

@math heading="London and Edinburgh" items="200 / 4 = 50 wagon-loads@fifty wagon;;50 wagons:  100 men · 400 horses@a hundred men;;land: 2 tons a man · sea: 25 to 33 a man@two tons a man"
Two hundred tons is fifty wagon loads, and fifty wagons mean a hundred men and four hundred horses. By land, that's two tons a man. By sea, between twenty five and thirty three. So a man on the ship does roughly the work of twelve to seventeen on the road.

@chapter Coasts and rivers
@art coast-and-inland
Hence Smith's geography. Industry first divides and improves on the sea coast and along navigable rivers, and reaches inland much later. He points to the Mediterranean, to Egypt and the Nile, to Bengal and eastern China. That sweep is the chapter's weakest part. It rests on what a European knew in 1776, and ignores the overland caravan trade.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the trade needs special goods@The first|Reading the limit as a fixed ceiling@The second|Taking the geography for a proof@The third"
The first slip is thinking a trade needs a big town because its goods are special. The nailer is the same everywhere; demand differs. The second is reading the limit as a fixed ceiling. In 1928 Allyn Young added that the division also widens the market. The third is taking the geography for a proof.
