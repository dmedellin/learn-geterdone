@chapter The question
@title text="The Annual Labour of Every Nation" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Introduction" art=thumb
What makes a nation rich? You might say gold, or land, or a full treasury. Adam Smith opens The Wealth of Nations with a different answer, and it's really a sum, written before he gives a single figure.

@quote text="the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniencies of life which it annually consumes" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Introduction and Plan of the Work"
The annual labour of every nation, he says, is the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniencies of life which it annually consumes.

@chapter A fund that refills
@art yearly-fund
Take it a phrase at a time. A fund is a store you draw on, and Smith means one that's refilled every year. Nothing here is a treasure laid up once. Necessaries and conveniencies is his plain phrase for goods: what you need to live, and what makes living easier.

@art trade-abroad
And the supply can come from the nation's own labour, or from what that labour buys from other nations. So already, foreign trade is a way of turning a nation's own work into the things it wants. Book Four argues that at length.

@chapter A quotient
@art share-the-produce
The next sentence turns the fund into a division. A nation is better or worse supplied according to the proportion its produce bears to the number of people who consume it. A rich country is one where that quotient is large, whatever the country's size.

@math heading="Produce per head" items="per head = produce / population@produce per head;;produce = workers × output per worker@workers times;;per head = share at work × output per worker@share at work"
So produce per head is the produce divided by the population. Write the produce as workers times the output of each worker, divide by everyone, and produce per head becomes the share at work times the output per worker.

@chapter Two circumstances
@art two-factors
Smith names what moves that quotient. First, the skill, dexterity and judgment with which labour is applied. Second, the proportion of people employed in useful labour. Mind the order: his first circumstance, skill, is the second factor in the formula, the output of each worker.

@words text="Skill seems to matter more" sub="the share at work comes second"
And he says the supply seems to depend more on the first circumstance than on the second. Among hunters and fishers, nearly everyone able to work does useful labour, yet they're poor. In civilized nations many don't labour at all, and still all are often abundantly supplied.

@chapter Putting numbers on it
@math heading="Contrast: 100 people each" items="hunters:  60 · 2 = 120;;120 / 100 = 6/5 a head@six fifths;;civilized:  40 · 12 = 480@forty at work;;480 / 100 = 24/5 a head@twenty four fifths;;(24/5) / (6/5) = 4@four times"
Smith gives no numbers here, so the lesson writes its own. Each nation has a hundred people. The hunters have sixty at work, each making two units: a hundred and twenty, or six fifths a head. The civilized nation has forty at work, each making twelve: four hundred and eighty, or twenty four fifths a head. That's four times as much for each person.

@art hunters-and-civilized
Four times as well supplied, with a third fewer at work. Now cut the civilized workers to twenty. The produce falls to two hundred and forty, twelve fifths a head, and they're still twice as well supplied as the hunters.

@chapter The other half
@words text="Same skill: 120 against 80" sub="2 units a worker in both nations"
Smith doesn't deny the share at work matters. Give both nations the same output, two units a worker, and the nation with more at work wins: the hunters' hundred and twenty units against the other's eighty. Here's one way to see why he ranks it second: skill can differ by any amount, but the share at work is a fraction, and it can't go above one.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="More at work always means more wealth@The first|Reading the produce as the wealth@The second|Taking the lesson's nations for Smith's figures@And the third"
The first slip is thinking more people at work always means more wealth. The second is reading the total produce as the wealth, instead of the produce per head. And the third is taking the lesson's nations for Smith's figures. They're written for the lesson. His portrait of the hunters is a conjecture from travellers' reports, and later scholars, like Marshall Sahlins in 1972, have challenged it.

@chapter The plan, and a try
@art five-books
The Introduction ends with a map. Book One explains the improvement in labour's productive powers, and how the produce is shared. Book Two is about capital stock. Book Three is Europe's policy, Book Four the theories behind it, and Book Five the revenue of the sovereign.

@words text="200 people · 50 at work · 12 each" sub="produce per head?"
Your turn. A nation has two hundred people, fifty at useful work, each producing twelve units a year. Multiply, then divide by everyone. You should find three units a head.
