@chapter The question
@title text="The Propensity to Truck and Barter" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 2" art=thumb
The pin factory showed what dividing work can do. But who decided to divide it? Smith's answer, in chapter two, is nobody. No one planned it.

@words text="A propensity, not a plan" sub="to truck, barter and exchange"
It's the slow, gradual consequence of a propensity in human nature, to truck, barter and exchange one thing for another. And that propensity has in view no such extensive usefulness. People weren't aiming at general wealth.

@art two-dogs
Smith says it's found in people and in no other animal. Nobody ever saw a dog make a fair and deliberate exchange of one bone for another with another dog.

@chapter Interest, not kindness
@words text="Give me that which I want, and you shall have this which you want"
Why does it matter? A person in civilized society needs the help of great multitudes, and his whole life is scarce enough to gain the friendship of a few. So he can't rely on their kindness. He has to show them it's in their interest. Every bargain says the same thing: give me that which I want, and you shall have this which you want.

@quote text="It is not from the benevolence of the butcher the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 2"
Then comes the famous sentence. It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.

@art market-stalls
Read it with the sentence before it, and it's narrower than the slogan. It's about the strangers we depend on, not friends or family. It doesn't say kindness is missing from life, only that you can't rely on it to supply a dinner.

@chapter The armourer
@art bow-and-venison
From the propensity, Smith derives the division. In a tribe of hunters or shepherds, one man makes bows and arrows more readily than the rest. He trades them for cattle or venison, and finds he gets more that way than if he went to the field himself. So bows become his chief business.

@chapter Putting numbers on it
@math heading="80 hours each" items="each alone:  80 / (2 + 8) = 8 of each@makes eight;;together:  2 · 8 = 16 of each@sixteen;;armourer, bows only:  80 / 2 = 40@only bows;;hunter, venison only:  80 / 2 = 40@only venison;;gain:  40 − 16 = 24 of each@twenty four"
Smith gives no figures, so the lesson writes some. Two men have eighty hours each. The armourer takes two hours a bow and eight a unit of venison; the hunter, the reverse. Making equal amounts of both, each makes eight of each, sixteen between them. If the armourer makes only bows and the hunter only venison, they get forty of each. A gain of twenty four of each.

@art specialise-bars
The rule that decides who makes what is that each makes the good where his cost, compared with the other's, is lower. That's David Ricardo's rule, from 1817, and it's the lab's rule, not Smith's. And the baseline of equal amounts is a choice made for the sum. It flatters the gain.

@chapter Philosopher and porter
@words text="20 of each → 22.5 of each" sub="nearly equal at both jobs"
Make the two nearly equal at both jobs, and the gain shrinks. Twenty of each becomes only twenty two point five. In the lab, the gain depends on a difference that's already there. But Smith's argument turns that round.

@art two-paths
He says the difference between a philosopher and a common street porter seems to come not so much from nature as from habit, custom and education. At six or eight they were perhaps very much alike. Different talents are more the effect of the division of labour than its cause.

@art common-stock
Dogs of different breeds can't help one another: the mastiff's strength doesn't support the greyhound's speed. But people's talents are brought, as it were, into a common stock, and each can buy what he needs of the others' produce.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the butcher as praise of selfishness@The first|Reading philosopher and porter as proof we start alike@The second|Taking the lab's gain as Smith's calculation@The third is"
The first slip is reading the butcher passage as praise of selfishness. The second is reading philosopher and porter as proof that people start alike. Smith says perhaps, and makes a claim about degree. The third is taking the lab's gain as Smith's calculation. And notice his assumption: the specialist's surplus finds a taker. The next chapter asks what happens if it doesn't.
