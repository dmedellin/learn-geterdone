@chapter The question
@title text="The Pin Factory" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 1" art=thumb
Smith says skill matters most to how well a nation is supplied. To show why, he picks a trade so small you can see all of it at once: making pins.

@quote text="could scarce, perhaps, with his utmost industry, make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 1"
Start with a man who doesn't know the trade or its machinery. He could scarce, perhaps, with his utmost industry, make one pin in a day, and certainly could not make twenty.

@chapter The trade, divided
@art wire-to-pin
Now the trade as it's really carried on. One man draws out the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth points it, a fifth grinds the top for the head. Smith counts about eighteen distinct operations, right down to putting the pins into the paper.

@words text="10 men · 18 operations" sub="18 / 10 = 1⅘ operations each"
He saw a small factory where only ten men worked. Spread eighteen operations over ten men and each does one and four fifths on average. That's why Smith says some of them did two or three.

@chapter The figures
@math heading="One small factory" items="12 · 4000 = 48000 pins a day@twelve pounds;;48000 / 10 = 4800 each@ten men share;;10 · 20 = 200 if all worked alone@worked alone"
When they exerted themselves, they made about twelve pounds of pins a day, with upwards of four thousand pins to the pound. That's upwards of forty eight thousand. Ten men share it, so each makes four thousand eight hundred. If all ten worked alone at twenty, they'd make two hundred.

@art pin-pile
So dividing the work adds forty seven thousand eight hundred pins a day. And mind the word upwards. Forty eight thousand is a floor, taken from one small factory on a good day, not a measured count.

@chapter The multiple
@math heading="The multiple" items="multiple = divided output / output alone@the multiple is;;4800 / 20 = 240@twenty pins;;4800 / 1 = 4800@just one pin"
The multiple is the output of one man when the work is divided, over what he could make alone. At twenty pins alone at most, it's two hundred and forty. If he could make just one pin alone, it's four thousand eight hundred.

@art multiple-bars
That's Smith's two hundred and fortieth part, and his four thousand eight hundredth. And if a man alone could make no pins at all, there's no multiple, because you can't divide by nothing. The ten have gone from no pins to forty eight thousand.

@chapter Three causes
@art three-causes
Why such a jump? Smith gives three causes. First, more dexterity in every workman. Second, saving the time lost in passing from one kind of work to another. And third, the invention of machines that make labour easier and shorter.

@list heading="Nails a day" items="Common smith: 200 to 300@a common smith|Smith used to nails: 800 to 1,000@used to nails|Boys who only made nails: upwards of 2,300@several boys"
For dexterity he uses nails. A common smith might make two or three hundred a day, and bad ones. A smith used to nails, eight hundred or a thousand. But several boys under twenty who'd never done anything else made upwards of two thousand three hundred each, more than seven times the common smith's three hundred.

@art steam-valve
For machines, he tells of a boy whose job was to open and shut a valve on an early steam engine. He tied a string from the valve's handle to another part of the machine, so he could go and play. One of the engine's greatest improvements, found by a boy who wanted to save his own labour.

@chapter What the example leaves out
@art woollen-coat
Then the chapter widens. A day labourer's woollen coat is the joint work of a multitude: shepherd, sorter, spinner, dyer, weaver, merchants, carriers and shipbuilders. Smith also limits his claim. Farming can't be divided so finely, because the seasons bring its tasks round one at a time.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading 48,000 as a measured count@The first|Using 240 for every trade@The second|Taking three causes as the whole story@And the third"
The first slip is reading forty eight thousand as a measured count. The second is using the multiple as the gain of every trade, when Smith chose the pin as his most favourable case. And the third is taking the three causes as the whole story. What the work does to the worker, Smith takes up only in Book Five.

@chapter Try it
@words text="12 lb · 4,000 a pound · 10 men · 20 alone" sub="each man's share, then the multiple"
Your turn. Multiply the pounds by the pins in a pound, divide by the ten men, and divide again by twenty. You should get four thousand eight hundred each, and a multiple of two hundred and forty.
