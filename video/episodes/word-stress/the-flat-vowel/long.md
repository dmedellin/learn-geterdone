@chapter About
@title text="The Flat Vowel" sub="Word Stress" art=thumb
Say about slowly: a, bout. Now say it the way you would in a sentence. The first part is so short that you hardly hear a vowel in it. The second part carries the word.

@art strong-and-quiet
The last three lessons found where the strong part goes. This one is about the parts around it. Every part of a word that isn't the strong part is weak. A weak part is shorter and quieter, and its vowel loses its clear shape.

@words text="the flat vowel  /ə/" sub="the start of about"
The sound it falls to most often has a name in this course: the flat vowel. It's the short, plain sound at the start of about, said with no effort at all.

@chapter What was counted
@art share-bar
The lab takes every word of two or more parts in the list, and keeps the parts the dictionary marks with no beat at all. That's a thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine words, and two thousand six hundred and twenty-eight weak parts. Then it reads the vowel of each one.

The flat vowel: a thousand three hundred and eighty-seven, more than half. The flat vowel with an r, as at the end of number: four hundred. The vowel of sit, three hundred and fifty-two. The vowel of see, three hundred and forty-eight. And another vowel, a hundred and forty-one.

@words text="1,387 + 400 = 1,787" sub="a little over two in three"
So the flat vowel is about half of all the weak parts. Add the four hundred with an r, and it's a little over two in three.

@chapter Four spellings, one sound
@math heading="Four spellings, one sound" items="about  ·  weak part: a@In about;;today  ·  weak part: to@In today;;listen  ·  weak part: ten@In listen;;minute  ·  weak part: ute@in minute"
Here's why the spelling hides it. Find the weak part of each word. In about, it's a. In today, to. In listen, ten. And in minute, it's the end, u-t-e.

@art four-to-one
The letters are a, o, e and u. But the sound is the same every time: the flat vowel. It has no letter of its own. It's written with whatever vowel letter the word had when it was written down. Say each one as its letter, and you'll say four different sounds, and none is the sound in the word.

@chapter The two that are not flat
@art ringed-dots
Two kinds of weak part aren't flat. Most of the parts that say the vowel of see are the y at the end of words like happy, city and money. The rest are mostly an i or e before another vowel, as in radio and area, or the front piece r-e, as in remind and report.

@words text="active · basic · morning" sub="-ive · -ic · -ing  →  the vowel of sit"
The vowel of sit belongs to endings: i-v-e in active, i-c in basic, and i-n-g in morning, evening and nothing. Both kinds are still weak. Say the y of happy quickly and lightly, and give the push to the first part.

@chapter Why it matters
@art sound-waves
A word said with a clear strong part and flat, quiet parts around it is a word a listener hears at once, even if one sound is wrong. The quiet parts are what make the strong part stand out.

@art level-bars
Give every part a clear vowel, and the strong part gets lost. That's why a student who says the flat vowel well sounds clearer than one who says every vowel clearly. One limit: the sounds are American, and a British speaker may use a different vowel in some weak parts.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="reading every vowel letter as itself@The first|thinking the flat vowel is careless@The second|thinking every weak part is flat@The third"
The first slip is reading every vowel letter as itself. The second is thinking the flat vowel is a careless way to speak. It isn't. It's the standard way. And the third is thinking every weak part is flat. The end of happy and the end of active aren't.

@chapter Try it
@words text="woman · listen · happy" sub="which weak part is not flat?"
Your turn. Find the weak part of woman, listen and happy, and say it. Which one isn't the flat vowel? Pause and try. The man of woman is flat. The ten of listen is flat. But the end of happy says the vowel of see.
