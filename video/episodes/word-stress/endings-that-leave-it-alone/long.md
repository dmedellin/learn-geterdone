@chapter Endings that change nothing
@title text="Endings That Leave It Alone" sub="Word Stress" art=thumb
The last lesson had endings that move the strong part, like education and ability. Most endings aren't like that. Add n-e-s-s to happy, and the strong part stays right where it was.

@art add-weak-dot
When you add n-e-s-s to happy, or e-r to teach, you make a longer word. Each part of a word is built around a vowel, and the ending adds one more part. But the longer word is strong on the same part as the word you began with.

@art six-tabs
The lab tests six of these endings. Here they are, each with a short word and the same word with the ending.

@list heading="Six endings" items="-ly  ·  quick, quickly@L-y|-ness  ·  dark, darkness@N-e-s-s:|-ment  ·  agree, agreement@M-e-n-t|-er  ·  teach, teacher@E-r: teach|-ful  ·  care, careful@F-u-l|-able  ·  accept, acceptable@a-b-l-e"
L-y: quick, quickly. N-e-s-s: dark, darkness. M-e-n-t: agree, agreement. E-r: teach, teacher. F-u-l: care, careful. And a-b-l-e: accept, acceptable. Add the ending, and leave the strong part where it was.

@chapter How often it's right
@math heading="The strong part stays" items="-ly  ·  83 of 87@eighty-three;;-ness  6 of 6  ·  -ful  7 of 7  ·  -able  10 of 10@six of six;;-ment  ·  31 of 32@thirty-one;;-er  ·  48 of 50@forty-eight"
The lab looks at pairs: a short word, and the same word with the ending. With l-y, the strong part stays put in eighty-three of eighty-seven pairs. With n-e-s-s, six of six. F-u-l, seven of seven, and a-b-l-e, ten of ten. M-e-n-t, thirty-one of thirty-two. And e-r, forty-eight of fifty.

@art stays-put
That's a hundred and eighty-five of a hundred and ninety-two pairs. The lab only forms a pair when both words are in the list and the dictionary. It puts back a dropped e or a changed y first, so happy and happiness are a pair, and so are argue and argument.

@chapter The misses for -ly
@words text="absolute → absolutely" sub="necessary → necessarily  ·  primary → primarily"
Three of the four misses for l-y are long words where the dictionary marks a strong beat early and a smaller one later. With the ending, the main beat moves to the later one. Absolute, absolutely. Necessary, necessarily. Primary, primarily.

@words text="to per·FECT  ·  PER·fect" sub="perfectly goes with the adjective"
The fourth, perfectly, is a mistake of reading. The dictionary's first reading of perfect is the verb, as in to perfect a skill, which is strong on the second part. But perfectly goes with the adjective, which is strong on the first.

@chapter One that moves, and two false alarms
@art moved-dot
The one miss for m-e-n-t is advertisement. Advertise is strong on its first part, but the dictionary puts the push of advertisement on its second part, ver. It's the only one of the thirty-two pairs where the push moves.

@art false-pair
The two misses for e-r are career and researcher. Career is a false pair. It isn't care with an ending, it's a word in e-e-r, and the last lesson's rule gets it right. And research is one of the words said two ways, so that pair was tried against the wrong reading.

@words text="beer · be   ·   shoulder · should   ·   only · on" sub="false pairs, counted right"
The lab pairs by spelling, so there are more false pairs, and nearly all of them land on the rule's side. Beer and be. Shoulder and should. Only and on. Both words happen to be strong on the first part, so they're counted right. The table prints every pair, so you can strike them out.

@chapter Keep the ending weak
@art quiet-ending
One more thing. The ending is quiet. Don't give f-u-l or n-e-s-s the vowel of the word they come from. The ending of careful is short. It isn't said like the word full. The same goes for m-e-n-t and a-b-l-e.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="thinking every ending moves the stress@The first|giving the ending a strong vowel@The second|trusting a pair because the spelling matches@The third"
The first slip is thinking every ending moves the stress. After t-i-o-n and i-t-y, it's natural to guess that, but these six don't. The second is giving the ending a strong vowel. And the third is trusting a pair just because the spelling matches, like bother and both.

@chapter Try it
@words text="happiness · agreement · careful · teacher" sub="where is the strong part?"
Your turn. Find the strong part of happiness, agreement, careful and teacher. Start from the short word. Happy is strong on its first part, so happiness is too. Agree is strong on its second, so agreement is too. Careful and teacher are strong on the first.
