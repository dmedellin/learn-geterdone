@chapter Money and decide
@title text="Nouns at the Front, Verbs at the Back" sub="Word Stress" art=thumb
Say money. Then say decide. Both words have two parts, and in each one, one part is stronger. In money, it's the first. In decide, it's the second. And which one is not luck.

@art two-beats
A part is one beat of a word. Money has two, decide has two, and tomorrow has three. In every word of two or more parts, one part is said longer, louder and higher than the others. This course calls it the strong part.

@words text="MON·ey  ·  de·CIDE" sub="the strong part helps a listener find the word"
And it matters. A listener uses the strong part to find the word. So if you put it in the wrong place, you're often not understood, even when every sound in the word is right.

@chapter The rule
@list heading="Two parts: the rule" items="noun  →  first  ·  action, brother, country, kitchen@A noun is|verb  →  second  ·  accept, agree, explain, enjoy@A verb is|adjective  →  first  ·  angry, famous, lazy@An adjective"
Here's the rule for words of two parts. A noun is strong on the first part: action, brother, country, kitchen. A verb is strong on the second: accept, agree, explain, enjoy. An adjective, a word for a quality, is strong on the first, like a noun: angry, famous, lazy.

@art thing-action
So you don't learn each word on its own. You ask what the word is doing in your sentence. A thing, or an action? I need advice uses a noun. I will believe it uses a verb.

@chapter How often it's right
@math heading="Scored against a dictionary" items="nouns  ·  207 of 231  =  89.6%@The noun rule;;verbs  ·  134 of 150  =  89.3%@The verb rule;;adjectives  ·  35 of 47  =  74.5%@the adjective rule"
The lab checks words of one class against a pronouncing dictionary. The noun rule is right for two hundred and seven of two hundred and thirty-one words. The verb rule, a hundred and thirty-four of a hundred and fifty. Both about nine in ten. And the adjective rule, thirty-five of forty-seven: three in four.

@chapter The nouns that break it
@art verb-to-noun
Twenty-four nouns break the rule, in three kinds. Ten are made from a verb: advice, belief, complaint, success. The verb, advise, believe, complain, succeed, is strong at the back, and the noun keeps it there.

@list heading="24 nouns" items="from a verb  ·  advice, belief, device, success …  ·  10@ten made from a verb|a number or a month  ·  eighteen, fifteen, July  ·  3@Three more|on their own  ·  hotel, guitar, hello, exam …  ·  11@And eleven"
So that's ten made from a verb. Three more are a number or a month: eighteen, fifteen and July. The push at the end is how a listener tells fifteen from fifty. And eleven sit alone, like hotel, guitar, hello and exam. Most came into English from French, which puts the push at the end, and they kept it.

@chapter Verbs and adjectives
@art weak-end
Sixteen verbs break the rule. Ten of them end in a weak second part: alter, differ, enter, listen, suffer, threaten. Say them, and the second part is only a flat sound and a consonant. It's too light to carry the push, so the push stays on the first part.

@art front-piece
The adjective rule misses twelve words, and every one begins with a front piece that takes no push. Afraid, alive, aware and unclear begin with a or un. Distinct, intense, precise and remote begin with a piece from Latin. So for adjectives, read the rule as: first, unless the word begins with a front piece.

@chapter Words that are both
@art one-spelling-two
The lab also lists forty-nine words that are both a noun and a verb, and are said two ways. Address, contract, increase, object, permit, present, record, and more.

@words text="a RE·cord  ·  to re·CORD" sub="a PRE·sent  ·  to pre·SENT"
Record is the clearest case. As a noun, a record is strong on the first part. As a verb, to record is strong on the second. The spelling is the same. The strong part tells the listener which word it is.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="thinking the strong part has no rule@The first|one place for a noun and its verb@The second|using the rule for every word@The third"
The first slip is thinking the strong part has no rule. The second is putting the strong part in the same place in a noun and its verb. I need a permit, and they permit it, are different words to the ear. The third is using this rule on longer words, like family or important.

@chapter Try it
@words text="action · believe · present · hotel" sub="where is the strong part?"
Your turn. Where is the strong part in action, believe, present the noun, and hotel? Pause and say them. Action is a noun, so the first part. Believe is a verb, so the second. A present is a noun, so the first. And hotel is one of the eleven nouns that sit alone, strong on the second.
