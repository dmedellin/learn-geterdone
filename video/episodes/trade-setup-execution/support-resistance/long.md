@chapter The question
@title text="Support and Resistance" sub="Trade Setup & Execution" art=thumb
Draw a line where price turned before, and wait for it to turn again. That's how most people start with support and resistance. And it's why so many charts end up covered in lines that all seem to matter, and none that actually help.

@words text="Where are meaningful reactions more likely?"
This lesson asks a narrower question. Where are meaningful reactions, breakouts, and role reversals more likely to affect a trade plan? The answer is a few well placed zones, not many exact lines.

@words text="Process and measurement" sub="no live data, no recommendations, no signals"
As with every lesson here, it teaches process and measurement. It doesn't give live market data, personal recommendations, or trade signals.

@chapter Three ideas
@art zone-band
First, zones. Treat support and resistance as reaction areas rather than exact prices. The center of a zone is only a reference. Its width should reflect the cluster of reactions, the volatility, and the timeframe.

@list heading="Core concepts" items="Zones, not exact prices@Zones|Weight by reaction@reaction|Acceptance beats a wick@acceptance"
Zones, then. Second, reaction. Give more weight to levels that produced meaningful turns, breakouts, or protected swings. And third, acceptance. A wick through a level is different from sustained trading and closes beyond it.

@art wick-vs-acceptance
On the left, a wick pokes through and the candle closes back. That's the level being tested. On the right, candles close beyond it and keep trading there. That's acceptance, and only that changes the picture.

@words text="Width follows the timeframe" sub="match the zone to the analysis timeframe"
So match a zone's width and importance to the timeframe you're analyzing. A zone built from daily reactions shouldn't be judged by one small hourly wick. That's low timeframe noise, and the daily zone answers a bigger question.

@chapter Placing the zones
@art too-many-lines
Use repeated reactions and structural turning points. Avoid drawing a line through every small candle. Too many levels remove any sense of priority, and they make every outcome look predictable after the fact.

@list heading="Level selection" items="Start with major swings@Start with major swings|Group nearby reactions@Group nearby reactions|Observe the interaction@Observe the interaction|Use the next opposing zone@Use the next opposing zone"
The process has four steps. Start with major swings: obvious highs, lows, range edges, and breakout origins. Group nearby reactions. Observe the interaction. And use the next opposing zone.

@art cluster-group
Grouping is the step people skip. If several reactions cluster at nearby prices, combine them into one zone instead of drawing many lines. Mark fewer, more meaningful areas, and update them only when the market's acceptance changes.

@chapter Reading the interaction
@words text="Rejection · Acceptance · Breakout · Retest · Sweep"
When price reaches a zone, classify what happens. A rejection, acceptance, a breakout, a retest, or a sweep. Each one means something different for the plan. A rejection reaches the zone and turns. Acceptance closes beyond it and keeps trading there. A sweep trades through and closes back inside. You name it from what price actually did, not from what you hoped it would do.

@art role-reversal
And watch for role reversal. A broken resistance zone can become support, and broken support can become resistance. Price breaks through, comes back, and the old ceiling is now a floor.

@art room-to-next
Finally, look across to the next opposing zone. It tells you how much room is available before you plan your entry and your target. If the next zone is close, there may be no practical room for a target at all, however clean the reaction looks.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="A level at every pivot@The first|A wick taken as a breakout@The second|Ignoring role reversal@The third|Low-timeframe noise for high-timeframe decisions@And the fourth"
The first mistake is drawing a level at every pivot. The second is treating a wick as a confirmed breakout, instead of requiring the close and acceptance your plan defines. The third is ignoring role reversal. And the fourth is using low timeframe noise for high timeframe decisions. Match a zone's importance and width to the timeframe you're analyzing.
