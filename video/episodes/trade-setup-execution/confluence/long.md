@chapter The question
@title text="Confluence" sub="Trade Setup & Execution" art=thumb
Price is above the twenty period moving average, above the fifty, and above the two hundred. That's three reasons to buy, isn't it? This lesson says it's probably one reason, counted three times.

@words text="Independent, relevant evidence"
Confluence means combining evidence. But the evidence has to be relevant and independent. Several correlated indicators aren't several separate reasons to trade.

@words text="Process and measurement" sub="no live data, no recommendations, no signals"
Like the rest of this course, the lesson teaches process and measurement. It doesn't give live data, personal recommendations, or trade signals.

@chapter Three ideas
@art independent-pillars
Independent evidence comes from factors that answer different questions. Context, location, trigger, and participation each ask something the others don't.

@list heading="Core concepts" items="Independent evidence@Independent evidence|Relevance@relevance|Decision@the decision"
So independent evidence is the first idea. The second is relevance. A factor only matters when it supports the specific setup and timeframe you're trading. And the third is about the decision. More evidence never removes the need for invalidation, adequate reward, and a no trade condition.

@chapter Correlated tools
@art correlated-lines
Here's the trap. A twenty period moving average, a fifty period moving average, and a two hundred period one are all derived from the same trend. Price above all three may still be one trend category, not three independent reasons.

@list heading="The lab's evidence list" items="Higher-timeframe structure@Higher timeframe structure|Key price location@key price location|Entry confirmation@entry confirmation|Volume participation@volume participation|Relative strength or weakness@relative strength|20 MA · 50 MA · RSI@moving averages"
The lab offers a menu. Higher timeframe structure, key price location, entry confirmation, volume participation, and relative strength or weakness. Then two moving averages, both trend derived, and an RSI condition, which is momentum derived. Those last three don't add three new questions.

@art stacked-same
So when you tick them, count correlated indicators as one reason. Three trend indicators rarely give you three independent reasons. Independent evidence is more useful than several tools measuring the same thing.

@art evidence-menu
Look at the menu again. The first five answer five different questions: the regime, the place, the trigger, who's taking part, and how the instrument compares with its benchmark. The last three are indicators: two derived from the trend, and one from momentum. The two moving averages, in particular, measure the same thing. That's why ticking more boxes doesn't automatically mean a better trade, or a more confident one.

@chapter The evaluation process
@list heading="Four checks" items="Check context@Check context|Check location@Check location|Check trigger@Check trigger|Check participation and room@Check participation"
Use a small number of independent categories. Check context: the higher timeframe regime supports the setup, or at least doesn't directly conflict with it. Check location: a meaningful level, range edge, or breakout area. Check trigger: wait for the event your entry model defines. And check participation and room.

@art room-check
That last check has two parts. Use volume or relative performance when it's relevant, and then verify that there's room to the target. Plenty of agreement means little if the next opposing level is right in front of you.

@words text="More evidence ≠ a decision" sub="still need invalidation, reward and a no-trade condition"
And remember the third core idea. Even a strong, independent set of evidence doesn't make the decision for you. You still need an invalidation, enough reward, and a written condition for not trading. The best answer can still be wait.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Counting correlated indicators separately@The first|Adding evidence after entry@The second|Ignoring a failed core condition@The third|Requiring every factor on every setup@And the fourth"
The first mistake is counting correlated indicators separately. The second is adding evidence after entry, which stops you reviewing the original decision honestly. The third is ignoring a failed core condition. And the fourth is requiring every factor on every setup, which can make your rules untestable.

@art failed-core
That third one is worth a picture. Extra indicators cannot make up for invalid structure, or for a lack of room. If a core condition fails, the pile of supporting evidence doesn't hold the trade up.
