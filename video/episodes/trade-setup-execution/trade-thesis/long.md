@chapter The question
@title text="Trade Thesis" sub="Trade Setup & Execution" art=thumb
I'm bullish on this chart. It sounds like a plan, but it isn't one. It doesn't say when you'd enter, where you'd be wrong, or where you'd take profit. And after the trade, nobody could check whether you followed it.

@art bias-arrow
This lesson turns a view of the market into a conditional plan. Context, a setup, a trigger, an invalidation, a target, and a no trade condition. Each piece is written before the trade, so each piece can be checked after it.

@words text="Process and measurement" sub="no live data, no recommendations, no signals"
A note on scope. The lesson teaches process and measurement. It doesn't provide live market data, personalized recommendations, or trade signals.

@chapter Three core ideas
@list heading="Core concepts" items="Context@Context|Trigger@Trigger|Invalidation@Invalidation"
Context states the higher timeframe structure, and the location that makes the setup relevant. Trigger defines the observable event that permits entry. Directional bias alone is not a trigger. And invalidation names the price or condition that proves the thesis wrong, before position size is calculated.

@art trigger-close
A trigger has to be something you can see happen. A close beyond a level. A retest that holds. A structure event. If you can't point to the candle where it happened, it isn't a trigger.

@art invalidation-line
And the order matters. Invalidation comes from the structure first. Only then do you work out a size that fits your risk budget. Not the other way round.

@chapter The process
@list heading="Four steps" items="Describe context@Describe context|Name the setup@Name the setup|Require a trigger@Require a trigger|Define failure and objective@Define failure"
Describe context: structure, location, and any higher timeframe conflict. Name the setup: one defined setup, such as a pullback, a breakout, or a reversal. Require a trigger: the exact close, retest, or structure event for entry. Define failure and objective: structural invalidation, a target, and the no trade condition.

@art thesis-card
A thesis is complete only when every decision can be evaluated objectively after the trade. Five lines: decision, setup, trigger, invalidation and target. Every line written, and every line checkable later without anyone rewriting it.

@chapter The worked example
@words text="The bullish pullback tab" sub="every line checkable at the decision candle"
The lesson works one example in full, on its bullish pullback tab. Every line is checkable against the chart at the decision candle. The idea is to read it once, then complete the other three tabs without it. And then the lesson makes an important point about that worked plan.

@list heading="The bullish pullback, worked" items="Context: daily bullish, back to the 108.0 breakout area@Context|Setup: pullback@setup is a pullback|Trigger: the 111.7 close@trigger is the close"
Context: daily bullish structure, with higher highs and higher lows, corrected to the one hundred eight area it broke out from. The decision is long, and the setup is a pullback. The trigger is the close at one hundred eleven point seven, the first close back above the whole correction.

@list heading="Failure and objective" items="Invalidation: 107.0@Invalidation is one hundred seven|Target: 115.0, the prior high@target is one hundred fifteen|No trade: a close below 108.1 first@no trade condition"
Invalidation is one hundred seven, under the correction's one hundred eight point one low, with room for a wick. The target is one hundred fifteen, the prior high, not chosen to make a ratio. And the no trade condition: a close below one hundred eight point one before the trigger cancels the plan.

@math heading="From the 111.7 trigger" items="risk = 111.7 − 107.0 = 4.7@risks four point seven;;reward = 115.0 − 111.7 = 3.3@makes three point three;;3.3 ÷ 4.7 = 0.70R@zero point seven"
From the one hundred eleven point seven trigger down to one hundred seven, this plan risks four point seven points, and up to one hundred fifteen it makes three point three. Three point three divided by four point seven is zero point seven R.

@words text="Complete ≠ worth taking"
So complete is not the same as worth taking. A later lesson, Risk to Reward, explains why zero point seven R matters. This lesson's job is narrower: every line is written, and can be checked.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Bias presented as a plan@The first|Invalidation from a desired dollar loss@The second|A target forced to reach 2R@The third|Changing conditions after entry@And the fourth"
The first mistake is bias presented as a plan. The second is choosing invalidation from the dollar loss you'd like, instead of from structure. The third is a target picked only to force two R, when real resistance or support appears first. And the fourth is changing conditions after entry. A thesis rewritten after every candle can't be tested.

@art target-level
That third one is subtle. A mathematical target isn't useful when a structural level sits in the way. The structure decides where the target can realistically be.

@chapter Try it
@art no-trade-range
Now the lesson's standard. Write all five lines on a tab you haven't seen resolved, without changing one of them after the later candles appear. And on the range and conflict tabs, the five lines are the no trade condition, written out. Sometimes the complete plan is to stand aside.

@words text="Decision · setup · trigger · invalidation · target" sub="no line changed after the reveal"
So the test is simple to say and hard to do. Write the five lines, let the evaluator agree, and then leave them alone while the rest of the chart plays out.
