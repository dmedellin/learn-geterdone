@chapter The question
@title text="Trade Management" sub="Trade Setup & Execution" art=thumb
You're in a trade and it's moving. Every candle seems to ask a question. Move the stop? Take some profit? Hold? Most of the damage in trading happens right here, when those questions get answered by feel.

@words text="Decide the milestones before entry"
This lesson's answer is to decide before entry. Holding, reducing, moving the stop, trailing, and exiting all happen at predefined milestones. And then discipline gets scored separately from the outcome.

@words text="Process and measurement" sub="no live data, no recommendations, no signals"
As with the rest of this course, it teaches process and measurement. It doesn't give live market data, personal recommendations, or trade signals. The trade in its lab is simulated.

@chapter Three ideas
@art milestone-ladder
First, a predefined plan. Choose your management milestones before entry, instead of reacting to each candle emotionally. They can be observable price or structure events, or R multiples, like the ones this lesson's plan uses. Either way, you can point to the moment each one happens.

@art stop-ratchet
Second, the stop only tightens. A stop may stay where it is, or move toward reduced risk. It should never move farther away after entry.

@art process-vs-outcome
And third, outcome is separate. A poor decision can make money, and a correct decision can lose. So you review both the process and the result, and you don't let one stand in for the other. A lucky winner can teach a bad habit, and an unlucky loser can hide a good one.

@chapter The lab's plan
@list heading="The written plan" items="Before +1R: don't move the stop@before plus one R|At +1R: move the stop to break-even@At plus one R|At +2R: reduce 50%@At plus two R|Then trail the remainder 1R behind@trail the remainder"
Here's the plan the lab scores you against. Do not move the stop before plus one R. At plus one R, move it to break even. At plus two R, reduce the position by fifty percent, then trail the remainder one R behind, from the next candle.

@art breakeven-move
Walk through it. Until the trade reaches plus one R, you leave the stop alone, even if it feels exposed. Once it reaches plus one R, the stop moves up to your entry. From then on, the stop sits at your entry instead of below it.

@art scale-half
At plus two R, half the position comes off. That takes profit on half the position, and leaves the other half working, under a stop that can now only move one way.

@art trailing-stop
And from the next candle, the remainder is trailed one R behind. The stop follows the trade up, but never moves back down. Every action is scored against this written plan, not against hindsight.

@chapter The process
@list heading="Trade management process" items="Start with the original plan@Start with the original plan|Act only at milestones@Act only at milestones|Never expand planned risk@Never expand planned risk|Review execution separately@Review execution separately"
Start with the original plan: the stop, targets, scale percentages, and trailing conditions, all recorded before entry. Act only at milestones. Never expand planned risk: don't widen the stop or add risk as invalidation approaches. And review execution separately, scoring whether you followed the plan before judging the money.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Moving to break-even too early@The first|Widening the stop to avoid a loss@The second|Partial profits without a rule@The third|Optimizing from one memorable trade@And the fourth"
The first mistake is moving to break even too early. Normal volatility can knock out a valid trade before the planned trigger. The second is widening the stop to avoid a loss, which turns controlled risk into a larger, unplanned loss. The third is taking partial profit without a repeatable rule. And the fourth is optimizing management from one memorable trade.

@words text="Rules need a broad sample"
That last one is subtle. One trade that would have done better with a different rule proves very little. Management rules need a broad sample across different market paths. Random scaling also changes your average wins, and makes backtesting unreliable.
