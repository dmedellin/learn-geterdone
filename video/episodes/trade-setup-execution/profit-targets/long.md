@chapter The question
@title text="Profit Targets" sub="Trade Setup & Execution" art=thumb
Where should you take profit? A common answer is two R, or three R, because those are nice round multiples of your risk. But the market doesn't know about your ratio. There might be a wall of resistance well before it.

@words text="Structure picks the target · R measures it"
This lesson turns the order around. Choose objectives from opposing structure first. Then, and only then, measure them in R. And decide in advance whether you'll exit once, scale out, or trail a remainder.

@words text="Process and measurement" sub="no live data, no recommendations, no signals"
As with the rest of this course, it teaches process and measurement. It doesn't give live market data, personal recommendations, or trade signals.

@chapter Structural objectives
@art opposing-levels
Targets come from structure. Prior highs, prior lows, range edges, and other opposing levels are your references. Mark the nearest objective, and the extended one beyond it. Those are the levels where the other side has already shown up before.

@art invented-targets
What you don't do is draw a line at two R and three R and call those targets. If visible opposing structure sits in front of them, the planned reward is unrealistic. In the lab, you start with invented two R and three R prices and move them to where the structure is.

@chapter Measuring in R
@math heading="Reward in R" items="1R = entry − initial stop@one R is the distance;;target R = (target − entry) ÷ 1R@divide the reward"
One R is the distance from entry to the initial stop. That's the unit. To measure a target, divide the reward, the distance from entry to target, by one R. You use R to evaluate the opportunity, never to invent where the target should be. For a short, the distances simply run the other way, below the entry instead of above it.

@chapter Three exit methods
@art single-target
The first method is a single structural target. It's best when there's a clear opposing level or a range objective. Its cost: no participation beyond the target.

@art scale-out
The second is scaling out. It suits two meaningful objectives, or uncertain continuation. Its cost: when price trends strongly, it reduces the reward the full position would have earned.

@art trail-remainder
And the third is trailing a remainder. It suits trend continuation with expanding structure. Its cost: it gives back some open profit before the exit.

@list heading="Every method has a cost" items="Single target: nothing beyond the target@Single target|Scale out: less when trends run@Scale out|Trail: gives back open profit@Trail"
So, side by side. Single target gives up anything beyond the target. Scale out gives up some reward when trends run. Trail gives back open profit. There's no free method. You pick the cost that fits the setup, and you pick it before entry, not while the candles are moving.

@chapter What changes the plan
@art give-back
The last step of the process is to define what changes the plan. Decide in advance whether new structure allows you to start trailing, or whether the original objective stays fixed. Then it's a rule, not a feeling in the middle of the trade. If the plan says the objective is fixed, it stays fixed, even when the trade is going well and more seems possible.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Inventing a target to reach a preferred R@The first|Moving the target farther after entry@The second|Taking profit from fear without a rule@The third|Assuming scaling always helps@And the fourth"
The first mistake is inventing a target to reach a preferred R multiple. The second is moving the target farther after entry, which turns a working plan into an untested attempt to capture more. The third is taking profit from fear, without a rule, which makes expectancy hard to measure. And the fourth is assuming scaling always improves results.

@words text="Scaling: less risk and volatility" sub="but possibly a smaller average win"
It doesn't. Scaling reduces risk and volatility, but it can also reduce your average win size. Whether that's a good trade off is something to measure, not assume.
