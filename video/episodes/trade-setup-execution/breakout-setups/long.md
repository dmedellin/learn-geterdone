@chapter The question
@title text="Breakout Setups" sub="Trade Setup & Execution" art=thumb
Price pushes through the top of a range. Do you buy right now? Many traders do, and many of them find price back inside the range a few candles later. This lesson separates the structural event, the break, from the decision to enter.

@words text="Enter · Wait · No trade"
At every candle there are three possible answers: enter, wait, or no trade. The lesson's lab replays confirmed, retested, failed, and crowded breakouts, and asks for one of those three, candle by candle.

@words text="Process and measurement" sub="no live data, no recommendations, no signals"
As always in this course, the lesson teaches process and measurement. It doesn't provide live market data, personal recommendations, or trade signals.

@chapter Three ideas
@art boundary-first
First, the boundary. The range edge or structural level must exist before the breakout happens. If you draw the line after price crosses it, you're describing the move, not planning for it.

@art close-and-hold
Second, acceptance. It's the difference between visiting a price and staying there. A close outside, followed by continued trading out there, or a successful retest, is stronger than a wick through the level.

@art crowded-room
And third, room. A valid breakout is not automatically a good trade. If the next opposing level is too close, there's nowhere for the trade to go. The break can be perfectly real, and the trade still not worth taking. Keep those two questions apart.

@chapter The process
@list heading="Four steps" items="Define the boundary@Define the boundary|Observe the break@Observe the break|Confirm acceptance@Confirm acceptance|Plan invalidation and target@Plan invalidation"
Define the boundary: mark the range edge or level before price reaches it. Observe the break: require the candle close rule your setup specifies. Confirm acceptance: continuation outside, or a successful retest, according to your entry model. And plan invalidation and target.

@art retest-entry
For invalidation, place the failure point beyond the reclaimed level. And confirm there's adequate room to the next opposing level before you call it a trade. A break is not complete just because price traded beyond the boundary.

@chapter Reading it candle by candle
@art first-wick
Here's how the answers change as candles arrive. A wick through the boundary, on a candle that hasn't closed, isn't a break yet. Intrabar movement can reverse before the candle on your analysis timeframe closes.

@list heading="What each moment calls for" items="A close outside, not yet accepted → wait@yet been accepted|A valid break with no room → no trade@no room|A close back inside → no trade@closes back inside"
After a close outside that hasn't yet been accepted, the answer is wait. When a valid break has no room to the next level, it's no trade. And once price closes back inside the range, it's no trade too. Wait isn't indecision. It means the close happened, and the acceptance your model asks for hasn't happened yet. The next candles, or a retest, will settle it.

@art failed-back-inside
That last case is where people get stubborn. When price returns inside and invalidates the model, cancel or reassess the setup. Don't redefine a failed breakout as a pullback just to stay in.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Entering on the first wick@The first|Chasing far above the level@The second|Ignoring nearby support or resistance@The third|Calling a failed breakout a pullback@And the fourth"
The first mistake is entering on the first wick beyond a boundary. The second is chasing far above the level, which can destroy reward to risk even when the breakout later works. The third is ignoring nearby resistance or support. And the fourth is redefining a failed breakout as a pullback.

@words text="Late entry, poor reward" sub="even when the breakout works"
That second one is easy to underestimate. The farther you buy above the level, the farther away your invalidation sits, and the less room is left to the target. The breakout can succeed, and the trade can still be a poor one.
