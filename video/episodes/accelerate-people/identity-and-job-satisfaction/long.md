@chapter The argument
@title text="Identity and Job Satisfaction" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 10" art=thumb
How people feel about their work isn't a side effect of delivery. Chapter ten of Accelerate argues it joins a cycle with it, and it measures that feeling in two separate ways.

@quote text="[P]eople who feel supported by their employers, who have the tools and resources to do their work, and who feel their judgment is valued, turn out better work." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 10"
Here's the argument in miniature. People who feel supported by their employers, who have the tools and resources to do their work, and who feel their judgment is valued, turn out better work.

@chapter Identity
@art belonging
The first measure is identity: how far you identify with the organization you work for. The authors expected teams that practice continuous delivery, and take an experimental approach to product development, to feel closer to the rest of the organization.

@list heading="How identity was measured" items="Statements adapted from Kankanhalli et al., 2005@Kankanhalli|Likert-type agreement scales@Likert type|A person's score: the mean of the items@average"
They asked how far respondents agreed with several statements adapted from Kankanhalli and colleagues, twenty oh five: being glad to work there, sharing the organization's values and goals, and the like. The answers were Likert type, and the items passed the statistical checks for a construct, so a person's identity is the average of their item scores.

@art handoff-batch
Identity predicted a generative culture and organizational performance: productivity, market share, profitability. The authors set it against the way many companies still work, handing requirements to a team that then delivers large batches. That leaves people with little control and little connection. The authors tell a story: Netflix's cloud architect, Adrian Cockcroft, was asked by a senior leader of a large company where he found his remarkable people. He'd hired them from that very company.

@chapter Job satisfaction
@list heading="The job satisfaction measure" items="Satisfied with your work@satisfied with your work|Given the tools and resources@tools and resources|Your skills put to good use@skills"
Job satisfaction is built differently. It looks at whether you're satisfied with your work, whether you're given the tools and resources you need, and whether the job puts your skills to good use. The second and third are how the authors connect it to DevOps.

@art toolbox
Taken together, the authors say, these three are what make the measure so impactful. The tools and the use of your skills are things an organization gives you, or doesn't.

@art feedback-loop
Good technical practices predicted job satisfaction. The reasoning: automation gives the rote tasks to computers, and leaves people the work of weighing evidence and deciding. Practices that put people in a feedback loop, like proactive monitoring and test and deployment automation, correlated strongly with it.

@chapter A virtuous circle
@art virtuous-circle
So the chain runs like this. Technical and Lean practices predict identity and job satisfaction. Supported people turn out better work, which means higher delivery performance and higher organizational performance. Faster delivery lets teams validate ideas sooner, which raises satisfaction again. The authors call it a virtuous circle.

@words text="Predict is not prove"
Hold the word predict at its own strength. In the authors' statistics it's a prediction in a survey, not a proof of cause. Chapter twelve explains what it means.

@chapter Two invented teams
@math heading="Preset alder, 7-point scale" items="respondent one: identity 6 + 6 + 5 = 17@six, six, five;;identity: 63/12 = 5.25@five point two five;;satisfaction: 60/12 = 5@sixty over twelve"
The lab uses six invented items, three for identity and three for satisfaction. None are from the book. In the preset alder, the first respondent answers six, six, five on identity, a sum of seventeen. Over four respondents, identity is sixty three over twelve answers, five point two five. Satisfaction is sixty over twelve, which is five.

@art two-columns
Now birch, a second invented team of four.

@math heading="Preset birch" items="satisfaction: 60/12 = 5@also add to sixty;;identity: 39/12 = 3.25@three point two five"
Birch's satisfaction answers also add to sixty, a mean of five. But its identity answers add to thirty nine, a mean of three point two five. Two teams, equally satisfied, and one identifies much less. That's why the chapter treats them as two constructs.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating them as one measure@one measure|Reading predicts as causes@causes|Satisfaction as liking the work@liking"
Three slips. Treating identity and satisfaction as one measure. Reading predicts as causes. And thinking satisfaction is only about liking the work, when it includes your tools and the use of your skills.
