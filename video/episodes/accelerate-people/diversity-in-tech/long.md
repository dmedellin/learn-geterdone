@chapter Position and limit
@title text="Diversity in Tech" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Chapter ten of Accelerate closes with diversity, and it opens with a position and a limit. The position: diversity matters. The limit: in the authors' own data, few teams are diverse.

@quote text="diversity is not enough. Teams and organizations must also be inclusive." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 10"
They recommend that teams hire and keep more women and members of underrepresented groups, and work on other kinds of diversity too, like including people with disabilities. Then they add: diversity is not enough. Teams and organizations must also be inclusive.

@chapter Whose findings
@art cited-shelf
Notice whose findings the benefits are. The research that diverse teams are smarter and perform better comes from others: Rock and Grant, Deloitte, Hunt and colleagues, and Woolley and Malone on collective intelligence. Accelerate's own survey doesn't report a test of them. What it contributes is a description of who answered.

@chapter Who answered
@math heading="Respondents who identified as female" items="2015: 5%@five percent;;2016: 6%@six percent in;;2017: 6.5%@six point five"
On gender, the authors began asking in twenty fifteen. Of all respondents, five percent identified as female that year, six percent in twenty sixteen, and six point five percent in twenty seventeen. They say that was much lower than they expected.

@list heading="Respondents reporting a team with" items="no women: 33%@thirty three|under 10% women: 56%@fifty six|under 25% women: 81%@eighty one"
Among respondents, thirty three percent reported a team with no women on it, fifty six percent a team under ten percent female, and eighty one percent a team under twenty five percent female. The passage doesn't name a year for these three.

@art team-grid
So most respondents work on teams with few women, or none. The authors say they started with binary gender to compare with existing research, and hope to extend their work to non-binary gender.

@math heading="2017: do you identify as underrepresented?" items="no: 77%@seventy seven;;yes: 12%@twelve percent;;prefer not to say or not applicable: 11%@eleven percent"
In twenty seventeen they asked whether respondents identify as underrepresented. Seventy seven percent said no, twelve percent yes, and eleven percent preferred not to respond or said not applicable.

@art globe-dots
The data came from around the world, so they couldn't ask about particular groups. The United States defines several minorities that wouldn't make sense elsewhere. And they say they haven't yet extended the research to people with disabilities.

@chapter What others report
@art leaving-door
From other research, the authors report that women in leadership is linked with higher financial performance, that there's no significant difference between men and women in STEM ability, and that women are leaving tech at a forty five percent higher rate than men. They also cite reports of harassment, microaggressions and unequal pay. That's their reading of other people's studies.

@chapter A share of respondents
@art two-funnels
Now a caution. Six point five percent of respondents is a share of the people who answered, not a measured share of the industry. The invitations went out through mailing lists and referrals, a snowball sample, and how a survey is distributed changes who answers. The book doesn't say which way its share differs from the field's.

@math heading="Preset first-wave: an invented region of 1,000" items="Group A: 240/800 = 30% answered@two hundred and forty;;Group B: 30/200 = 15% answered@thirty of two hundred;;ratio 2, gap 15 points@a ratio of two;;B in the sample: 30/270 = 11.1%@eleven point one;;B in the field: 200/1000 = 20%@twenty percent of the field"
The lab shows the mechanism on invented numbers. Two hundred and forty of Group A's eight hundred answer: thirty percent. Thirty of two hundred in Group B answer: fifteen percent. That's a ratio of two and a gap of fifteen points. So Group B is eleven point one percent of the sample, against twenty percent of the field.

@art reach-out
In the second wave, an invitation goes out through a list Group B uses, and sixty of its two hundred answer. Both groups now answer at thirty percent, and Group B is sixty of three hundred answers: twenty percent, the same as the field. The shares matched only because the answering rates did.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Respondents' share as the industry's share@industry|Benefits shown in the book's own data@own data|Diversity without inclusion@inclusion"
Three slips. Reading six point five percent of respondents as the share of women in the industry. Assuming the book shows the benefits of diversity in its own data. And treating diversity alone as the goal, without inclusion.
