@chapter Give power away
@title text="The Role of Managers" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 11" art=thumb
Chapter eleven of Accelerate ends with managers: the people answerable for staff, and often for budgets and resources. They tie the business's aims to what the team does. And at best, a manager is also a leader.

@quote text="Knowledge is power, and you should give power to those who have the knowledge." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 11"
The authors close their list of what managers can do with one sentence that explains the whole list. Knowledge is power, and you should give power to those who have the knowledge.

@art pass-the-key
Managers hold the budgets and the time. The authors want them to hand authority to the people closest to the work.

@chapter What a manager can do
@list heading="What managers can do" items="Make it safe@safe|Invest in people's capabilities@capabilities|Remove obstacles@obstacles|Make deployments less painful@less painful|Make metrics visible and aligned@visible|Delegate authority@delegate"
They can create an environment where people feel safe, invest in their people's capabilities, and remove obstacles to work. They can make deployments less painful, make performance metrics visible and align them with the organization's aims, and delegate more authority to employees.

@art obstacle-cleared
Keep removing obstacles in mind. It comes back later, in the lab.

@words text="Investment in DevOps ↔ delivery performance" sub="a high correlation"
They can also visibly invest in DevOps and in professional development. The authors report a high correlation between investment in DevOps and delivery performance.

@art learning-time
The chapter makes the investment concrete: a training budget people know about, with time in the working day to use it. A technical conference once a year, reported back to the team. Hack days, yak days for technical debt, and protected time to experiment, like twenty percent time.

@chapter Three things for culture
@list heading="Tips for a strong team culture" items="Collaboration across functions@collaboration|A climate for learning@learning|Tools@tools"
The tips gather around three things the authors tie closely to delivery performance and strong culture: collaboration across functions, a climate for learning, and tools.

@art game-day
For collaboration: build trust with other teams over time, and reward work that eases collaboration. A box describes game days, which Kripa Krishnan runs at Google: planned, simulated outages, so engineers who don't usually work together learn to before a real disaster. For learning, they add blameless postmortems. For tools, let practitioners choose their own, and make monitoring a priority.

@words text="Advice, not survey figures" sub="tool choice: a predictor of continuous delivery"
Hold those at their exact strength. Tool choice was a predictor of continuous delivery, and having the tools you need contributes to job satisfaction. But the conference and hack day lists are advice, with no survey figure behind any one of them.

@chapter A number that sounds like a finding
@art two-groups
Now the lab, on forty invented teams. Twenty have a learning budget, and twenty don't.

@math heading="Preset budget" items="with a budget: 13/20 = 65%@thirteen of the budget;;without: 7/20 = 35%@seven met it;;ratio 13/7 = 1.86@one point eight six times;;gap: 30 points · odds ratio: 3.45@three point four five"
Thirteen of the budget teams met their goal for the quarter: sixty five percent. Without a budget, seven met it: thirty five percent. So budget teams were one point eight six times as likely to meet it. The gap is thirty points, and the odds ratio, three point four five, is a different number. Only the ratio of shares means times as likely.

@chapter A third factor
@art split-groups
Now split the same forty by one more fact: does the team's manager also remove obstacles?

@math heading="Preset by-obstacles" items="budget, obstacles removed: 12/16 = 75%@twelve of sixteen;;no budget, obstacles removed: 3/4 = 75%@three of four;;budget, not removed: 1/4 = 25%@one of four;;neither: 4/16 = 25%@four of sixteen;;ratio within each level = 1@ratio is one"
Budget teams with obstacles removed: twelve of sixteen, seventy five percent. No budget, obstacles removed: three of four, also seventy five. Budget without: one of four, twenty five. Neither: four of sixteen, also twenty five. Within each level the ratio is one. The one point eight six came from the budget teams being mostly the ones whose managers remove obstacles.

@words text="Invented data, a narrow point"
That's invented data, not a claim about real teams, and the chapter doesn't say budgets are worthless. The point is narrower: a ratio describes the teams in front of you, and a third factor can stand behind it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="1.86 times as the budget's effect@effect|A budget with no time to use it@no time|Ratio, gap and odds ratio mixed up@mixed up"
Three slips. Reading one point eight six times as the effect of the budget. Funding training and leaving no time to use it. And getting the ratio, the gap and the odds ratio mixed up.

@chapter Try it

Your turn. Of thirty invented teams, fifteen make their metrics visible, and nine met their goal. Fifteen don't, and three met it. Find each share, the ratio and the gap. Then find the odds ratio, and say why it differs.
