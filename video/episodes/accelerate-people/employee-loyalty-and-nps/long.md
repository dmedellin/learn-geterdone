@chapter One question
@title text="Employee Loyalty and the Net Promoter Score" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Would you tell a friend to work here? Chapter ten of Accelerate borrows that one question from customer loyalty and asks it of employees.

@quote text="NPS is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 10"
The chapter sums up the arithmetic itself. NPS is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters. That's all of it, but it has an edge.

@art megaphone
Why borrow from customers? The chapter credits promoters with spending more, costing less to win and keep, and talking the company up. It opens with the claim that engaged, satisfied employees are loyal, less prone to burnout, and help drive profitability, productivity and market share. The authors cite earlier work that found customer NPS correlated with company growth in many industries.

@chapter Three bands
@art three-bands
Answers run from zero to ten, and three bands do the work. Nine or ten is a promoter. Seven or eight is a passive: satisfied, but much less enthusiastic. And anything from zero to six is a detractor.

@list heading="Two versions for employees" items="The organization as a place to work@the organization|The team as a place to work@the team"
For employees the authors asked two versions: one about the organization as a place to work, and one about the team.

@chapter The finding
@art two-crowds
The authors compared the share of promoters among people on high performing teams with the share on low performing teams.

@math heading="Promoters were more likely on high performers" items="organization question: 2.2× as likely@two point two;;team question: 1.8× as likely@one point eight"
Promoters were two point two times as likely on high performing teams for the organization question, and one point eight times as likely for the team question. Notice what's compared: the proportion of promoters, not the score itself. The Preface says the employee questions were added in the survey of twenty sixteen, and the chapter gives no year for this comparison, so neither do we.

@list heading="Significantly correlated with the score" items="Customer feedback shaping products@customer feedback|Seeing work flow from idea to customer@idea to customer|Identifying with the organization's values and goals@values and goals"
The employee score was also significantly correlated with three things: gathering customer feedback and letting it shape products, teams seeing their work travel from idea to customer, and employees identifying with the organization's values and goals. That's a correlation, read at the strength the chapter gives it.

@chapter The arithmetic
@art ten-answers
Now the lab. Ten invented answers to one question: ten, nine, nine, nine, eight, eight, seven, six, five, three.

@math heading="The preset team" items="promoters: 10, 9, 9, 9 = 4 of 10 = 40%@forty percent are promoters;;passives: 8, 8, 7 = 30%@thirty percent are passive;;detractors: 6, 5, 3 = 30%@detractors;;score = 40 − 30 = 10@forty minus thirty"
Four answers are nine or ten, so forty percent are promoters. Three are seven or eight, so thirty percent are passive. Three are six or below, so thirty percent are detractors. The score is forty minus thirty, which is ten. The passives count in the total, but not in the subtraction.

@chapter Crossing an edge
@art edge-step
Now move one answer. Someone who said eight says nine instead.

@math heading="One answer moves" items="8 → 9: promoters 50%, score 50 − 30 = 20@fifty minus thirty;;8 → 7: still passive, score stays 10@stays at ten"
The passive becomes a promoter, so five of ten promote, and the score is fifty minus thirty, twenty. Ten points, from one person. But if someone moves from eight to seven, both are passive, and the score stays at ten. An answer matters only when it crosses an edge.

@art empty-cloud
And the score can't tell you why. Nothing in the subtraction says whether the detractors are unhappy with the work, the pay or a manager.

@words text="1 person = 10 points" sub="on a team of ten"
A team of ten also has a coarse scale: every respondent is worth ten points. The authors' comparison rests on thousands of respondents, and it's an association in their survey, between high performing teams and a greater likelihood of promoters.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Leaving passives out of the total@passives out|Reading the score as the mean answer@mean answer|Hearing 2.2 times as a higher score@higher score"
Three slips. Leaving the passives out of the total, which overstates both percentages. Reading the score as the mean answer, when it counts people in bands. And hearing two point two times as a higher score, when it's a ratio of proportions of promoters.
