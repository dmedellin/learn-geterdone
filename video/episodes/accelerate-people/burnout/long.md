@chapter The argument
@title text="Burnout" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 9" art=thumb
When someone on a team burns out, what does a manager usually do? Chapter nine of Accelerate says they reach for the wrong thing.

@quote text="Technology managers, like so many other well-meaning managers, often try to fix the person while ignoring the work environment" cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Chapter 9"
In the authors' words: technology managers, like so many other well-meaning managers, often try to fix the person while ignoring the work environment. That one sentence carries the whole section.

@chapter What burnout is
@art faded-flame
Burnout is more than being overworked. It can make what you once loved about work seem dull, and it often shows as helplessness. Its symptoms are feeling exhausted, cynical or ineffective, little sense of accomplishment, and work spilling into the rest of your life.

@words text="$300 billion a year" sub="Maslach, 2014, as the book reports it"
The chapter cites research comparing the health cost of stressful jobs to secondhand smoke and obesity, and a cost of three hundred billion dollars a year to the US economy, attributed to Maslach in twenty fourteen. Those are other researchers' figures, not the book's survey. From them the authors draw a duty of care.

@chapter Six risk factors
@list heading="Maslach's six risk factors" items="Work overload@overload|Lack of control@control|Insufficient rewards@rewards|Breakdown of community@community|Absence of fairness@fairness|Value conflicts@values"
The authors rely on Christina Maslach, who pioneered the study of job burnout, and on Leiter and Maslach, twenty oh eight. Six organizational factors predict it: work overload, lack of control over decisions, insufficient rewards, a breakdown of community, an absence of fairness, and a mismatch between your values and the organization's.

@art fix-the-room
All six are things management has the power to change. Yet most organizations work on the individual and leave the setting alone, though Maslach's research shows that changing the setting is likelier to work.

@chapter What went with it
@art three-questions
To measure it, the authors asked three things. Did you feel burned out or exhausted? Detached, cynical or useless? Was the job harming your life outside it? People working with better technical practices and Lean practices felt less burned out.

@list heading="Most strongly linked to burnout" items="Organizational culture@culture|Deployment pain@deployment pain|Effectiveness of leaders@leaders|Investment in DevOps@investment|Organizational performance@organizational performance"
Five factors went with burnout most strongly. Organizational culture first: pathological, power oriented cultures. Then deployment pain, the effectiveness of leaders, investment in DevOps, and organizational performance. The advice follows: blame free environments, fix what hurts most, limit work in process, and give people time to learn.

@chapter Lived values
@art two-circles
When your values and the organization's don't match, burnout is likelier. And the values that count are the ones employees feel in everyday work, not the mission statement on the wall.

@chapter Scoring it
@art flipped-item
Now the score. Some survey items are worded the other way round. Agreeing that you have energy left for life outside work means less burnout. So before averaging, you turn those answers round: on a seven point scale, eight minus the answer.

@math heading="Respondent one, items b3 and b4 reversed" items="answers 6, 5, 2, 2@six, five, two, two;;reversed 6, 5, 8 − 2 = 6, 8 − 2 = 6@eight minus two;;sum 23, mean 23/4 = 5.75@five point seven five;;team mean 26.5/6 = 4.417@four point four one seven"
The first respondent answers six, five, two, two. The last two are reversed, so eight minus two is six, twice. The scores sum to twenty three, a mean of five point seven five. Across six invented respondents, the team mean is four point four one seven, and three are above the lesson's own line of five. The book gives no such line.

@chapter A missed reversal
@math heading="Same answers, nothing reversed" items="respondent one: 15/4 = 3.75@three point seven five;;team: 3.833@three point eight three three;;above the line: none@no one"
Forget the reversal and respondent one falls to three point seven five. The team falls to three point eight three three, and no one is above the line. The most worn out person now looks no different from a contented colleague. That's the wrong message for a manager.

@art score-line
And even scored properly, a line on a chart is only a place to start asking questions. The authors want managers to ask people what's stopping them, and fix that.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Fixing the person@fixing the person|Averaging raw answers@raw|Trusting the printed values@printed"
So watch for three slips: fixing the person instead of the work, averaging raw answers with reversed items left in, and reading the printed values as the ones that count.

@chapter Try it
@words text="A 4, 5, 2 · B 2, 1, 5 · C 3, 4, 3" sub="5-point scale, x3 reversed"
On a five point scale, the third item is reversed, so it's six minus the answer. A answers four, five, two. B answers two, one, five. C answers three, four, three. Find the team mean, then redo it with nothing reversed.
