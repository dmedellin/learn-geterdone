@chapter The question
@title text="Primogeniture, Entails and Tenants" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 2" art=thumb
Suppose a field could be drained for ninety pounds, and every year after it would grow fifteen pounds more. Who would actually pay for it? Smith's answer depends entirely on who holds the land, and on what terms.

@art great-estate
When the German and Scythian nations overran the western Roman empire, the land was engrossed by a few great proprietors. That might have been a passing evil, divided again by inheritance or sale. It wasn't.

@chapter Kept whole
@quote text="The law of primogeniture hindered them from being divided by succession; the introduction of entails prevented their being broke into small parcels by alienation." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 2"
The law of primogeniture hindered them from being divided by succession; the introduction of entails prevented their being broke into small parcels by alienation.

@art one-heir
Primogeniture made sense when land meant power and protection. Every great landlord was a petty prince, and to divide it was to ruin it. But laws frequently continue in force long after the circumstances that made them reasonable are gone. Entails, binding land to a line, Smith calls completely absurd.

@chapter The great proprietor
@art embellished-acres
And it seldom happens that a great proprietor is a great improver. Improving land, like any commercial project, needs exact attention to small savings and small gains. A man born to a great fortune rarely manages it. He embellishes the acres near his house at ten times their worth, and leaves the rest.

@chapter Slaves and metayers
@words text="Acquire no property → eat much, labour little"
Then the people who worked the land. At first, almost all were slaves. Smith's verdict is flat. Slave work, though it appears to cost only maintenance, is in the end the dearest of any. A person who can acquire no property has no interest but to eat as much and labour as little as possible.

@art half-share
Next came the metayer. The landlord supplied seed, cattle and tools, and the produce was split equally. As a freeman, the metayer wanted the whole crop as big as possible. But he'd never improve at his own expense, because the landlord, who laid out nothing, took half of whatever it produced.

@chapter Farmers and leases
@art secure-tenant
Last came farmers who used their own stock and paid a fixed rent. On a lease, they might improve, if they could expect to recover the cost before it expired. In England, Smith says, the tenant's security came to equal the proprietor's. In France a lease held against a buyer for only nine years, lately twenty seven.

@chapter Does it pay?
@math heading="Cost 90 · adds 15 a year · 9 years" items="farmer: 1 · 15 · 9 − 90 = 45@farmer keeps;;metayer: (1/2) · 15 · 9 − 90 = −22.5@metayer keeps;;with tithe: (9/10) · 15 · 9 − 90 = 31.50@tithe"
Now the arithmetic, with a cost and a yield written for the lesson. On a nine year lease, the farmer keeps fifteen a year for nine years, minus ninety. He gains forty five. The metayer keeps half, sixty seven and a half, minus ninety. A loss of twenty two fifty. A farmer paying tithe keeps nine tenths, and gains thirty one fifty.

@math heading="The term that just pays" items="farmer: 90 / 15 = 6 years@the farmer;;metayer: 90 / ((1/2) · 15) = 12 years@the metayer"
How long must each hold the land to break even? Divide the cost by what he keeps each year. For the farmer, ninety over fifteen, six years. For the metayer, ninety over seven and a half, twelve years. Halve the share, and the term doubles.

@art lease-term
So on the same land, with the same improvement and the same term, the farmer improves and the metayer doesn't. Give the metayer twenty seven years and he gains a hundred and twelve fifty. The share, not the land, is what stops him. And the slave, who bears nothing and keeps nothing, gains nothing.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking it's about the land, not the holder@The first|Reading slavery as unprofitable everywhere@The second|Treating the 90 and 15 as Smith's@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the argument is about the land, rather than who holds it. The second is reading Smith as saying slavery was unprofitable everywhere. He said sugar and tobacco could bear its expense, corn couldn't. And the third is treating the ninety and the fifteen as Smith's. Only the years are his.
