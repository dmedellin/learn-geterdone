@chapter The question
@title text="The Rise of Towns" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 3" art=thumb
After Rome fell, the country belonged to great lords in their castles. The towns were full of tradesmen of almost servile condition. Yet the towns got their liberty first. How?

@art castle-and-booths
The townsfolk travelled from fair to fair like pedlars, and paid taxes at manors, bridges and booths, under names like passage, pontage, lastage and stallage. Even their privileges show what they'd lacked: the right to marry off a daughter without the lord's consent, or to leave goods to their children.

@chapter The farm of the town
@art farm-of-the-town
The step that freed them was financial. The king's revenue from a town was let to farm, and the burghers themselves got credit enough to take it, jointly and severally answerable for the whole rent. They paid it by their own bailiff, freed from the insolence of the king's officers.

@words text="In fee · a rent certain, never afterwards to be augmented"
In time the farm was granted in fee, for ever, at a rent certain, never afterwards to be augmented. Because the payment was perpetual, the exemptions became perpetual too. The town became a corporation, with magistrates, a council, bye laws and walls.

@chapter Extraordinary
@words text="The branch of revenue most likely to grow"
Smith stops to say this is odd. It must seem extraordinary that the sovereigns of Europe swapped, for a rent that could never rise, the branch of their revenue most likely to grow on its own, without any expense or attention.

@math heading="A farm of £100, fixed for ever" items="year 1: 100 / 100 = 100%@first year;;year 60: 100 / 200 = 50% · gives up 100@year sixty;;year 100: 100 / 320 = 31.25% · gives up 220@year one hundred"
Here the dues are written for the lesson. The farm is fixed at a hundred pounds, when the dues are worth a hundred. In the first year, that's all of them. By year sixty, the dues are two hundred, and the farm is half. By year one hundred, the dues are three twenty, and the king gives up two twenty a year.

@art growing-dues
Across the six years the lab lists, the fixed farm raises six hundred where the dues would have raised one thousand one hundred and sixty five. And in a declining town, with dues down to sixty, the same fixed hundred is more than all of them. Then the loss falls on the burghers.

@chapter Why the king did it
@art king-and-burghers
So why did he do it? The king couldn't protect the weak from the great lords, and the lords despised the burghers as a parcel of emancipated slaves. Mutual interest disposed them to support the king, and the king to support them against the lords. The kings worst off with their barons, like King John, were the most generous.

@chapter Security first
@art refuge-in-the-walls
Order and good government, with liberty and security, came to the cities while the country was exposed to every sort of violence. People who aren't secure settle for bare subsistence. A villain who saved a little would run to a town, and if he hid from his lord for a year, he was free for ever.

@art cloth-and-corn
Towns on the sea could draw food from the far corners of the world. Venice, Genoa and Pisa grew rich supplying the crusades. Some manufactures came from foreign commerce, like the silk of Lucca. Others grew from farming. A fine cloth holds the price of eighty pounds of wool, and sometimes several thousand weight of corn.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the figures are Smith's@The first|Reading the farm as a gift to the towns@The second|Thinking towns made the country poor@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the figures are Smith's. He gives none. The second is reading the farm as a gift to the towns. It was a bargain, and in a declining town the burghers lose. And the third is thinking Smith says the towns made the country poor. He says the opposite.
