@chapter The question
@title text="The Natural Progress of Opulence" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 1" art=thumb
Why do nations grow rich in different orders? Smith opens Book Three with a picture of what would happen if nothing got in the way. It starts with the oldest trade of all, between the town and the country.

@quote text="of every civilized society is that carried on between the inhabitants of the town and those of the country." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 1"
The great commerce, he says, of every civilized society is that carried on between the inhabitants of the town and those of the country. Rude produce goes one way, manufactures the other.

@chapter Who gains?
@art town-and-country
The town gains its whole subsistence from the country. So you might think the town's gain is the country's loss. Smith denies it. The gains of both are mutual and reciprocal. The country buys more manufactured goods with less of its own labour than it would spend making them, and the town gives it a market for its surplus.

@chapter A farmer and a weaver
@math heading="Working alone · 1,200 hours each" items="farmer: 1,200 / (2 + 6) = 150 of each@farmer needs;;weaver: 1,200 / (6 + 2) = 150 of each@weaver;;together: 300 corn, 300 cloth@Together"
The lesson puts numbers on it, written for the lesson. A farmer and a weaver each have twelve hundred hours. The farmer needs two hours for corn and six for cloth, and the weaver the reverse. Working alone for an equal amount of both, each makes a hundred and fifty of each. Together, three hundred corn and three hundred cloth.

@math heading="Each keeps to his cheaper good" items="farmer on corn: 1,200 / 2 = 600@sticks to corn;;weaver on cloth: 1,200 / 2 = 600@weaver to cloth;;gain: 600 − 300 = 300 of each@gain"
Now the farmer sticks to corn, six hundred units. And the weaver to cloth, six hundred units. That's a gain of three hundred of each. Swap half each way, and each ends the year with three hundred of corn and three hundred of cloth, twice what he made alone.

@art two-stacks
One caution. The lab decides who makes what by relative cost, which is Ricardo's rule from eighteen seventeen, after Smith. In these presets each person is simply better at one good, so Smith's case and Ricardo's agree.

@chapter Land near the town
@art near-and-far
Smith adds a point about location. Corn grown a mile from town sells at the same price as corn from twenty miles off, but the distant corn's price must also pay for its carriage. So the nearby owners gain the whole value of that carriage.

@words text="No one says the country loses by the town"
And he's dry about the moral. Of all the absurd speculations about the balance of trade, nobody ever pretended that the country loses by its commerce with the town, or the town by the country that maintains it.

@chapter Subsistence first
@art surplus-feeds-town
Then the order. Subsistence comes before conveniency and luxury, so the industry that provides it must come first. The town lives on the country's surplus, what's left over after the cultivators are fed. The town can only grow as that surplus grows.

@art capital-order
And natural inclination pushes the same way. Upon equal, or nearly equal profits, most men would rather put their capital in land. It's more under their view and command, while the trader must trust his to the winds and the waves. So capital goes first to agriculture, then to manufactures, and last of all to foreign commerce.

@chapter Europe went the other way
@art inverted-order
Then the twist. In all the modern states of Europe, Smith says, this order has been in many respects entirely inverted. Foreign commerce brought in the finer manufactures, and those together drove the main improvements in farming. Manners and customs left over from the old governments forced this unnatural and retrograde order.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the natural order as a law of history@The first|Thinking the town lives off the country@The second|Taking the lab's rule as Smith's@And the third"
The first slip is reading the natural order as a law of history. It's a standard to measure Europe against. The second is thinking the town lives off the country at its expense. And the third is taking the lab's relative cost rule as Smith's own.
