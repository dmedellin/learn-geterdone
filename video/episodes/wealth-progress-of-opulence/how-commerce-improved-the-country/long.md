@chapter The question
@title text="How Commerce Improved the Country" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 4" art=thumb
The feudal lords had armies of followers, and the law couldn't break their power. Then, Smith says, something far smaller did. It was the chance to buy a pair of diamond buckles.

@art three-effects
Smith says the commerce of towns improved the country in three ways. First, it gave the country a great and ready market for its produce. Second, merchants bought land, and they're generally the best of all improvers. A merchant is commonly a bold, a country gentleman a timid undertaker.

@chapter The third effect
@words text="Order, good government, liberty and security"
The third effect is the one he cares about. Commerce and manufactures gradually brought order and good government, and with them liberty and security, to people who had lived in a continual state of war, and of servile dependency on their superiors. This, though the least observed, is by far the most important. Hume, he says, is the only writer who'd noticed it.

@chapter The lord's table
@art great-hall
Here's how. With no foreign commerce and no fine manufactures, a great proprietor has nothing to swap his surplus for. So he consumes the whole in rustic hospitality at home. Westminster Hall was the dining room of William Rufus. The Earl of Warwick was said to feed thirty thousand people a day at his manors.

@art retainers-in-line
The people he fed had nothing to give back, so they had to obey him, for the same reason soldiers must obey the prince who pays them. That dependence was the barons' power. Cameron of Lochiel had a rent of no more than five hundred pounds, yet he took eight hundred of his own people into the rebellion of seventeen forty five.

@chapter Something to buy
@quote text="All for ourselves, and nothing for other people, seems, in every age of the world, to have been the vile maxim of the masters of mankind." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter 4"
Commerce changed that by giving the lords something to buy, which they could consume themselves, without sharing. All for ourselves, and nothing for other people, seems, in every age of the world, to have been the vile maxim of the masters of mankind.

@art diamond-buckles
So for a pair of diamond buckles, perhaps, or something as frivolous, they gave up the maintenance of a thousand men for a year, and with it the whole weight and authority it gave them. The buckles were all their own.

@chapter Shares of maintenance
@math heading="£10,000 a year · £10 a person" items="old way: 10,000 / 1,000 / 10 = 1@old way;;broadcloth: 4,000 / 4,000 / 10 = 1/10@broadcloth;;jewels and buckles: 1,000 / 10,000 / 10 = 1/100@jewels"
A man of ten thousand a year could once keep perhaps a thousand families, so ten pounds a family. In the old way, each retainer's share is one: wholly the lord's. In a preset written for the lesson, four thousand on broadcloth from four thousand makers is a tenth each. A thousand on jewels from ten thousand makers is a hundredth.

@art spread-thin
Across four trades, the same ten thousand pounds keeps thirty thousand people in part, and nobody wholly. Each of them can be maintained without him. That's Smith's point. They are all more or less independent of him.

@chapter An unintended revolution
@art long-lease
Retainers were dismissed, farms enlarged, and lords wanting more rent had to grant long leases, which made tenants independent. The lords sold their birthright, not like Esau in hunger, but in the wantonness of plenty, for trinkets and baubles. Regular government came to the country as to the city.

@words text="Two orders who had not the least intention to serve the public"
A revolution of the greatest importance was brought about by two orders of people who had not the least intention to serve the public. The proprietors acted from childish vanity, the merchants from their pedlar principle of turning a penny wherever a penny was to be got.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading it as praise of vanity@The first|Taking the lab's trades as history@The second|Treating independence as equality@And the third"
The first slip is reading the chapter as praise of vanity. Smith calls it childish. The second is taking the lab's trades as history. Only the ten thousand pounds and the thousand families are his. And the third is treating independence as equality. The lord kept his wealth, and lost only his power to command.
