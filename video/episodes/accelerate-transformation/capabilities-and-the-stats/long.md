@chapter Two reference appendices
@title text="Capabilities to Drive Improvement, and the Stats" sub="Accelerate, Appendices A and B" art=thumb
Accelerate ends with two references. Appendix A lists the capabilities. Appendix B lists the statistical findings. And Appendix B opens with a reminder to the reader.

@quote text="Correlation looks at how closely two variables move together (or don’t) but it doesn’t tell us if one variable’s movement predicts or causes the movement in another variable." cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Appendix B"
Correlation looks at how closely two variables move together, or don't, but it doesn't tell us if one variable's movement predicts or causes the movement in another variable.

@chapter Twenty four in five
@list heading="Appendix A: 24 capabilities" items="Continuous delivery: 8 (Ch. 4)@Continuous delivery has eight|Architecture: 2 (Ch. 5)@Architecture has two|Product and process: 4 (Ch. 8)@Product and process|Lean management and monitoring: 5 (Ch. 7, 13)@Lean management|Cultural: 5 (Ch. 3, 5, 10, 11)@cultural"
Appendix A lists twenty four capabilities the analysis tied to better delivery performance, in five categories, in no particular order. Continuous delivery has eight, from chapter four. Architecture has two: loosely coupled architecture and empowered teams. Product and process has four. Lean management and monitoring has five. And cultural has five.

@art handy-list
The authors call it a handy list, and the Quick Reference at the front is its short form. Some entries carry qualifications that are easy to lose: peer review does better than external change approval boards, and a team should keep its test data to the least it needs.

@chapter Read the verbs
@list heading="Appendix B's verbs" items="Is correlated with: they move together@correlated|Predicts: one construct affects another@predicts|Drives, affects, impacts: words for predicts@drives"
Appendix B states the findings by category, and it defines its verbs first. Is correlated with means two things move together. Predicts, which the authors call inferential prediction, is stronger: one construct affects another, tested with regression where an experiment can't be run. And chapter two says drives, affects and impacts are other words for predicts. It's still not a randomized test.

@art verb-scale
So read the verb on each line: correlation first, then prediction, and beyond both, the causal test the book didn't run.

@chapter Three kinds of line
@art across-years
Some lines state a result across years. High performers were twice as likely as low performers to exceed organizational performance goals.

@words text="+50% market cap growth over three years" sub="2014 follow-up · just over 1,000 respondents · 355 companies"
Some state one year and sample, and say so. A follow-up to the twenty fourteen data used just over a thousand respondents in three hundred and fifty five companies. At publicly traded ones, high performers' market capitalization had grown fifty percent more over three years. Later years couldn't repeat it; the data were too few.

@math heading="Who answered: women among respondents" items="2015: 5%@five percent;;2016: 6%@then six;;2017: 6.5%@six point five"
And some describe who answered. Women were five percent of respondents in twenty fifteen, then six percent, then six point five percent in twenty seventeen. That's a fact to weigh when Fowler's foreword wonders how the sample reflects IT.

@art candid-notes
The appendix is also candid. Teams with the strongest transformational leaders were no more likely, and perhaps less, to be high performers. And the delivery performance construct uses three of the four measures, with change fail rate highly correlated and left out. Reasons not to read the capabilities as a recipe.

@chapter Six teams
@art six-profiles
Now the lab, using Appendix A as its frame. Six teams of one invented organization each list the capabilities they have. They hold eighteen, fourteen, eight, thirteen, seven and eleven of the twenty four.

@math heading="Preset six-teams, summed by hand" items="continuous delivery: 26 of 48@twenty six of forty eight;;architecture: 5 of 12 = 41.7%@five of twelve;;product and process: 10 of 24@ten of twenty four;;lean management: 11 of 30 = 36.7%@eleven of thirty;;cultural: 19 of 30@nineteen of thirty"
Sum each category by hand and divide by its size times six. Continuous delivery: twenty six of forty eight. Architecture: five of twelve. Product and process: ten of twenty four. Lean management and monitoring: eleven of thirty, the lowest at thirty six point seven percent. Cultural: nineteen of thirty. And team experimentation is in place in none of the six.

@chapter Same total, two shapes
@art same-total
Now two teams with thirteen each. Orchid is spread across every category. Elm is lopsided: six of eight in continuous delivery and four of five in culture, but one of four in product and process and one of five in lean management. Same total, two pictures. That's why Appendix A sorts the capabilities and doesn't rank them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Every line is a cause@cause|A capability total is a maturity score@maturity|A figure travels beyond its year and sample@year"
Three slips. Reading every line of Appendix B as a cause. Reading a capability total as a maturity score. And carrying a figure out of the year and sample that produced it.
