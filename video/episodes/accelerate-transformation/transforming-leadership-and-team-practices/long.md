@chapter The better question
@title text="Transforming Your Leadership, Management, and Team Practices" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 16" art=thumb
Leaders often ask the Bells, how do we change our culture? The second half of their chapter sets that question aside, and arrives at a warning.

@quote text="there is no checklist or playbook. You can’t “implement” culture change." cite="Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell, Accelerate, Chapter 16"
In their words: there is no checklist or playbook. You can't implement culture change.

@list heading="Better questions" items="How do we learn to learn?@learn to learn|How can learning be made safe for others?@safe|How do new behaviours become habits?@habits"
Instead, they prefer better questions. How do we learn to learn? How can learning be made safe for others? And how do new behaviours turn into habits? At ING, it began with one leader asking himself those questions.

@chapter A leader who learned first
@art stretch-steps
That leader hired coaches to challenge everyone, himself first. His managers met each quarter to learn, and spent the months between making it habit. The Bells' picture is of stretching, and stretching again, just as the team felt comfortable.

@art time-log
The first exercise was concrete. The managers were challenged to develop leader standard work: showing work visually, short meetings at a board, and steady coaching, in place of long meetings and firefighting. To do it, they first logged where their days went, for several weeks, then shared what they found and designed new ways to work.

@words text="A tenth of their time, freed" sub="one management team's report"
Three months later, one said he'd never go back. And the managers reached a goal they'd set: a tenth of their time available for what they chose. That's one team's report in the Bells' account. The first step was a measurement of themselves.

@chapter Routines first
@quote text="when you change the way you work, you change the routines, you create a different culture" cite="a tribe lead, quoted by Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell, Accelerate, Chapter 16"
Trying to imitate another company's specific practices, the Bells say, is contrary to the essence of a generative culture. A tribe lead they quote puts the order plainly: when you change the way you work, you change the routines, you create a different culture.

@art starred-table
The chapter ends its argument with a table of team, management and leadership practices. The Bells mark with an asterisk the ones research has found to go with high performance. The rest aren't ones the research has shown. And they say it isn't a checklist but a distillation, to develop your own.

@list heading="Make it your own" items="Learn from others without copying@copying|Don't contract the change out@contract|Grow your own coaches@coaches"
Their closing advice is from experience. Keep the right mindset: learning, not the practices, and certainly not the tools. Learn from other enterprises without copying them. Don't contract the change out to a large consulting firm, though outside coaching may help at first. Grow your own coaches. Lead by example, with discipline and patience.

@chapter What the research adds
@art leader-and-team
The research supports part of this. Appendix A lists transformational leadership as a capability. But chapter eleven also reports that teams with the top tenth of reported leadership were no more likely, and perhaps less, to be high performers. In the Bells' story, too, a leader starts the change, and a management team, coaches and squads carry it.

@chapter Counting a quarter
@math heading="Orchid, an invented team" items="start: 4 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 9 of 24@nine of the twenty four;;added: visible flow, WIP limits, visual displays, learning culture@adds;;end: 4 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 13 of 24@thirteen"
The lab counts Appendix A capabilities by category. Orchid starts with nine of the twenty four. Over a quarter it adds visible flow of work, limits on work in process, visual displays and a learning culture. It ends with thirteen. Three categories changed; continuous delivery and architecture didn't.

@art tally-not-culture
Read that count as a place to ask a question. Why those four, and how did the team come to them? The count is of what a team reports it has. It doesn't show routines changed, let alone culture. And treating the list as a score to raise is exactly the checklist thinking the chapter warns against.

@chapter Where people slip
@art no-playbook
There's no playbook to follow, and that may be the hardest part to accept.

@list heading="Common slips" items="The table as a checklist@checklist|Hire a firm to implement it@firm|A capability count as a culture@count"
Three slips. Treating the table of practices as a checklist. Hiring a firm to implement the new way of working. And reading a quarter's capability count as a change of culture.
