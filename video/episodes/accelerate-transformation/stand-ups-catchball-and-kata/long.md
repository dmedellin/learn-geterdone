@chapter The stand-up
@title text="Stand-ups, Catchball and Improvement Kata" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 16" art=thumb
The Bells' tour of an ING squad reaches its daily stand-up. What happens there, and what happens to a problem someone raises, is the subject of this lesson.

@art standup-board
The squad stands in front of its board of demand and work in process. Each member says in a few words what they're working on, what's in the way, and what's finished, and the board is updated as they speak. It runs about fifteen minutes. The Bells report it has cut time in meetings, though they give no measurement.

@words text="Problems are not solved at the stand-up"
And here's what the meeting is not for. Problems are not solved at the stand-up. A problem goes to whoever can help, climbs a level when it must, and stays on the board until the answer comes back down.

@chapter Catchball
@art catchball
The Bells call this pattern of vertical and horizontal communication catchball. The same channels carry learning among squads, chapters and tribes. The tribe lead, Jannes Smit, learns from them too, including what squad members hear from customers, and he adapts his goals.

@quote text="a continuous, rapid feedback cycle of learning, testing, validating, and adjusting" cite="Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell, Accelerate, Chapter 16"
They describe the whole exchange as a continuous, rapid feedback cycle of learning, testing, validating, and adjusting. Lean practitioners call it strategy deployment, or Hoshin Kanri, and it's also known as PDCA: plan, do, check, act.

@chapter The leader's part
@list heading="Everyone's job" items="Do the work@Do the work|Improve the work@improve the work|Develop the people@develop the people"
Smit visits the squads and asks what they're learning and how he can support them, instead of asking why something isn't getting done. That takes coaching and modelling. The job, for leaders and everyone, is threefold: do the work, improve the work, and develop the people.

@art slow-down
A fixed share of each squad's time is set aside for improvement, though the chapter doesn't say how much. And when the squads were asked to be twice as effective, Smit told them that if quality wasn't there, they shouldn't release. He'd cover for them. The Bells call it slowing down to speed up.

@art blend-loop
On problem solving, one chapter tried A three, Kata, Lean startup and others, then settled on a blend of its own. The loop: study the present situation, test an idea against a measure, and make what works the standard.

@chapter A current condition
@math heading="Preset current: an invented fortnight" items="8 deployments, 4 a week@eight deployments;;median lead time: (54 + 68) ÷ 2 = 61 h@sixty one hours;;change fail rate: 2 ÷ 8 = 25%@twenty five percent;;median restore: (9 + 15) ÷ 2 = 12 h@twelve"
The chapter doesn't say what ING measured, so the lab borrows the book's four delivery measures from chapter two. An invented team's fortnight has eight deployments, four a week. Sorting the lead times, the middle two are fifty four and sixty eight, a median of sixty one hours. Two of eight failed, twenty five percent. And the median restore is twelve hours.

@list heading="Invented targets" items="Median lead time under one day@under one day|Fail rate 15% or less@fifteen percent|At least 7 deployments a week@seven|Median restore under an hour@under an hour"
That's a current condition. Now the team states four targets of its own: a median lead time under one day, a fail rate of fifteen percent or less, at least seven deployments a week, and a median restore under an hour.

@math heading="Preset next fortnight" items="lead time 19 h: met@nineteen hours;;fail rate 1 ÷ 10 = 10%: met@ten percent;;5 a week: not met@five deployments;;restore 3 h: not met@three hours"
The next fortnight: a median lead time of nineteen hours, met. One failed change of ten, ten percent, met. Five deployments a week, not met. One incident, restored in three hours, not met. All four moved the right way, and two crossed their line.

@art four-gauges
Note too that the book's six answer options are coarse. Deployment frequency falls in the same option in both fortnights, between once a day and once a week, though the count rose from four to five a week. A target on the count sees what the option can't.

@art small-sample
Read it as something to learn from. With ten changes, one more failure would make twenty percent and lose a target. The lab can't say whether the stand-up caused the better fortnight, and the Bells don't claim it did.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Solving problems at the stand-up@Solving|The four measures are ING's@ING's|One fortnight is a trend@trend"
Three slips. Solving problems at the stand-up, which stops it being fifteen minutes. Taking the four measures to be ING's, when they're the survey's. And reading one fortnight as a trend.
