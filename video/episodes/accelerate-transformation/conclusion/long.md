@chapter The last advice
@title text="Conclusion" sub="Accelerate" art=thumb
Accelerate ends with three pages from its three authors, a look back over years of surveying people in technology, and one instruction.

@quote text="you can’t buy or copy high performance" cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Conclusion"
Here it is. You can't buy or copy high performance.

@art own-path
A team has to develop its own capabilities, along a path that fits its context.

@art software-everywhere
The one point they say held every time: delivery performance matters to nearly any business, since nearly any business runs on software. And many things affect it: leadership, tooling, automation, and a culture that keeps learning.

@chapter Looking back
@list heading="The book's three parts" items="Part I: what they found@Part one|Part II: the science behind it@Part two|Part III: one view of the capabilities in practice@Part three"
Part one set out what they found, starting from the case that delivery performance feeds organizational performance, commercial and noncommercial. Part two summarized the science: the design and the analysis. And part three is the Bells' chapter, one view of what following the capabilities looks like.

@art long-road
The ask is to leaders and teams alike: improve in technology and business process, in how work feels, and in how improvement is run. It takes lasting effort and investment over time. What the research gives you is a list of capabilities worth developing, not a method of installing them.

@chapter Unequivocal
@quote text="our research is unequivocal" cite="Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate, Conclusion"
Then comes a sentence worth looking at twice. Our research is unequivocal, the authors write, and the results are worth it. Read it fairly, which means reading it with the rest of the book.

@list heading="The limits the book states" items="Fowler: perceptions, a sample, confirm by other methods@foreword|Chapter 12: inferential prediction, no causal analysis@inferential prediction|Chapter 15: a snowball sample@snowball"
Martin Fowler's foreword cautions that the data capture perceptions, the sample may not reflect all of IT, and he'd be more confident once other teams using other approaches confirm it. Chapter twelve says impact claims used inferential prediction, and causal analysis wasn't done. Chapter fifteen names the limits of a snowball sample.

@art three-limits
So the fairest reading of unequivocal is that the survey's results were consistent over its years, on the relationships it tested. It isn't a claim of proven cause, and the authors' own account of their method doesn't make one.

@chapter Twice as likely
@art two-shares
One finding the summary rests on is easy to misread. Chapter two reports that across several years, high performers were twice as likely as low performers to exceed their organizations' goals for profitability, productivity and market share, as respondents rated them. Twice as likely compares two shares.

@math heading="Preset twice: three groups of nine" items="high: 6 of 9 = 66.7%@six of the high group;;low: 3 of 9 = 33.3%@three of the low;;ratio of shares: 2 · gap: 33.3 points@ratio of shares of two;;odds ratio: (6/3) ÷ (3/6) = 4@odds ratio"
On an invented sample of twenty seven teams, six of the high group met their goals, sixty six point seven percent. Three of the low group did, thirty three point three. That's a ratio of shares of two, and a gap of thirty three point three points. The lab also prints the odds ratio, four. Twice as likely is the two, not the four.

@math heading="Preset uneven" items="high: 6 of 8 = 75%@six of eight;;low: 5 of 10 = 50%@five of ten;;ratio 1.50 · odds ratio 3@one point five"
Now groups of different sizes. Six of eight high group teams met their goals, seventy five percent. Five of ten low group teams did, fifty. The ratio is one point five, and the odds ratio three. Same direction, different size. Twice as likely is a number about the sample that produced it.

@art arrow-unknown
And a two row table can't say which way the relationship runs, or that raising delivery performance would lift the share meeting goals. It rests on respondents' own ratings, in groups clustered from the same survey. And it says nothing about your team. Your own log will give your own figures.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Unequivocal as proof of cause@proof|Twice as likely as twice the odds@twice the odds|Copy the high performers@copy"
Three slips. Reading unequivocal as proof of cause. Taking twice as likely for twice the odds. And reading the conclusion as, copy the high performers. The authors say the opposite.
