@chapter The question
@title text="A High-Performing Management Framework in Practice" sub="Accelerate, Chapter 16" art=thumb
Part three of Accelerate is written by guests: Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell of Lean IT Strategies. They open chapter sixteen by asking what a management framework looks like in practice, rather than in theory.

@quote text="a lightweight, high-performance management framework that connects enterprise strategy with action, streamlines the flow of ideas to value, facilitates rapid feedback and learning" cite="Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell, Accelerate, Chapter 16"
They ask for a lightweight, high-performance management framework that connects enterprise strategy with action, streamlines the flow of ideas to value, facilitates rapid feedback and learning. Peak technical performance, they argue, is necessary but not sufficient.

@chapter A case, not a finding
@art case-not-chart
Read the chapter for what it is. Parts one and two report what a survey found. Chapter sixteen is the Bells' account of repeated visits to ING Netherlands, a global bank, and the staff's words are personal communications. It's good evidence of what one organization does, and no evidence of how often it works elsewhere.

@chapter The leadership Obeya
@art obeya-room
The visit begins with Jannes Smit, an IT manager who began experimenting seven years earlier with ways to make his teams learn together. His warning: you have to understand why, and not just copy the behaviours. His leadership Obeya is a glass walled room where his work, priorities and actions are shown to anyone passing.

@list heading="The Obeya's four zones" items="Strategic improvement@strategic improvement|Performance monitoring@performance monitoring|Portfolio roadmap@portfolio roadmap|Leadership actions@leadership actions"
It has four zones: strategic improvement, performance monitoring, the portfolio roadmap, and leadership actions. Red and green make problems plain, and each IT objective is tied, in measurable terms, to the enterprise's strategy.

@chapter Tribes, squads and chapters
@art tribes-squads
Around the room is an organization of a particular kind. Each line of business is a tribe. A tribe holds squads: small, self steering teams, each with a product owner and a customer mission.

@art two-pizzas
Squads are kept small by the two pizza rule. Most squads mix engineers and marketers, which ING calls BizDevOps. Chapters group people of one discipline across squads.

@words text="No fixed structure"
And the Bells say the model has no fixed structure. A new bridging role, the product area lead, emerged from experience. Tribes and squads are a record of what ING's learning produced, not a design to adopt.

@chapter The squad's board
@art squad-wall
The squad's own Obeya is what your team could borrow most readily. Its walls show what the squad is aiming at and how far it is from that, problems that are new or passed up, the demand on the squad, and the work in process and work done. Showing demand, the Bells say, keeps work in process small.

@words text="WIP limits · visual displays · visible flow" sub="capabilities in Appendix A"
The research has something to say about two of these walls. Appendix A lists limits on work in process, visual displays, and making the flow of work visible as capabilities. Chapter sixteen claims no measurement of its own for ING. It shows what those capabilities look like when people live with them.

@chapter Numbers on a board
@math heading="Preset board: eight items over ten days" items="item-days: 6 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 7 + 5 + 5 + 4 = 40@forty item days;;average WIP: 40 ÷ 10 = 4@forty over ten;;throughput: 8 ÷ 10 = 4/5 a day@four fifths;;lead time: 40 ÷ 8 = 5 days@five days;;check: 4/5 · 5 = 4@Little's law"
Now an invented squad. Over ten days, eight items add up to forty item days. So the average work in process is forty over ten, four. The squad finishes eight in ten days, four fifths of an item a day. And the average lead time is forty over eight, five days. Four fifths times five gives four back. That's Little's law, which the chapter neither states nor needs.

@math heading="Preset limit" items="WIP 4: 4 ÷ 4/5 = 5 days@usual four;;WIP 2: 2 ÷ 4/5 = 2.5 days@limit of two"
Now compare the usual four in process with a limit of two, keeping throughput at four fifths. Lead time falls from five days to two and a half.

@art bet-on-throughput
But notice the assumption: that throughput holds when the limit is set. That's the whole bet of a limit, not a result of it. A squad that cuts work in process and sees throughput fall in step has learned something different, and its board will say so.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The ING visit is a research finding@research finding|Copy ING's structure@Copying|A WIP limit shortens lead time by itself@by itself"
Three slips. Reading the visit to ING as a research finding. Copying ING's structure, against Smit's own advice. And thinking a limit on work in process shortens lead time by itself.
