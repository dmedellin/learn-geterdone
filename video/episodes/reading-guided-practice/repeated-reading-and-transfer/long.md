@chapter The question
@title text="Repeated Reading and Transfer" sub="Reading Faster · Guided Practice" art=thumb
Read a passage three times, and you'll read it faster the third time. Almost everyone does. But is that gain yours, or does it belong to the passage? This lesson finds out with four readings.

@art three-plus-one
Read one passage three times at your natural pace, then a matched passage you've never seen, once. Do all four in one sitting, with short pauses. It's the one exception to the Daily Drill's limit of two timed readings a sitting.

@chapter Where the method comes from
@words text="Repeated readings" sub="S. Jay Samuels, 1979"
S. Jay Samuels described the method of repeated readings in nineteen seventy nine, for children learning to read fluently. Read a short passage again and again until it goes smoothly, then move to the next.

@art school-studies
Therrien's twenty oh four meta analysis looked at studies with school age students, with and without learning disabilities. Repeated reading improved their reading of the practised passages, and also of new ones, though less on the new ones.

@words text="A question, not an answer"
For a skilled adult reader, that finding is a question, not an answer. The studies weren't of readers like you.

@art new-page
So in this drill, the new passage is the test. The gain on the repeated passage will almost certainly be large. The new passage, read once at your natural pace with its six questions, is the only reading that measures you.

@chapter Two gains
@math heading="Two gains, both from reading one" items="repeat gain = reading 3 − reading 1@repeat gain;;transfer gain = new passage − reading 1@transfer gain"
There are two gains, both measured from the first reading. The repeat gain is reading three minus reading one. The transfer gain is the new passage minus reading one.

@art known-path
The repeat gain is large for almost everyone, because by the third time you know the words, the order of the ideas, and where the passage is going. It's a gain in knowing that passage. The transfer gain is the part that went with you to a text you hadn't seen.

@chapter A worked example
@math heading="Practice-effect preset" items="readings 1, 2, 3: 240, 300, 340@two forty;;new passage: 248@two forty eight;;repeat gain = 340 − 240 = +100@a hundred;;transfer gain = 248 − 240 = +8@plus eight"
Here's a reader whose three readings ran at two forty, three hundred and three forty words a minute. Their new passage ran at two forty eight. The repeat gain is three forty minus two forty, a hundred. The transfer gain is two forty eight minus two forty: plus eight.

@art big-small-gain
A hundred words a minute came from knowing the text. Eight went with the reader to a new one. And eight is well inside the difference two passages make on their own.

@words text="New passage at 275: +35" sub="at best, above the range once"
Now put the new passage at two seventy five, a transfer gain of thirty five. That's a bigger number, and still one reading of one new passage. Against this reader's baseline range, it would be at best above the range once.

@chapter A shared starting point
@art shared-start
Both gains share the first reading, and it's one reading of one passage too. A slow first reading inflates both gains. A fast one shrinks both. That's why you also check the new passage against your baseline range, which your log can do.

@chapter Still useful
@words text="It helps you know a text" sub="it isn't a new speed"
None of this makes repeated reading worthless. Reading a passage you need to know well several times does help you know it. What the drill rules out is reading the third reading's rate as a new speed. The three readings are logged as a condition. The new passage is logged as a test.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The third reading is your new speed@The first|Measuring from a slow first reading@The second|Taking a school-age finding as an adult one@And the third"
The first slip is believing the third reading shows your new speed. It shows that you know the passage. The second is measuring the transfer gain against a slow first reading. And the third is taking a school age finding as an adult one. That's a reason to test it on yourself, not a result about you.

@chapter Try it
@words text="220, 270, 310 · then new passage 232" sub="repeat gain? transfer gain?"
Your turn. Readings of one passage at two twenty, two seventy and three ten, then a new passage at two thirty two. What are the two gains? You should get a repeat gain of ninety, and a transfer gain of twelve.
