@chapter The question
@title text="Reading with a Pacer" sub="Reading Faster · Guided Practice" art=thumb
A pacer is a highlight that moves down a passage line by line, at a rate you set. It's the screen's version of the hand that speed reading courses taught readers to sweep down the page. Does it help you?

@art two-pages
The drill finds out with two timed readings: one at your own pace, and one on a matched passage behind the pacer, set at the middle of your baselines. Each is followed by six questions, and the two effective rates are compared.

@chapter The real question
@words text="With the pace fixed, did you understand as much?"
The paced rate probably came out close to the target, because the highlight set it. So that rate says little on its own. The real question is the other one: with the pace fixed, did you understand as much?

@art steady-or-hurried
Some readers find the highlight steadies them, and their score holds. Others find it hurries them past a sentence they wanted to read again, and the score drops. Your two effective rates say which happened today.

@chapter Where the pacer comes from
@art hand-sweep
The pacer is the oldest technique in the speed reading tradition. The Evelyn Wood courses, from nineteen fifty nine, taught readers to sweep a hand down the page and let the eyes follow. The claim was that with training, the eyes take in whole lines at a glance, and you read far faster and understand as well.

@words text="A guide can't widen what the eye takes in" sub="Rayner and colleagues, 2016"
The review of the research that Keith Rayner wrote with four colleagues, published in twenty sixteen, explains why no guide can widen what the eye takes in at each stop. What a guide can do is set a steady pace, and discourage drifting and needless going back.

@chapter How long a line stays lit
@math heading="Pacer at 250 words a minute" items="one word = 60,000 / 250 = 240 ms@one word;;12 × 240 = 2,880 ms a line@twelve words;;8 words → 1,920 ms · 15 words → 3,600 ms@eight words"
The lab lights each line for its words times sixty thousand, over the target, in milliseconds. At two fifty, one word gets two hundred and forty milliseconds. So a line of twelve words stays lit for two thousand eight hundred and eighty. On a phone, eight words gets one thousand nine hundred and twenty, and fifteen words gets three thousand six hundred.

@art line-highlight
A long line stays lit longer than a short one, so the pace in words a minute is the same, whatever the layout.

@chapter One plausible pair
@math heading="Steadier preset" items="A, unpaced: 240 × 4/6 = 160@four of six;;B, pacer at 250: 250 × 5/6 ≈ 208.3@five of six;;B − A ≈ 48.3@forty eight point three"
Here's one plausible pair. Reading A, unpaced: two forty words a minute, four of six right, so an effective rate of one sixty. Reading B, with the pacer at two fifty: five of six right, about two oh eight point three. B is ahead by about forty eight point three. It's tempting to stop there.

@math heading="Where the 48.3 comes from" items="1 question at 250 = 250/6 ≈ 41.7@forty one point seven;;B with 4 right: 250 × 4/6 ≈ 166.7@four right;;only ≈ 6.7 above A@six point seven"
But B's rate is only ten words a minute higher. Nearly all the difference is one more question. At two fifty, one question is worth about forty one point seven understood words a minute. Give B four right instead of five, and it's about one sixty six point seven, only about six point seven above A.

@art one-question
A one question difference is not a result. In the no difference preset, both get five of six, and the gap is about eight point three: just the highlight doing what it was told. Most first comparisons look like this. Small, and inside what one question can produce.

@chapter Ask yourself
@list heading="Three questions for your log" items="Did you finish each line, or let words go?@finish each line|Did you want to look back?@look back|Were you watching the text or the highlight?@the highlight"
Did you finish each line before the highlight moved, or keep up by letting words go? Did you want to look back at a line and find it had gone dark? Was your attention on the text, or on the highlight?

@art log-note
Answer them in a sentence in your log, because the numbers can't. In your log, the unpaced reading is a test and the paced one is a condition, so only the first counts toward a verdict. The pacer stays in this course as a practice tool.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking it makes the eyes work faster@The first|Reading the rates, not the effective rates@The second|Expecting every line to take the tile's time@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the pacer makes the eyes work faster than they can. Set too fast, it just makes you leave words behind, and the questions show it. The second is reading the rates instead of the effective rates. And the third is expecting every line to take the tile's time.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Pacer at 300 wpm · line of 10 words" sub="how long is it lit?"
Your turn. A pacer is set at three hundred words a minute. How long does it light a line of ten words? Work out one word first. You should get two hundred milliseconds a word, and two thousand for the line.
