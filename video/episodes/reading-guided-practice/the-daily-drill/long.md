@chapter The routine
@title text="The Daily Drill" sub="Reading Faster · Guided Practice" art=thumb
The Daily Drill is the routine the rest of this Subject keeps going. Ten to fifteen minutes a day. One unread passage, read behind the pacer at your hold target, and scored.

@art calendar
After you press Done, the lab asks whether your attention left the text, then gives you the six questions. And every fifth session is different: it's a test, at natural pace, with no pacer.

@art hourglass
So here are the rules. One pool passage a day, with the pacer at your hold, and at most two timed readings in a sitting.

@list heading="The rules" items="Every fifth session: a test@Every fifth session|3 holds in a row: target up by a twentieth@Three holds|2 misses in a row: down by a twentieth@Two misses|Attention gone: shorter, not slower@shorter"
Every fifth session, a test. Three holds in a row, four or more right each time, raise the target by about five percent, a twentieth. Two misses in a row lower it by the same. And if your attention left the text, make the next session shorter, not the pacer slower.

@chapter Why each rule is there
@art tired-reader
A session is short because a timed reading done while tired or interrupted measures the tiredness. That's also why you stop after two. A third measures your tiredness.

@words text="A paced rate is the target's, not yours"
The test has no pacer because a paced rate is the target's. Only an unpaced reading can show whether your own pace has moved. And the verdict still reads the median of the last three tests.

@art small-steps
The target moves by a twentieth, so one good day or one bad one can't move it far. And three holds against two misses makes it a little harder to go up than to come down.

@chapter Three holds
@math heading="Three holds at 288" items="5/6, 4/6, 5/6: three holds@five, four and five;;288 × 21/20 = 302.4@twenty one twentieths;;302.4 × 21/20 = 317.52@three more holds"
Here's a reader with three paced readings at two eighty eight that scored five, four and five of six. All three held. So the next target is two eighty eight times twenty one twentieths, three oh two point four. Three more holds there would raise it to three seventeen point five two.

@chapter Two misses, and a test
@math heading="Two misses at 288" items="3/6, 3/6: two misses@three of six;;288 × 19/20 = 273.6@nineteen twentieths"
Another reader's last two paced readings at two eighty eight scored three of six each. Two misses in a row bring the target down, to two eighty eight times nineteen twentieths: two seventy three point six.

@words text="4, 3, 5, 4 at 288 → stay at 288" sub="four sessions since the last test: today is a test"
A third reader scored four, three, five and four. The last three aren't all holds, and the last two aren't both misses, so the target stays at two eighty eight. And four practice sessions since the last test make today a test: no pacer, natural pace.

@chapter Tests use standard passages
@art pool-bands
The pool holds eighteen passages, rising from easy to hard, served in order and never repeated. It starts with eight easy ones. Your baselines were standard ones, so a test on an easy passage would come out faster for that reason alone. When a test is due, Start skips ahead to the next unread standard passage.

@chapter What the research supports
@words text="Deliberate practice" sub="Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993)"
Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch Romer, in nineteen ninety three, called work like this deliberate practice: effortful, aimed at the edge of what you can do, with immediate feedback. Their study was of musicians, and how much of expert performance such practice explains is disputed.

@words text="Unlikely to double or triple" sub="Rayner and colleagues, 2016"
On reading itself, Rayner and colleagues' twenty sixteen review concluded that readers are unlikely to double or triple their rate while understanding as well as at their normal pace. The routine borrows the shape of practice, not a promise.

@art log-curve
What it guarantees is a log in which your tests can show what happened, whichever way it goes. From here on, every lesson ends by sending you back to this session.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="As much as possible, as fast as possible@The first|Counting paced readings as progress@The second|Raising the target after one good day@And the third"
The first slip is reading as much as possible, as fast as possible. A session's length is set by your attention, not by ambition. The second is counting paced readings as progress. They go up when you raise the target. And the third is raising the target after one good day.
