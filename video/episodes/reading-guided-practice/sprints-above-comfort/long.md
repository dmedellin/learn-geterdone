@chapter The question
@title text="Sprints Above Comfort" sub="Reading Faster · Guided Practice" art=thumb
A sprint is a short reading behind the pacer at about twice your baseline rate, with no questions. It's the speed reading tradition's way of making your ordinary pace feel slow. Does it work? This lesson measures it.

@art sprint-then-test
The drill runs in a fixed order. First the sprint: about a minute behind the highlight at twice your rate, taking in what you can. Then, straight after, the test: a new passage at your natural pace, with six questions.

@chapter The claim
@art reset-dial
The tradition's claim is that a sprint resets what feels normal. After a minute at a pace you can't follow, your ordinary pace feels slow, and you read faster without trying. If you felt it, the drill takes the feeling seriously enough to measure it.

@words text="The feeling is one thing" sub="the test's rate and score are another"
The feeling is one thing. The rate and the score of the test after it are another.

@chapter Twice as fast
@math heading="The sprint's line time" items="480 = 240 × 2@twice;;12 × 60,000 / 480 = 1,500 ms@fifteen hundred;;half of 2,880 ms at 240@half"
From a baseline of two forty, the sprint target is twice that, four eighty. At four eighty, a twelve word line is lit for twelve times sixty thousand over four eighty: fifteen hundred milliseconds. That's half what it had at two forty.

@art no-questions
And the sprint has no questions on purpose. At twice your rate, you won't take in every word. Its rate is the pacer's, the target you typed. The sprint is the treatment, not a measurement of you. The reading that measures you is the test after it.

@chapter Judging the test
@art range-band-test
When you score the test, the lab puts the range of your own baseline rates into the range boxes, and the verdict compares your test with it. The verdict reads your test's rate, not its effective rate, because the range is made of rates.

@math heading="After-sprint preset" items="range: 238 to 251@two thirty eight;;test: 268 wpm, 5 of 6 → ≈ 223.3 effective@two sixty eight;;268 − 251 = 17 above@seventeen above;;verdict: above the range once@above the range once"
Here's a reader with a baseline range of two thirty eight to two fifty one. After the sprint, their test reads at two sixty eight words a minute, with five of six right, about two twenty three point three effective. That's seventeen above the top of the range. Verdict: above the range once.

@art one-in-four
That verdict is exactly as strong as it sounds. With nothing changed at all, a fourth reading is the highest of four about one time in four. It's worth writing down. It is not yet a change.

@words text="247 wpm, 5 of 6 · inside the range" sub="no-effect preset"
Same reader, another day: a test at two forty seven, five of six right, about two oh five point eight effective. Inside the range. The sprint felt the same, and the test shows nothing a reading without one wouldn't have shown.

@chapter What would make it evidence
@art comparison-days
To find out whether sprints move your pace, you need tests after sprints across several days, and then the median of the last three above your baseline range. Even then, was it the sprint, or the practice around it? That takes tests without sprints as well, for comparison.

@list heading="Testing sprints properly" items="Sprint for about a minute@Sprint for about|Read the next passage at natural pace@natural pace|Judge it by the rule@Judge it|Keep a comparison@Keep a comparison"
So: sprint for about a minute, on a passage you won't be tested on. Straight after, read the next passage at your natural pace and log it as a test. Judge it by the rule. And keep a comparison: on other days, take tests without a sprint first.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="After a sprint you read faster for good@The first|Taking felt speed for measured speed@The second|Reading the sprint's rate as your own@And the third"
The first slip is believing that after a sprint you read faster for good. The second is taking the felt speed for the measured one. Your natural pace feels slow after a sprint, which is the point, but the feeling isn't the rate. And the third is reading the sprint's rate as your own. It's logged as a condition, not a test.
