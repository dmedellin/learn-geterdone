@chapter The scheme
@title text="Simple Reproduction: The Three Great Exchanges" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 20" art=thumb
These are the best known figures in volume two: a whole economy in two departments.

@math heading="Marx's scheme" items="I:  4000c + 1000v + 1000s = 6000 means of production@means of production;;II:  2000c + 500v + 500s = 3000 articles of consumption@articles of consumption;;total value 9000@nine thousand"
Department one: four thousand of constant capital, a thousand of variable, a thousand of surplus value, six thousand of means of production. Department two: two thousand, five hundred and five hundred, three thousand articles of consumption. Total value, nine thousand.

@art two-blocks
The rate of surplus value is a hundred per cent in both. All surplus value is spent. The question is whether this nine thousand can be disposed of so that next year starts exactly where this one did. Marx finds three great exchanges that clear the whole product.

@chapter Exchange one: within II
@art within-two
The first is inside department two. Its workers' five hundred of wages and its capitalists' five hundred of surplus value must be spent on means of consumption, and department two's own product contains a thousand of exactly that. So that thousand disappears into its own households.

@chapter Exchange two: between them
@art between-departments
The second is between the departments. Department one's thousand of wages and thousand of surplus value must also be spent on consumption. But its product is machines and materials. Meanwhile department two still holds two thousand of consumer goods whose value is its constant capital, and it needs means of production to replace what it used up. The two meet.

@quote text="I (v + s) = II c." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 20"
So on the basis of simple reproduction, department one's v plus s must equal department two's c. Two thousand each way.

@chapter Exchange three: within I
@art within-one
The third is inside department one. What's left is its four thousand of constant capital, means of production only department one can use. Coal goes to the ironworks, iron to the colliery, machines to the machine builder. None of it is ever anyone's revenue.

@math heading="Every part counted once" items="within II:  500v + 500s = 1000@a thousand;;between:  2000 each way@each way;;within I:  Ic = 4000@four thousand;;1000 + 2·2000 + 4000 = 9000@nine thousand"
Check it. A thousand within department two, two thousand each way between them, and four thousand within department one. That adds back to nine thousand, every part counted once.

@art money-round
Then Marx puts the money back. Department one pays a thousand pounds in wages. Its workers buy from department two, and department two spends the same thousand on means of production from department one. By means of fifteen hundred pounds, Marx says, five thousand pounds of commodities have circulated.

@chapter When it doesn't balance
@art unsold-surplus
Raise department one's surplus value to eleven hundred. Now its v plus s is twenty one hundred, against two thousand. Two thousand still goes each way, but a hundred of means of production has no buyer. Lower it to nine hundred, and a hundred of department two's product is unsold instead.

@words text="I 4000c + 875v + 875s · II 1750c + 376v + 376s" sub="1,750 = 1,750"
Marx gives a smaller scheme in the next chapter, eight thousand two hundred and fifty two in all. Its department one v plus s is seventeen fifty, and so is department two's c. It closes too. The condition is a proportion, not a size.

@chapter Smith, and an objection
@words text="Dept II's product = all v + s"
This explains Smith's mistake. Department two's product, three thousand, equals the whole year's wages and surplus value in both departments. From the consumer's side, every price does resolve into incomes, but only for means of consumption.

@words text="A balance is an accident"
The standard objection is to what the condition can show. It's an accounting identity at given values. It says nothing about how an unplanned economy comes to meet it, and holding prices at value leaves out the mechanism that might adjust. Marx's own remark in the next chapter is that under this mode of production, a balance is an accident.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Any buyer, anywhere, will do@The first|Ic is someone's income@The second|Returning wages enrich the capitalist@And the third"
The first slip is thinking each department's product only needs a buyer somewhere. The buyer has to be in the other department, in the right amount. The second is reading department one's constant capital as somebody's income. And the third is supposing the return of wages enriches the capitalist. It only gets back the money he paid out.
