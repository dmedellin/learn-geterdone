@chapter Quesnay saw it
@title text="Former Discussions: Quesnay and Smith" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 19" art=thumb
Before Marx, economists had asked how a year's product replaces itself. This chapter reads them, and finds where part of the answer went missing.

@art seed-kept-back
Start with the man Marx admires. Quesnay was a court physician who turned to economics in his sixties. His Tableau starts from last year's harvest and notices something easy to miss: part of the harvest is seed. It doesn't circulate. It stays with its producers, to serve as capital again.

@words text="Farming keeps reproduction in view"
Marx says Quesnay saw this thanks to the limits of his horizon. For him, farming was the only sphere where labour yields a surplus, and farming's cycle from seed to harvest keeps the reproduction of capital in plain view.

@chapter Smith's dogma
@art three-parts
Then Adam Smith, in seventeen seventy six. On most things Marx builds on Smith. Here he says Smith went backwards. The price of every commodity, Smith writes, finally dissolves into wages, profit and rent. So does the price of the whole annual product.

@words text="value = v + s" sub="the c is gone"
Rent and profit are both parts of surplus value. So in Marx's letters, the dogma reads: the value of a commodity is v plus s. The c, the means of production used up, is gone.

@chapter The horse
@art horse-regress
Smith saw the objection. A fourth part may seem needed to replace the farmer's capital, the wear of his horses and implements. But the horse's price, he says, is itself rent, wages and profit.

@quote text="He refers us from one line of production to another, and from that to a third." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 19"
Marx replies that the horse's price also contains means of production used up in breeding it, and Smith never says so.

@chapter The sum Smith skips
@math heading="Marx's simple scheme, Chapter 20" items="c = 4,000 + 2,000 = 6,000@six thousand;;v + s = 1,500 + 1,500 = 3,000@three thousand;;annual product = 9,000@nine thousand;;missing:  9,000 − 3,000 = 6,000 = c@missing"
The lab does the sum the dogma skips. In Marx's simple reproduction scheme, constant capital is six thousand, and wages and surplus value fifteen hundred each, three thousand together. The annual product is nine thousand. The dogma counts three thousand, so six thousand of c is missing.

@math heading="Marx's spinner" items="£372 + £50 + £78 = £500@five hundred;;income:  £50 + £78 = £128@a hundred and twenty eight"
One firm shows it too. Marx's spinner has three hundred and seventy two of means of production, fifty of wages and seventy eight of surplus value. That's five hundred of yarn, of which only a hundred and twenty eight is anyone's income.

@chapter Two phrases that sound alike
@quote text="The first mistake of Adam Smith consists in identifying the value of the annual product with the annual product in values." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 19"
Read it slowly, because the two phrases sound alike.

@art year-labour
The annual product in values is what this year's labour created: v plus s, three thousand in the scheme. The value of the annual product also includes means of production made in earlier years, whose value merely reappears. Underneath is the twofold character of labour: useful labour made every product, but this year's value creating labour made only a third of their value.

@art machine-wages
Smith came close. He noticed that workers who make machines are paid wages that are their income, though nothing they make can be eaten. His successors went the other way. Say concluded the whole product is divided as revenue. It's never the original thinkers, Marx says, who draw the absurd conclusions.

@chapter The regress
@art regress-shrinks
Smith's defenders have a stronger reply. Follow the horse back, and each step leaves a smaller remainder of means of production. Sraffa made that exact in nineteen sixty: the remainder never reaches zero, but can be made as small as you like.

@words text="This year's 6,000 must be replaced"
Marx wrote against Smith, not Sraffa. What the chapter asks about is one year's product, out of which this year's six thousand of means of production must be replaced, however the regress into earlier years comes out.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The whole price resolves into incomes@The first|The regress is a proof@The second|Capital for one is revenue for society@And the third"
The first slip is resolving the whole price of the annual product into incomes. The second is treating Smith's regress as a proof. And the third is confusing revenue for one person with revenue for society. The wages of machine makers are income, but the machines they make are nothing anyone can live on.
