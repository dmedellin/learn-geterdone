@chapter From one capital to all
@title text="The Object of the Inquiry" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 18" art=thumb
Up to here, the book has followed one capital at a time. Volume one took it into the factory. The first two parts of volume two followed it through its circuits and turnovers. Now Marx puts it in its place.

@art thread-in-cloth
Every individual capital, he writes, forms but an individual fraction of the aggregate social capital, just as every capitalist is an element of the capitalist class. The spinner's circuit is one thread in a cloth.

@chapter Consumption comes on stage
@quote text="This total process comprises both the productive consumption (the immediate process of production) together with the metamorphoses (materially considered, exchanges) which promote it, and the individual consumption together with its corresponding metamorphoses, or exchanges." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 18"
Productive consumption is production itself, cotton and spindles used up making yarn. The new item is individual consumption.

@art household-spending
That's what a worker does with wages, and a capitalist with surplus value: the bread, the coat, the coal for the grate. In the single circuit, that spending happened offstage. In society's movement it's on stage, because selling the commodities implies their purchase by the working class.

@art no-outside-market
For one firm it was enough to assume its product found a buyer, and what it needed was on sale somewhere. For society, those assumptions are the question. There's no market outside society to send the problem to.

@chapter Two departments
@art two-departments
Chapter twenty gives the rule this lesson uses. Society's product falls into two great sections. Department one makes means of production, things that will be used up producing something else. Department two makes means of consumption, things that pass into the households of workers and capitalists.

@art coal-two-ways
The rule is by use, not by what the thing is. Coal burned under a mill's boiler belongs to department one. The same coal in a kitchen grate belongs to department two.

@chapter Sorting a product list
@math heading="Machines 300 · coal 150 · bread 150" items="coal to the mill:  100 → Dept I@a hundred;;coal to kitchens:  50 → Dept II@fifty;;Dept I:  300 + 100 = 400@four hundred;;Dept II:  150 + 50 = 200@two hundred"
A small society makes machines worth three hundred and coal worth a hundred and fifty, and bakes bread worth a hundred and fifty. A hundred of the coal goes to a mill, fifty to kitchens. Sorted by use, department one holds four hundred, the machines and the mill's coal. Department two holds two hundred, the bread and the kitchen coal.

@words text="Misfiled: Dept I 450 · Dept II 150"
File the kitchen coal with the rest, just because it's coal, and department one rises to four hundred and fifty. Now fifty of supposed means of production stand where no capitalist will buy them to produce with.

@words text="Dept I 6,000 · Dept II 3,000" sub="Marx's totals, Chapter 20"
Marx's own totals, which the rest of this course takes apart, are six thousand of means of production and three thousand of means of consumption.

@chapter Still capitalist as a whole
@words text="The aggregate capitalist"
A tempting shortcut is to suppose that, added up, one firm's capital is another's income, so the whole product is income. Say drew that conclusion, and Proudhon took it from him. Marx replies that taken as a unit, society is still capitalist. We're dealing with the aggregate capitalist.

@art stock-company
Society is then one stock company, and it shares with many others the peculiarity that everyone knows what he puts in, but not what he'll get out.

@chapter Two is a simplification
@words text="Dozens of industries" sub="Leontief's input-output tables"
The standard objection is that real products won't stay in one column. Coal, electricity and a lorry serve both factories and households, and Leontief's input output tables use dozens of industries. Marx grants the mixed cases. The course keeps two, because the question needs only one line: used up in production, or in a household.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="For society, the whole product is income@The first|Sorting by what a thing is@The second|Money advanced limits production@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the whole product is income for society. Of nine thousand of Marx's product, only three thousand can go into any household. The second is sorting by what a thing is, not its use. And the third is taking the money advanced as the limit of production, when workers can be worked longer or harder.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Malt → brewery → beer → households"
Your turn. A maltster sells malt to a brewery, and the brewery sells beer that households drink. Which department does each product belong to? Then ask: with Marx's totals, what's the most households can buy in a year?
