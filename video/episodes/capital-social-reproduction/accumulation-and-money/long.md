@chapter Why save up?
@title text="Accumulation in Department I and the Money It Needs" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 21" art=thumb
Now the scheme starts to grow. But before surplus value can become new capital, Marx says, it has to be hoarded as money.

@art lumpy-machine
Why save up at all? Because a new machine or a new mill comes in lumps, and a year's surplus value may not buy one. So capitalist A sells his surplus product and keeps the money, year after year, building up a new potential money capital.

@words text="Potential money-capital" sub="in practice, a simple hoard"
It's potential capital because it's meant to become productive capital. But in practice he merely accumulates a simple hoard. And that money isn't new wealth. It was circulating, and it has stopped.

@chapter Where are the buyers?
@art hoarders-sell
The difficulty follows at once. Money is withdrawn from circulation and hoarded by selling commodities without buying anything after. If every capital does that, it seems inexplicable where the buyers are to come from.

@words text="Every sale is a purchase?" sub="Say, Treatise, 1803"
This is where Say's law comes in, and it deserves its full strength. Say argued that products are paid for with products. Nobody sells except to buy, so the sum of sales is the sum of purchases, and a general glut is impossible. In its modern form, what one person saves, another borrows and spends.

@chapter Two groups
@art investors-buy
Marx doesn't name Say here, but his section bears on the law. Department one's capitalists are at different stages. While A and others are building hoards, another group, B and others, have hoards big enough to invest, and they buy with money saved earlier.

@art balanced-meet
The two groups meet as buyers and sellers, each in only one of those roles. The one sided sales and the one sided purchases cancel, if they're equal. And nothing makes them equal. A balance, Marx writes, is an accident under the crude conditions of this production.

@chapter The lab's three cases
@math heading="Dept I: s = 1000, half accumulated = 500" items="balanced:  250 − 250 = 0@evenly;;all hoard:  500 − 0 = +500 unsold@still hoarding;;all invest:  0 − 500 = −500 unmet demand@spending an old hoard"
The lab takes department one's thousand of surplus value, and accumulates half, five hundred. Split it evenly, and hoarders sell two hundred and fifty while investors buy two hundred and fifty: zero. If every accumulating capitalist is still hoarding, five hundred of surplus product is unsold. If every one is spending an old hoard, five hundred of demand has no product.

@art glut-pile
Saving becomes investment here only when someone else is spending an older hoard at the same moment, and in the same amount.

@chapter Department II's side
@quote text="Hence there would be an overproduction in department II, equal in volume to the expansion of production in department I." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 21"
Department two meets the same problem from the other side. If department one keeps five hundred of its surplus product to accumulate at home, only fifteen hundred of its v plus s is left to exchange against department two's two thousand.

@words text="1,500 against 2,000"
So you can't simply start accumulating from Marx's simple scheme. It has to be rearranged first, which is what the next lesson's scheme does.

@chapter The banks
@art bank-gathers
There's an obvious way out. Banks gather these hoards and lend them. Marx mentions it with an edge: some men take pleasure when all these potential capitals become loanable capital in the hands of bankers.

@words text="Interest balances saving and investment?" sub="loanable funds · Keynes, 1936"
The standard objection builds on that. In the loanable funds view, the interest rate moves until saving equals borrowing, and Say's law is restored through the bank. The chapter doesn't answer that; it sets credit aside. Keynes later argued that saving and investment are decided by different people, and brought into line by changes in income and output.

@quote text="It is a dead weight of capitalist production." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 21"
While it waits, Marx says, the hoard is quite unproductive.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Every sale is a purchase@The first|The hoard is new wealth@The second|Balance is the normal case@And the third"
The first slip is taking it for granted that every sale is a purchase. The second is thinking the hoard is new wealth. It's money that was circulating and stopped. And the third is assuming the balance between hoarders and investors is normal. Nothing fixes it.
