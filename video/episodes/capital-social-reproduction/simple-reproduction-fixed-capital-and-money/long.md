@chapter Mr X's machine
@title text="Simple Reproduction: Fixed Capital and Money" sub="Capital, Volume II, Chapter 20" art=thumb
Marx's own illustration is a spinner he calls Mr X. What his spinning machine loses in wear reappears in the value of his yarn.

@art mr-x-hoard
When the yarn is sold, that part of the price is put by in money, year after year, and spent only when the machine has to be replaced, all at once.

@quote text="It settles down beside the productive capital and retains the form of money." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 20"
The money for wear isn't turned back into the machine whose loss it makes good. It settles down beside the productive capital.

@chapter The difficulty
@words text="IIc = 2,000, of which 200 is wear"
Here's the problem for last lesson's scheme. Department two's two thousand of constant capital includes wear, say two hundred, that it wants to keep as money this year. So department two sells two thousand to department one, but buys only eighteen hundred back.

@words text="200 pounds falling from the clouds?"
Then department one would have to make good the two hundred in money that never returns to it, and two hundred of its own product would stay unsold. Assuming department one just supplies the money, Marx says, is as legitimate as assuming two hundred pounds fall every year from the clouds.

@chapter Machines of different ages
@art two-sections
Marx's solution: department two isn't one capitalist. Its machines are of different ages. One group, section one, has machines that expire this year, and spends its hoard on new ones, buying from department one and selling nothing back. Another, section two, sells the part of its product that carries wear, and keeps the money.

@art money-cancels
Section one's money goes to department one. Department one spends it on section two's goods, and it ends as section two's hoard. The two one sided acts cancel, if they're equal. Such a balance, Marx says, would seem to be a law of reproduction on the same scale.

@chapter When they don't match
@math heading="Gap = renewals − wear hoarded" items="balanced:  200 − 200 = 0@renew two hundred;;short renewals:  100 − 200 = −100@only a hundred;;bunched:  300 − 200 = +100@three hundred"
The lab computes the gap as renewals less wear hoarded. Hoard two hundred and renew two hundred: zero. Renew only a hundred: minus a hundred, so a hundred of department one's product has no buyer. Renew three hundred, a year when many machines expire together: plus a hundred, and department one is a hundred short.

@art unsold-crates
In every case, department one's v plus s still equals department two's c. The scheme balances in value, and the product still doesn't clear.

@math heading="Marx's own case" items="section 1 renews 180;  section 2 holds 200@a hundred and eighty;;20 of I unsold + 20 of IIc unsold = 40@forty"
Marx's own case is a hundred and eighty against two hundred. There remain twenty of department one's product unsold on one side, and twenty of department two's on the other. Commodities worth forty remain unsaleable.

@quote text="The result would be a crisis—a crisis in production—in spite of the fact that reproduction had taken place on the same scale." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume II, Chapter 20"
Economists blamed crises on disproportions in growth. That one can arise when fixed capital is merely renewed, Marx says, is new to them.

@chapter A planned surplus
@art reserve-stock
In a society that planned its production, he says, the remedy would be a continuous relative overproduction, a reserve of fixed capital and materials beyond the year's needs. Such a surplus is not an evil in itself, but it is an evil under capitalist production.

@chapter An objection
@art bunched-renewals
The standard objection is that the conditions are set to make the mismatch show. With thousands of firms and machines of every age, expiring and wearing will usually be close, and banks lend one firm's depreciation fund to another.

@words text="The same as among men"
Marx's claim isn't about size. The mismatch arises under his strictest assumptions, with no growth and no credit, because the machines that expire vary from year to year, above or below the average, the same as among men.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Depreciation balances itself@The first|The gap runs one way only@The second|The wear hoard is revenue@And the third"
The first slip is thinking depreciation balances itself for society, as it does in one firm's books. The second is supposing the gap can run only one way. Too many renewals one year is often followed by too few. And the third is taking the wear hoard as revenue. Spent on consumption, it would eat the machine.
