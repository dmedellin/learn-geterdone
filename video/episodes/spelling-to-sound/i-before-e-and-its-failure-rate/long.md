@chapter The famous saying
@title text="I Before E, and Its Failure Rate" sub="Spelling to Sound" art=thumb
I before e, except after c. It's the best known spelling rule in English. Many people repeat it when they aren't sure how to spell believe or receive. So how often is it right?

@art two-tiles-order
The rule says that when i and e sit together, you write i-e. But if the letter before the pair is c, you write e-i. The lab runs it on every i-e and e-i in the list of two thousand eight hundred words. There are fifty-eight.

@chapter The score
@art score-bar
The rule is right on thirty-six of the fifty-eight. That's sixty-two point one percent, a little over six in ten. If you trust it, you'll spell about four in ten of these words wrong. A student who knows that will check the dictionary. A student who doesn't will trust the saying.

@math heading="Four words, four results" items="friend, believe  ·  ie, no c before it  ·  right@Friend and believe;;receive  ·  ei after c  ·  right@Receive is;;weight, eight  ·  ei said ay  ·  miss@Weight and eight;;science  ·  ie after c  ·  miss@And science"
Friend and believe follow the rule. Receive is the rule working the other way: after c, it's e-i. Weight and eight break it. And science breaks it too, in a different way. That difference is the point.

@chapter Three kinds of miss
@art three-piles
The twenty-two misses aren't random. Sorted, they make three groups.

@list heading="22 misses" items="eigh  ·  eight, eighteen, eighty, weigh, weight, neighbor, neighborhood, height@The first group|cie  ·  ancient, efficient, science, society, species …@The second group|alone  ·  either, neither, foreign, protein, weird@The third group"
The first group is e-i-g-h. Eight, weigh, weight, neighbor, height and three more. That's eight words. The second group is c-i-e: ancient, efficient, science, society, species and four more. That's nine. The third group is five words that stand alone: either, neither, foreign, protein and weird.

@art weight-scale
Look at the first group. In most of them, the letters say ay, as in weigh. Height has the sound of the word eye. So these words aren't about ee at all, and the saying was never about them.

@art split-pair
The second group is where except after c fails. The rule says e-i after c, and all nine have i-e. In science, the i and the e are said apart. In efficient, the c is part of a sh sound.

@chapter A better question
@art narrow-sieve
The saying was made to help with words said with ee, like believe. So the lab asks a narrower question. Keep only the words where the letters line up with the parts of the word, and the dictionary has ee at that place. That leaves eighteen words.

@words text="40 set aside" sub="14 don't line up · 26 not said ee"
Forty words were set aside. In fourteen, the letters and the parts of the word can't be matched one to one. And twenty-six are said with something other than ee at that place.

@math heading="Only where the pair says ee" items="14 of 18 right  =  77.8%@fourteen times;;misses: either, neither, protein, species@either"
On those eighteen, the rule is right fourteen times. That's seventy-seven point eight percent. The four misses are either, neither, protein and species. The first three have e-i with no c. The last has i-e after a c.

@chapter What to keep
@list heading="Four facts" items="i before e, for words said with ee@Keep the first half|either, neither, protein@Learn either|after c, ei only where it says ee  ·  receive@after c|eigh is a group of its own@And learn that"
So here's what to keep. Keep the first half of the saying, for words said with ee. Learn either, neither and protein as three words. Learn that after c, the saying is right only where the pair says ee, as in receive. And learn that e-i-g-h is a group of its own.

@words text="after c: 2 of 11" sub="receive · perceive"
The second half of the saying is the weaker one. Of eleven words with a c before the pair, only two follow it: receive and perceive.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="treating a saying as a rule@The first|thinking the c part is safe@The second|using it on every ie and ei@The third"
The first slip is treating a saying as a rule. It's easy to remember, and that's why it has lasted. The second is thinking the c part is the safe part. It's the weaker half. And the third is applying it to every pair, like weight and height, where the letters don't say ee.

@chapter Try it
@words text="weight · receive · science · foreign" sub="which one follows the rule?"
Your turn. Which of these follows the rule: weight, receive, science or foreign? Pause and think. Receive has e-i after a c, which is what the rule says. The other three break it.
