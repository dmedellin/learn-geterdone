@chapter Moses and the prophets
@title text="Surplus Value into Capital" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 24" art=thumb
Last lesson, the capitalist ate every penny of surplus value. Now he adds it to his capital.

@quote text="Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets!" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 24, §3"
Marx parodies the economists who made accumulation a commandment. Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets! But it's also a tribute. The formula expressed the historical mission of the bourgeoisie, and classical economy took it in bitter earnest.

@chapter The spinner's £2,000
@art spinner-capital
Go back to the chapter's start. A spinner advances ten thousand pounds, four fifths in cotton and machinery, one fifth in wages. At a hundred percent rate of surplus value, his yarn is worth twelve thousand, and two thousand is surplus value. Two thousand pounds is two thousand pounds. You can't see or smell surplus value in it.

@math heading="Capitalising £2,000" items="c: £1,600 · v: £400@sixteen hundred"
To capitalise it, he lays it out exactly as before: sixteen hundred pounds in cotton and the like, four hundred in more spinners. That needs extra means of production and extra workers already on the market.

@art spiral
The working class supplies the workers, since wages keep it and let it increase. The circle of simple reproduction, in Sismondi's word, becomes a spiral.

@chapter The ball rolls on
@quote text="The original capital of £10,000 brings in a surplus-value of £2000, which is capitalised. The new capital of £2000 brings in a surplus-value of £400, and this, too, is capitalised" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 24, §1"
It's the old story, Marx says: Abraham begat Isaac, Isaac begat Jacob. The original capital of ten thousand pounds brings in a surplus value of two thousand, which is capitalised. The new capital of two thousand brings in a surplus value of four hundred, and this, too, is capitalised. And so the ball rolls on.

@math heading="All of s capitalised" items="year 1: £10,000 + £2,000 = £12,000@twelve thousand;;original share: 10,000 / 12,000 = ⅚@five sixths;;each year: capital × 6/5@six fifths;;year 5: £10,000 · (6/5)⁵ = £24,883 4s@twenty four thousand;;original share: about 40%@forty percent"
After one year the capital is twelve thousand, and the original is five sixths of it. Each year the capital is multiplied by six fifths. After five years it's twenty four thousand, eight hundred and eighty three pounds, four shillings, and the original advance is only about forty percent.

@art vanishing-original
Marx says the original capital becomes a vanishing quantity beside the capital accumulated. Capitalise only half, and after five years it's sixteen thousand, one hundred and five pounds, two shillings, with the original at sixty two percent. Consume it all, and you're back to simple reproduction, at ten thousand.

@chapter Not one atom
@words text="“not one single atom of its value”"
Where did the first ten thousand come from? By his own labour and his forefathers', the economists say, and Marx lets that stand for now. But the additional two thousand? There is not one single atom of its value that doesn't owe its existence to unpaid labour.

@art smith-error
Marx also corrects Smith and Ricardo, who held that capitalised surplus value is all spent on wages. The spinner shows the error in one line: sixteen hundred of the two thousand goes into cotton and machinery.

@chapter Abstinence
@art faust-two-souls
As capital personified, the capitalist is driven by competition to accumulate. As a man, he wants to enjoy himself: Faust's two souls. Dr Aikin's Manchester history of seventeen ninety five tells of manufacturers who lived like misers, then built brick houses. Not until seventeen fifty eight was a businessman seen with his own carriage.

@art abstinence
Then Nassau Senior replaced the word capital with the word abstinence. Marx's ridicule runs a page. If corn isn't all eaten but some is sown, that's the capitalist's abstinence. If wine is left to mature, that too.

@words text="Senior: “abstinence” · Marshall: “waiting”"
But the idea outlived the ridicule. Marshall called it waiting, and Boehm Bawerk and Fisher built interest on preferring present goods. Marx answers that the capitalist's spending grows with his accumulation, without one necessarily restricting the other.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Accumulation as abstinence@The first|All capitalised s goes to wages@The second|The original stays the bulk@And the third"
The first slip is calling accumulation abstinence. The arithmetic is common ground. What it rewards is the dispute. The second is treating all capitalised surplus value as wages. And the third is thinking the original capital remains the bulk of a growing capital.

Your turn. Take the spinner's ten thousand pounds, four parts constant to one variable, at a hundred percent. Capitalise three quarters of each year's surplus value. Work out the capital after two years, and what share of it the original still is.
