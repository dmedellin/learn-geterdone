@chapter Not yet a comparison
@title text="National Differences of Wages" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 22" art=thumb
You've probably heard wages compared across countries, by the day or the hour, to make a point about who's better off or who can compete. Marx says such comparisons aren't yet comparisons.

@art many-differences
Everything that sets the value of labour-power differs between countries: necessaries and their prices, training costs, women's and children's labour, productivity, the length and intensity of the day. So first bring day wages to a uniform working day. Then turn the time wage into a piece wage, since only output measures how productive and intense the labour is.

@chapter A scale of national labours
@quote text="These national averages form a scale, whose unit of measure is the average unit of universal labour." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 22"
Within one country, labour below average intensity doesn't count as normal labour. On the world market the national averages themselves differ. These national averages form a scale, whose unit of measure is the average unit of universal labour.

@art intensity-scale
An hour of more intense national labour creates more value, expressed in more money. And on the world market, more productive national labour also counts as more intense, so long as competition doesn't force that nation to sell down to its goods' value.

@words text="A higher money wage proves nothing about real wages"
Two consequences follow. Money has a lower relative value where capitalist production is more developed, so nominal wages there are higher. That proves nothing about real wages, the necessaries the money buys.

@chapter Cheaper to the capitalist
@quote text="in England wages are virtually lower to the capitalist, though higher to the operative than on the Continent of Europe." cite="J. W. Cowell, Factory Commission, 1833, quoted by Marx"
And the day's wage can be higher while the price of labour against its product is lower. Marx's evidence is English. Cowell, of the Factory Commission of eighteen thirty three, concluded that in England wages are virtually lower to the capitalist, though higher to the operative than on the Continent of Europe.

@art spindles
The factory inspector Redgrave reported in eighteen sixty six that Continental labour, despite lower wages and longer hours, was dearer in proportion to its product. His table counts spindles per person: fourteen in France, thirty seven in Prussia, seventy four in Great Britain.

@chapter One hour of spinning
@math heading="England against the Continent" items="per lb: England 6d / 2 = 3d · Continent 4d / 1 = 4d@per pound;;relative wage: 3d / 4d = ¾@three quarters;;England: (2 · 8d − 6d) / 6d = 166.67%@a hundred and sixty six;;Continent: (8d − 4d) / 4d = 100%@a hundred percent"
The lab sets two countries side by side, with illustrative figures. England pays sixpence an hour for two pounds of yarn, the Continent fourpence for one. A pound is worth eightpence on the world market. Per pound, England pays threepence and the Continent four, a relative wage of three quarters. England's rate of surplus value is a hundred and sixty six point six seven percent. The Continent's is a hundred percent.

@art two-countries
The country with the higher money wage has the higher rate of surplus value.

@art railway-navvies
English contractors building railways in Eastern Europe and Asia found the same: the higher wages of English navvies roughly matched their higher intensity, and the relative price of labour moved opposite to the money wage.

@math heading="Two other cases" items="England at 1 lb an hour: (8 − 6) / 6 = 33.33%@one pound an hour;;world value 6d: England 100% · Continent 50%@sixpence"
Change the output and it flips. Give England only one pound an hour, and its relative wage is three halves, and its rate falls to thirty three percent. Or let competition force the world value of a pound down to sixpence: the relative wage stays three quarters, and the rates fall to a hundred percent for England and fifty for the Continent.

@chapter Carey and unit labour cost
@art unit-cost
Henry Carey argued in eighteen thirty five that national wages are proportional to productivity. Marx called the conclusion absurd, and accused Carey of shuffling a confused mass of statistics. But his premise lives on. Economists compare countries by unit labour cost, Marx's wage per unit of output. The dispute is over the rate of surplus value, which exists only on Marx's definitions.
