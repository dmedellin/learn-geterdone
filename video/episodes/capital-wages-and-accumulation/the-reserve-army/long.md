@chapter The rising composition
@title text="The Reserve Army of Labour" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 25" art=thumb
Last lesson held the composition of capital fixed. Now Marx lets it rise, and the result is one of his best known claims.

@art more-material
As productivity grows, one worker turns more raw material into product in the same time, with more machinery, more buildings, more power. So constant capital grows faster than variable. Spinning capital that was half constant early in the eighteenth century might be seven eighths constant now. The value ratio rises less than the material one, because means of production cheapen as they multiply.

@quote text="If in the meantime the original capital, say £6,000, has increased to £18,000, its variable constituent has also increased. It was £3,000, it is now £3,600." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 25, §2"
Marx puts it as a sum. If in the meantime the original capital, say six thousand pounds, has increased to eighteen thousand, its variable constituent has also increased. It was three thousand, it is now thirty six hundred.

@math heading="Marx's sum" items="1:1: £6,000 / 2 = £3,000 v@half and half;;4:1: £18,000 / 5 = £3,600 v@four to one"
At half and half, six thousand has three thousand variable. At four to one, eighteen thousand over five is thirty six hundred. The capital tripled. The variable part, which is what employs workers, rose by a fifth.

@chapter Concentration and centralisation
@art centralisation
Individual capitals grow by accumulation: concentration. Against it runs centralisation, capital attracting capital. Larger capitals beat smaller ones, and credit gathers scattered money into a few hands. Capital grows in one place to a huge mass in a single hand, because it has elsewhere been lost by many.

@words text="The world would still be without railroads"
Its speed is the point. The world would still be without railroads, Marx says, if it had to wait for a few capitals to accumulate enough. Joint stock companies did it at a stroke. And every centralised capital renews its equipment in the latest form, which speeds the rise in composition.

@chapter Capital triples
@art capital-triples
Now carry it further, period by period, with the composition rising each time.

@math heading="c/v rises 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 · s/v 100%" items="capital: 6,000 · 9,000 · 12,000 · 15,000 · 18,000@six thousand;;v = capital / (1 + c/v) = £3,000 every period@three thousand every period;;workers: 300 throughout@three hundred;;supply in period 5: 420 · reserve 120@a hundred and twenty"
The lab runs Marx's rising ratios, one a period, from his six thousand. With all surplus value capitalised, capital goes six, nine, twelve, fifteen, eighteen thousand. But the variable capital is three thousand every period. At ten pounds a head, that's three hundred workers throughout. The supply grows by thirty a period, so by period five there are a hundred and twenty workers capital doesn't want.

@chapter The reserve army
@art reserve-army
Accumulation itself produces a relatively redundant population, Marx says, in direct ratio to its own energy. And this reserve is not a misfortune of capitalist production, but a condition of it.

@quote text="It forms a disposable industrial reserve army, that belongs to capital quite as absolutely as if the latter had bred it at its own cost." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 25, §3"
It forms a disposable industrial reserve army, that belongs to capital quite as absolutely as if the latter had bred it at its own cost. Capital expands by fits and starts, into railways or a sudden new market, and needs workers ready to throw at the decisive point.

@art ten-year-cycle
The modern cycle of about ten years, through average activity, high pressure, crisis and stagnation, depends on the reserve being formed, absorbed and formed again. And the over work of the employed swells the reserve, while the reserve's competition forces over work on the employed.

@chapter Both sides at once
@art two-sides
Marx rejects the theory that high wages make workers multiply until wages fall. Farmers didn't wait for that. When wages rose, they brought in machinery. Supply and demand for labour aren't independent forces. Capital works on both sides at the same time: its accumulation raises demand, and its rising composition raises supply.

@words text="Kaldor, 1961: capital to output roughly constant"
The record since is the strongest objection. Employment grew with capital through the twentieth century, and Kaldor's stylised facts of growth include a roughly constant ratio of capital to output. Defenders appeal to cheapening means of production. Critics say that empties the law. The lab assumes the rise you give it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Accumulation always increases employment@The first|The reserve comes from population growth@The second|Centralisation is concentration@And the third"
The first slip is assuming accumulation always increases employment. The second is explaining the reserve army by population growth. With the same supply, a rising composition gives a reserve of a hundred and twenty, and a constant one gives none. And the third is confusing centralisation with concentration.
