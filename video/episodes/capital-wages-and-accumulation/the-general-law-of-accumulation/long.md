@chapter Who is in the reserve
@title text="The General Law of Capitalist Accumulation" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 25" art=thumb
Last lesson, accumulation produced a reserve army. Now Marx asks who is in it, and what its existence means for the whole working class.

@list heading="Three forms" items="Floating@floating|Latent@latent|Stagnant@stagnant"
Every worker belongs to it while partly or wholly unemployed. Besides its acute form in a crisis and its chronic form in dull times, it always has three forms: the floating, the latent, the stagnant.

@art floating
The floating form lives in the centres of modern industry. Boys are taken on in great numbers and discharged when they come of age. Labour-power is used up so fast that, a Manchester medical officer reported, the average age at death in the labouring class was seventeen, against thirty eight for the upper middle class.

@art latent-stagnant
The latent form is in agriculture, where a part of the rural population is always on the point of moving to the towns. The farm labourer stands with one foot already in the swamp of pauperism. The stagnant form is employed, but irregularly, with maximum working time and minimum wages.

@art pauperism
Below all three is pauperism: those able to work, orphans and pauper children, and the old, the maimed and the widowed. Pauperism is the hospital of the active labour army and the dead weight of the reserve.

@chapter The law
@quote text="This is the absolute general law of capitalist accumulation. Like all other laws it is modified in its working by many circumstances" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 25, §4"
The law is a chain of proportions. The greater the social wealth and the energy of its growth, the greater the reserve army, relative to the active army, and the greater official pauperism. This is the absolute general law of capitalist accumulation. Like all other laws it is modified in its working by many circumstances.

@art ratio-bars
The lab computes only the middle term: the reserve as a ratio of those employed.

@chapter Three runs
@math heading="The boom, period 5" items="v: 34,200 / 6 = 5,700@thirty four thousand;;workers: 5,700 / £12 = 475@four seventy five;;reserve: 500 − 475 = 25@twenty five;;ratio: 25 / 475 = 1/19@one nineteenth"
In the boom, at two hundred percent, capital grows fast, the composition rises, and the wage is set to rise from ten pounds to twelve. In period five, thirty four thousand two hundred at five to one gives fifty seven hundred variable, four seventy five workers at twelve pounds. Five hundred are available, so twenty five are in reserve: a ratio of one nineteenth.

@art boom-and-reserve
In period one, all three hundred were employed at ten pounds, and the ratio was zero. By period five, the wage is a fifth higher, employment up by more than half, and a reserve has formed. Higher wages and a growing reserve aren't contradictory on the chapter's arithmetic.

@math heading="Slump and long run" items="slump: ratio 3/7 → 3@three sevenths;;long run: employment flat at 300 · ratio 2/5@two fifths"
In the slump, nothing is capitalised and the composition creeps up, so the same capital employs fewer and fewer, and the ratio climbs from three sevenths to three. In the long run, with Marx's ratios, employment stays at three hundred and the ratio reaches two fifths.

@chapter Be his payment high or low
@quote text="It follows therefore that in proportion as capital accumulates, the lot of the labourer, be his payment high or low, must grow worse." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 25, §4"
Now the passage. It follows therefore that in proportion as capital accumulates, the lot of the labourer, be his payment high or low, must grow worse. Hold on to be his payment high or low. Marx isn't saying pay must fall.

@art two-poles
He's saying the worker's position worsens as the methods of raising productivity turn into means of domination. Accumulation of wealth at one pole is, at the same time, accumulation of misery, agony of toil, ignorance and degradation at the other, on the side of the class that produces its own product as capital.

@words text="“the poor have been growing less poor”"
Gladstone, presenting his budget in eighteen sixty three, said that while the rich had grown richer, the poor had grown less poor. Marx answers: if the working class has only become less poor while producing an intoxicating growth of wealth for others, it has remained relatively just as poor.

@chapter Which reading?
@words text="Bernstein, 1899"
Read as predicting falling real wages, the law was contradicted by British wages from the later nineteenth century, and Eduard Bernstein said so in eighteen ninety nine. Read as a claim about relative position and dependence, it's harder to refute and harder to test. The course leaves the question open.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Real wages must always fall@The first|Pauperism is the whole reserve@The second|A law of nature@And the third"
The first slip is reading the law as predicting that real wages must always fall. The second is taking official pauperism for the whole reserve army. And the third is treating it as a law of nature, as Ortes and Townsend did. Marx calls it peculiar to capitalist production.
