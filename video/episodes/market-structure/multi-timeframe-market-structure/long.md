@chapter The question
@title text="Multi-Timeframe Market Structure" sub="Market Structure" art=thumb
The daily chart is rising. The one hour chart is falling. So which one is right? It's a question that freezes a lot of new traders, and the answer is that they're both right, about different things.

@words text="Context above. Execution below."
The higher timeframe tells you the environment. The lower timeframe tells you whether the pullback is still unfolding, or has produced a usable trigger. Context above, execution below.

@chapter Three jobs
@art timeframe-stack
Timeframes answer different questions, and mixing their jobs creates contradictory signals. So give each one a job. There are three: regime, location, and trigger. You read them in that order, from large to small.

@art regime-uptrend
Regime comes from weekly and daily structure. It shows the larger trend, the major range, and the important protected swings. Mark the protected swing, because that's the level the bigger picture rests on.

@art location-zones
Location asks where price is right now. At support, at resistance, at a range edge, or at an unhelpful midpoint? On a three chart desk that's the middle chart's job. With the two charts this lab uses, the daily is read twice, once for direction and once for position.

@art ltf-shift
And the trigger lives on the lower timeframe. It shows the pullback, the reaction, the structure shift, and the precise invalidation you'd execute against.

@chapter A pullback, not a reversal
@art pullback-inside-trend
Here's the key picture. A lower timeframe downtrend can be nothing more than a pullback inside a larger uptrend. Seen up close it looks bearish. Seen from above, it's a dip in a rising market.

@words text="Lower TF bearish ≠ short" sub="first ask: is it the higher TF's pullback?"
So lower timeframe bearish does not automatically mean short. First ask whether that bearish move is simply the pullback of a bullish higher timeframe.

@art ltf-shift zoom=in
In the lab's example, the daily pullback's lowest close holds above the marked level. On the hourly, the same pullback stops making lower lows, and then takes out its last lower high. That's the same reading one scale down. And a daily close below that level would break both at once.

@chapter Four steps
@list heading="The workflow" items="Regime@Regime|Location@Location|Trigger@Trigger|Invalidation@Invalidation"
Read from large to small. Regime: is the higher timeframe bullish, bearish, or ranging, and where's the protected swing? Location: is price at a meaningful edge, a prior breakout, a protected swing, or merely the middle? Trigger: has the lower timeframe actually changed character in your direction? Invalidation comes next.

@words text="Invalidation" sub="a lower-TF swing that fails the trigger, not the thesis"
Invalidation is defined on the execution timeframe. Which lower timeframe swing proves the trigger failed, without contradicting the higher timeframe thesis? That's the level your plan rests on.

@chapter Saying it out loud
@quote text="Daily structure is bullish. Price is pulling back into the prior daily breakout. The 1-hour structure remains bearish, so I will wait for a 1-hour higher low and break upward before considering an entry." cite="A useful analysis sentence, Multi-Timeframe Market Structure Lab"
Here's the kind of sentence the lesson asks for. Daily structure is bullish. Price is pulling back into the prior daily breakout. The one hour structure remains bearish, so I will wait for a one hour higher low and break upward before considering an entry.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Three cautions" items="Forcing every timeframe to agree@The first|Trusting a pattern in midrange@The second|Choosing timeframes after the move@The third"
The first caution: don't force every timeframe to agree. Disagreement often just describes a pullback. The second: a midrange setting lowers clarity. A perfect lower timeframe pattern in the middle of a higher timeframe range may still offer weak context. And the third: choose your timeframes before you analyze. Changing them after the move invites hindsight.

@art midrange-pattern
That middle one is easy to miss. The small pattern can look textbook, but if it sits in the middle of a big range, there's no meaningful edge behind it.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Act, or wait?" sub="aligned bullish · aligned bearish · wait for lower-TF · no clear edge"
Now the lesson's drill. You get a pair of charts and a stated location. Read the regime, the location, and the trigger, and choose the response both charts support. Aligned bullish, aligned bearish, wait for lower timeframe confirmation, or no clear higher timeframe edge. Waiting and standing aside are real answers here, not failures. The point is to choose what both charts actually support.
