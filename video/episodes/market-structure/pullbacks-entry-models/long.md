@chapter The question
@title text="Pullbacks & Entry Models" sub="Market Structure" art=thumb
You can be right about direction and still take a poor trade. The entry can be too late, too early, or in a place the structure doesn't support. So once you know the direction, how do you actually get in?

@art three-entries
This lesson compares three ways in. Each one trades off entry price, confirmation, and the distance to your invalidation. None of them is universally best.

@chapter Measuring the cost
@art rr-zones
To compare them you need one number. Reward to risk, written as an R multiple: the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. A later lesson makes R the unit of the whole plan. Here it's the number that shows what each entry costs.

@math heading="R multiple" items="R = distance to target ÷ distance to stop@distance to the target"
In symbols, R is the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. A farther stop, with the same target, means a smaller R.

@chapter Three families
@art aggressive-touch
The aggressive entry gets in at the expected reaction zone, before the lower timeframe has shifted. You get a better location, but more false starts, because nothing has confirmed the pullback is over yet.

@art confirmation-shift
The confirmation entry waits. It wants a reaction, a local structure shift, and a defined swing that can serve as invalidation. It uses the reaction low the pullback shifted from as that invalidation.

@words text="Confirmation trades price for evidence"
That's the tradeoff in one line. Confirmation gives up some entry price to gain evidence that the pullback has ended.

@art continuation-break
The continuation breakout enters as price resumes through a continuation level. That's the strongest proof of the three, but usually a later price, and a wider risk of chasing.

@chapter Side by side
@list heading="What each one pays" items="Aggressive: best location, least proof@aggressive entry|Confirmation: some price for evidence@confirmation entry|Continuation: most proof, latest price@continuation breakout"
Put them side by side. The aggressive entry buys location and pays in false starts. The confirmation entry pays a little price to see the pullback end. And the continuation breakout pays the most in price, in exchange for the most proof. Your choice depends on which cost you're willing to carry.

@art three-entries zoom=in
On one pullback, those three entries sit at three different prices. Same direction, but a different price and a different invalidation each time, so a different R.

@chapter The entry sequence
@list heading="Direction is not an entry" items="Trend@Trend|Location@Location|Reaction and trigger@reaction and trigger|Invalidation@invalidation"
Direction alone is not an entry. You need four things. Trend: the active higher timeframe structure and the protected swing that keeps it alive. Location: a prior breakout, a range edge, a support or resistance zone, or another planned area. Then a reaction and trigger, and finally an invalidation.

@words text="Touch · local shift · continuation" sub="chosen before the setup"
For the trigger, you choose whether your model enters on the touch, on a local shift, or only after continuation resumes. And you choose it before the setup appears.

@words text="Invalidation sets the size" sub="define the price, then size around the distance"
Then define the price that proves the entry model wrong, and size the trade around that distance. The stop comes first. The size follows from it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common mistakes" items="Buying because it looks cheap@The first|Waiting until location is gone@The second|Moving the model after entry@The third|Ignoring the failed pullback@And fourth"
The first mistake is buying because price looks cheap. A deep pullback may already have invalidated the trend. The second is waiting for so much proof that location disappears, which worsens entry price and reward to risk. The third is switching models after entry. And fourth, ignoring the failed pullback.

@art failed-pullback
That last one deserves a picture. Once the protected structural level breaks, waiting for a better entry can turn into denial. The pullback isn't deep any more. It has failed, and the trade idea it supported is gone.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Match the rule to the model" sub="aggressive · confirmation · continuation · no trade"
Now try the lesson's drill. You get a stated rule, and you match it to the entry model it describes. Aggressive, confirmation, continuation breakout, or no trade. Choose the one that matches the rule, not the one that looks best in hindsight. Then say what that model pays, in price or in proof.
