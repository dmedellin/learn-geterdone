@chapter The question
@title text="Volume & Relative Strength" sub="Market Structure" art=thumb
Price shows you direction. But two more questions matter. How many people took part in that move? And is this asset actually leading its benchmark, or just drifting along with it?

@words text="Volume → participation" sub="Relative strength → leadership"
Volume helps you judge participation. Relative strength asks whether the asset leads or lags its benchmark, even when both charts are green, or both are falling. Neither one replaces structure. They add evidence to it.

@chapter Volume against its own baseline
@art volume-baseline
Start with volume context. Compare the current bar with the asset's own recent activity. A big bar matters more when it's unusual for that market. In the lesson's lab, the baseline is the average of the ten bars before the event bar.

@math heading="Volume baseline" items="baseline = average of the 10 bars before the event bar@the ten bars before;;event volume vs baseline → expanded or not@expanded or not"
So the baseline is the average of the ten bars before the event bar. Then you ask whether the event volume expanded or not against it. It's judged against the market's own recent habit, not against an absolute number.

@words text="2,240 vs a baseline near 950" sub="the lab's example break bar"
In the lab's example, the bar that closed above the range traded two thousand two hundred forty, against a baseline near nine hundred fifty. And no bar after it fell back to that baseline.

@chapter Follow-through decides
@art follow-through-hold
That last part matters most. Read conventionally, activity expanded at the new prices instead of drying up. That's what separates a break that holds from one that fades. A close back inside the range would break the reading.

@art exhaustion-fade
Because high volume can come with continuation, or with exhaustion. The candles after the event show whether the move was accepted. So a huge bar on its own can't tell you which kind you're looking at. Only the next candles can.

@list heading="Volume can be distorted by" items="Earnings@Earnings|News@news|Index rebalancing@index rebalancing|Closing auctions@closing auctions"
And one big bar doesn't prove intent. Earnings, news, index rebalancing, and closing auctions can all distort activity. So always read volume together with price structure and follow-through.

@chapter Relative strength
@art normalized-lines
Now leadership. To compare an asset with its benchmark, normalize both. In the lesson's comparator, both series start at one hundred, so their paths show percentage performance instead of raw price differences. Both can be rising. The question is which one is rising faster, as a percentage, and only the normalized paths answer it.

@art ratio-line
Then look at the ratio, the asset divided by the benchmark. When the ratio rises and finishes above its starting level, the asset is improving against the benchmark. That's leadership.

@words text="Relative strength ≠ RSI"
A word on names. Relative strength here means performance compared with another asset or a benchmark. It's not the RSI momentum indicator, which is a separate oscillator. Same words, different tool. In this lesson, relative strength is always a comparison between two things.

@chapter A confirmation stack
@art confirmation-stack
The lesson puts it in order. Structure first: define the trend, the range edge, the breakout, or the protected swing. Participation second: compare event volume with recent activity, and note whether up candles or down candles attract more of it. Leadership third: prefer assets improving against the benchmark when that agrees with your thesis. And follow-through decides.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Judging volume in absolute terms@The first|Trusting one big bar@The second|Mixing up relative strength and RSI@The third"
Three slips. The first is judging volume in absolute terms instead of against the market's own baseline. The second is trusting one big bar without looking at what follows. And the third is mixing up relative strength with RSI.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Expanded? Accepted? Leading?" sub="two drills"
The lesson ends with two drills. In the first, judge the event bar against the ten bars before it, then let the follow-through decide. In the second, compare percentage performance, not whether each return is positive or negative, and say whether the asset leads, lags, or roughly matches its benchmark. Both drills also let you answer that the numbers aren't enough to judge. Sometimes that's the honest reading.
