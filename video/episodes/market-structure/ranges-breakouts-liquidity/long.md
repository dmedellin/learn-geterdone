@chapter The question
@title text="Ranges, Breakouts & Liquidity Sweeps" sub="Market Structure" art=thumb
Price pokes above the top of a range. Is that a breakout? It's tempting to say yes the moment the line is crossed. This lesson asks a better question.

@words text="Did price close beyond it, hold there, and build structure?"
Not, did price cross the line. Did it close beyond it, hold there, and build structure? By the end you'll be able to classify a finished sequence, rejection, breakout, retest, or sweep, from its closes alone.

@chapter The range
@art range-edges
A range is a negotiation zone. Price rotates between a recognizable high and low, and those edges matter because traders keep reacting there. The middle usually tells you less than the edges do.

@words text="Acceptance" sub="close beyond · time outside · holds or builds structure"
Acceptance is the opposite of a quick poke. Price closes beyond a boundary, spends time outside it, and either holds there or forms new structure.

@chapter Liquidity and the sweep
@art resting-orders
So what is liquidity here? It's the orders resting just beyond an obvious level. The stops of traders positioned inside the range, and the entries of traders waiting for the break.

@art wick-sweep
A sweep trades far enough through the level to fill those orders, finds nothing behind them, and comes back. Price runs beyond the level, rejects, and closes back inside. The failed break is the evidence.

@words text="Wick out, close inside → sweep candidate" sub="Strong close outside → evidence, not proof"
That gives a working rule. A wick beyond the boundary with a close back inside is a sweep candidate. A strong close outside is evidence of a breakout, but not final proof.

@chapter Four outcomes
@art rejection-turn
Classify the sequence, not a single candle. First, a rejection. Price reaches the boundary and rotates away without materially trading through it.

@art close-outside
Second, a confirmed breakout. A close lands outside the range, and the candles after it stay accepted beyond the boundary.

@art retest-hold
Third, breakout and retest. Price exits, comes back to the old boundary, holds it from the other side, and then resumes.

@art wick-sweep zoom=in
And fourth, the liquidity sweep. Price trades clearly through the level, closes back inside, and the rejection often speeds back through the range.

@words text="Degree separates a sweep from a rejection"
What separates a sweep from a rejection is degree. A rejection reaches or grazes the boundary and turns. A sweep runs well past it, and comes back. That long run is exactly what fills the orders resting beyond the level.

@chapter Reading the evidence
@list heading="Read it candle by candle" items="Watch the close@the close|Watch the follow-through@the follow-through|Ignore the wick alone@the wick alone"
In the lesson's boundary reader you play each scenario candle by candle. Watch the close and the follow-through, rather than reacting to the wick alone. A candle that tags the high and can't close above it is read as sellers still defending the high, and the rotation stays inside. If a later candle closes above the high and holds there, that reading breaks, and you relabel the sequence.

@chapter Your journal
@list heading="What to write down" items="Define the range before the break@Define the range|Record the closing behavior@Record the closing|Wait for the next evidence@Wait for the next|State invalidation precisely@state invalidation"
The lesson ends with four journal habits. Define the range before the break: the high, the low, the midpoint, and how many reactions made the edges meaningful. Record the closing behavior: outside, at the edge, or back inside? Wait for the next evidence. And state invalidation precisely.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Calling a wick a breakout@The first|Judging one candle, not the sequence@The second|Reading the middle like an edge@The third"
Three slips follow from all this. The first is calling a wick a breakout, when the close came back inside. The second is judging one candle instead of the whole sequence, because the next candles decide the label. And the third is treating the middle of a range as if it said as much as the edges.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Rejection · Breakout · Retest · Sweep" sub="classify from the closes alone"
Now the drill. The labels are hidden. Look at a completed sequence around the range boundary and choose the classification that best explains the whole thing, using only the closes and what came after them. Ask two things every time. Where did the candle close? And what did the next candles do with that close?
