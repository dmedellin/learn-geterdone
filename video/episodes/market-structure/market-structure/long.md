@chapter The question
@title text="Market Structure" sub="Market Structure" art=thumb
Look at a price chart without any labels and ask one plain question. Is this bullish, bearish, ranging, or changing its mind? And then a harder one. Which price would prove your answer wrong?

@words text="Bullish · Bearish · Range · Transition" sub="and the level that proves it wrong"
That's the test this lesson builds toward. You label an unmarked chart, then name the level the label rests on. To get there you only need three ideas, and a habit of looking at closes.

@chapter Candles and closes
@art candle-anatomy
Each candle sums up one span of time, an hour or a day. The body runs from the open to the close. The thin wicks above and below reach the high and the low that span touched. An up colored body closed above its open. A down colored body closed below it.

@words text="Rules key on the close" sub="a wick through a level = tested, not broken"
A wick shows where price went. The close shows where price was accepted when the span ended. So every rule in this course keys on the close. A wick through a level that closes back on the right side of it is a level tested, not a level broken.

@art swing-points
Structure is read from swing points. A swing high is a candle whose high stands above the highs on either side of it. A swing low is the mirror. Don't label every candle. Label the moves that visibly changed direction, and leave the rest alone.

@chapter Three structures
@art bullish-staircase
Bullish structure is higher highs and higher lows. It's read as buyers willing to pay more than last time, so each dip is met before it reaches the previous low. The latest meaningful higher low is usually the level buyers must defend. A close below it ends the reading.

@art bearish-staircase
Bearish structure is the mirror. Lower highs and lower lows, read as sellers accepting less than last time. Each bounce is capped before the previous high. The latest meaningful lower high is the level sellers must defend, and a close above it ends that reading.

@art range-box
A range is different. Both sides defend the edges and neither defends the middle, so buying shows up near the low and selling near the high. Inside a range, apparent trends often fail. It stops being a range when price closes beyond an edge instead of turning back inside.

@words text="One red candle ≠ invalidation"
And one more thing. A trend is not invalidated by one red candle. The structural level matters more than the candle's color.

@chapter Terms as evidence
@list heading="Four terms" items="BOS: break of structure@break of structure|CHoCH: change of character@change of character|HL: protected low@protected low|LH: protected high@protected high"
Four terms, used as evidence. A break of structure is a swing breaking in the direction of the trend, like a new high above the previous high in a bullish market. A change of character is the first meaningful break against the trend. The protected low is the higher low that launched the move through the prior high. The protected high is its bearish mirror.

@words text="CHoCH = a warning" sub="confirmation: the opposite swing sequence"
Treat a change of character as a warning, not a verdict. Confirmation normally needs the opposite swing sequence to form. And a close beyond the protected swing weakens or invalidates the thesis it was protecting.

@chapter Two timeframes
@art two-timeframes
One stock can show several structures at once. The daily chart can be bullish while the one hour chart pulls back bearishly. That's the two timeframes disagreeing, not the daily reading failing. Use the higher timeframe for direction and the lower one for timing.

@quote text="Daily structure is bullish. The 1-hour chart is pulling back bearishly. I wait for the 1-hour to reclaim its protected high before considering a bullish entry." cite="Example trade narrative, Market Structure Lab"
Here's the lesson's example narrative. Daily structure is bullish. The one hour chart is pulling back bearishly. I wait for the one hour to reclaim its protected high before considering a bullish entry.

@chapter A simple process
@list heading="The process" items="Start high, then zoom in@Start high|Require a close@require a close|Write conditions@write conditions"
A simple process beats more indicators. Start high, then zoom in: read weekly or daily direction first, then use four hour or one hour structure to plan. Then require a close beyond the level, because a wick can be a sweep, not a break. And write conditions, not predictions.

@words text="Above X, bullish. Below X, invalid."
A conditional plan sounds like this. Above X, bullish. Below X, invalid. It tells you what you'd do either way.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Labelling every candle@The first|Treating a wick as a break@The second|Naming the latest swing, not the defended one@The third"
Three slips are worth naming. The first is labelling every candle instead of the turns that matter. The second is treating a wick as a break when the close came back. And the third is naming the most recent swing as your invalidation, when the level that counts is the one the current reading is defending.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Label it, then name the level" sub="bullish · bearish · ranging · transition"
Now try the lesson's drill. Take an unmarked chart and focus on the sequence of meaningful highs and lows, not the last candle. Choose the dominant structure. Then pick the price your reading rests on, the level that would prove it wrong.
