@chapter Summoned to Manchester
@title text="Senior's “Last Hour”" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 9" art=thumb
Nassau Senior was Oxford's first professor of political economy, a serious economist, and the author of the abstinence theory of profit. In eighteen thirty six, the Manchester mill owners wanted an economist on their side.

@quote text="was summoned from Oxford to Manchester, to learn in the latter place the political economy that he taught in the former." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 9, §3"
Marx tells it with relish. One fine morning Senior was summoned from Oxford to Manchester, to learn in the latter place the political economy that he taught in the former.

@art factory-act
The Factory Act of eighteen thirty three had limited the hours of young persons, and the movement for a ten hour day was growing. Under the Act, Senior wrote, a mill employing anyone under eighteen could work eleven and a half hours a day.

@chapter Senior's analysis
@art half-hours
His figures. A manufacturer invests a hundred thousand pounds, and the year's product is worth a hundred and fifteen thousand. Divide that among the twenty three half hours of the day. Twenty replace the capital, one replaces the wear on the mill, and the last two produce the net profit.

@words text="The whole net profit is derived from the last hour"
So, he concluded, the whole net profit is derived from the last hour. Cut the day by one hour, and the net profit is destroyed. Cut it by an hour and a half, and even the gross profit goes. Lengthen it to thirteen hours, and the net profit is more than doubled.

@chapter Separate c from v
@art shorter-day
Marx's first answer is short. If workers spend most of the day reproducing cotton, coal and machinery, then a shorter day uses less of them, in proportion. You gain just as much as you lose.

@art c-out-of-the-hours
Then he takes the analysis seriously. Senior put wages in one sum with the raw material. Separate them. Marx supposes the separated account keeps the manufacturers' story, so wages and net profit are equal in value. The worker adds value only by working, in proportion to the hours.

@math heading="Senior's 11½ hours" items="necessary = surplus = 11½ / 2 = 5¾ h@five and three quarter;;rate: 5¾ / 5¾ = 100%@a hundred percent"
So of eleven and a half hours, five and three quarter hours reproduce the wages, and five and three quarters produce the net profit. The rate of surplus value is a hundred percent.

@words text="He performs no such miracle"
How can an hour's yarn be worth five and three quarter hours? Because four and three quarters were already in the cotton and machinery it used, and the spinner added one. He performs no such miracle as replacing the cotton. Its value goes into the yarn because he spins it.

@chapter Recomputing the predictions
@math heading="Senior's predictions, recomputed" items="an hour off: 4¾ / 5¾ = 19/23 ≈ 82.61%@nineteen twenty thirds;;an hour and a half off: 4¼ / 5¾ = 17/23 ≈ 73.9%@seventeen twenty thirds;;13 hours: 7¼ / 5¾ = 29/23 ≈ 126.09%@twenty nine twenty thirds"
Now recompute. Take an hour off, and it's four and three quarters over five and three quarters, nineteen twenty thirds, about eighty two point six percent, not zero. An hour and a half off gives seventeen twenty thirds, about seventy four percent. And thirteen hours gives twenty nine twenty thirds, about a hundred and twenty six percent, not more than doubled.

@art last-hour-clock
The manufacturers, Marx says, had invented more stories about the last hour than the millenarians about the day of judgment. It's all bosh. And the cry outlived Senior: in eighteen forty eight James Wilson, founder of The Economist, raised it against the Ten Hours Bill.

@chapter The stronger case
@art idle-machines
Senior's position has a stronger form, and it's fair to state it. Much of a mill's capital loses value with time, through interest and machines going out of date, whether or not they run. Spread over fewer hours, those costs weigh more. Marx's reckoning assumes wear is proportional to hours worked. Whether the last hour arithmetic is right is settled. How much a shorter day costs is not.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The last hour makes all the profit@The first|Lumping c and v together@The second|Hours spent replacing the machinery@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the last hour produces the whole profit. The profit is made in every surplus hour. The second is lumping constant and variable capital together. And the third is thinking the worker spends separate hours replacing the machinery.
