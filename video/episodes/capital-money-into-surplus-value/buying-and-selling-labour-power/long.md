@chapter The riddle
@title text="The Buying and Selling of Labour-Power" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 6" art=thumb
Last lesson left a riddle. Surplus value can't come from the money, which only realises prices, or from the resale. So it must come from the commodity bought first. Not from its value, since it was bought at value, but from its use.

@words text="A use-value that is a source of value"
Moneybags needs a commodity whose use-value has the peculiar property of being a source of value. And he finds one: labour-power, the mental and physical capabilities a person exercises whenever they produce anything useful.

@chapter Free twice over
@quote text="the owner of money must meet in the market with the free labourer, free in the double sense" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 6"
For his money to become capital, the owner of money must meet in the market with the free labourer, free in the double sense.

@art free-twice
First, the seller owns himself, and sells his labour-power for a definite time only. Sell it once for all, and he'd be a slave. Second, he has no other commodity for sale. With leather and tools, he'd sell boots instead. Free of masters, then, and free of property.

@art long-history
Nature doesn't produce owners of money on one side and people with nothing but their capacity to work on the other. That's the result of a long history, which Marx tells at the end of the volume. For now he takes it as given, as Moneybags does.

@chapter What it's worth
@art subsistence-bundle
The rule from chapter one applies. Labour-power is worth the labour time needed to produce it. It exists only in a living person, so its value is the value of the necessities that keep that person working.

@words text="A historical and moral element"
And here's a qualification no other commodity needs. What counts as necessary depends on climate and the habits of the class. So a historical and moral element enters the value of labour-power. In a given country, at a given time, the average is practically known.

@math heading="Six hours from three schedules" items="food and fuel: 4 / 1 = 4 h a day@four hours every day;;clothes: 7 / 7 = 1 h a day@last a week;;furniture: 365 / 365 = 1 h a day@lasts a year;;daily value: 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 h@six hours;;6 h at ½s an hour = 3s@three shillings"
Things are bought on different schedules, so spread each over its life. In the lab, food and fuel worth four hours every day count whole. Clothes worth seven hours that last a week add one hour a day. Furniture worth three hundred and sixty five hours that lasts a year adds one. That's six hours, Marx's figure. At his rate, that's three shillings.

@art children-and-training
Two more items enter. The worker is mortal, so the sum includes the upkeep of his children. Add two hours a day for them, and the figure is eight hours. And a trade that needs training adds its cost, spread over the working life.

@words text="The physical minimum is a floor" sub="a price at the floor is below the value"
The bare physical minimum is a floor, below which labour-power survives only in a crippled state. A price at that floor is below the value, not equal to it.

@chapter A capacity, not labour
@art capacity-vs-use
Rossi objected that labour-power apart from subsistence is a phantom. Marx answers with a joke. When we speak of capacity for labour, we don't speak of labour, any more than capacity for digestion is digestion. The worker sells a capacity. How long it's used is settled afterwards.

@words text="The worker gives credit to the capitalist"
One detail matters later. Labour-power is usually paid for after it's used, at the end of the week, so the worker gives credit to the capitalist. Marx sets that aside for now.

@chapter A very Eden
@quote text="There alone rule Freedom, Equality, Property and Bentham." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 6"
With the price settled, Marx follows buyer and seller out of the market, which he calls a very Eden of the innate rights of man. There alone rule Freedom, Equality, Property and Bentham.

@art factory-door
Freedom, because both contract as they choose. Equality, because equivalent meets equivalent. Property, because each disposes of his own. And Bentham, because each looks only to himself. Behind the factory door, marked no admittance except on business, we'll see what the buyer does with what he bought.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The worker sells labour@The first|Value as a bare minimum@The second|Leaving out things bought rarely@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the worker sells labour, so many hours of work done. The second is reading the value as a bare physical minimum. And the third is leaving out things bought rarely. Drop the clothes and furniture and you get four hours, a third too low.
