@chapter Selling dear
@title text="Contradictions in the General Formula" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 5" art=thumb
The explanation of profit that comes first to most people is the one Marx takes first: the seller charges more than the thing is worth. Shops mark goods up. A merchant buys cheap and sells dear.

@words text="£100 → cotton → £110" sub="where does the £10 come from?"
If that were the source, last lesson's ten pounds would need no further explanation. This chapter argues it can't be the source, not for the capitalist class as a whole.

@chapter What's true in it
@art wine-and-corn
Marx starts by granting what's true. In an exchange, both sides can gain in use-value. A wine grower trading with a farmer may get more corn than he could have grown with the same labour, and the farmer more wine.

@words text="Not paid for twice over"
Condillac built a theory of trade on that: each side gives less for more. Marx replies that he's run use-value and exchange value together. Commodities aren't paid for twice over, once for their use-value and again for their value.

@chapter Everyone marks up
@art ring-of-traders
So suppose sellers can sell above value. Every seller marks up ten percent, so what's worth a hundred sells for a hundred and ten. But every seller is also a buyer, and pays ten percent too much in turn.

@math heading="Three traders, 10% all round" items="before: £100 + £100 + £100 = £300@three hundred before;;each: +£10 as seller, −£10 as buyer@as a seller;;after: £300@three hundred after"
In a ring of three traders, each holding a hundred pounds of value, that's three hundred before. Each gains ten pounds as a seller and loses ten as a buyer. Three hundred after. Our friend gained ten as a seller only to lose it again as a buyer.

@art tribute-towns
Colonel Torrens said consumers pay more than goods cost. Marx answers that the consumer's money was itself the price of something he sold. You'd need a class that only buys, and it gets its money from somewhere. Like the towns of Asia Minor, which paid tribute to Rome, then sold the Romans goods too dear. Their goods were still paid for with their own money.

@chapter Cheating
@quote text="The value in circulation has not increased by one iota, it is only distributed differently between A and B." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 5"
That leaves cheating, which certainly happens. Marx's example: A has wine worth forty pounds, B has corn worth fifty. A is clever enough to get the corn for his wine. The value in circulation has not increased by one iota, it is only distributed differently between A and B.

@math heading="Wine for corn" items="before: A £40 + B £50 = £90@ninety;;after: A £50 + B £40 = £90@still ninety;;A +£10 · B −£10 · total +£0@total"
Before, forty plus fifty is ninety. After, A holds the corn worth fifty and B the wine worth forty: still ninety. A is ten up, B is ten down, and the total hasn't moved.

@art two-piles
A single capitalist can grow rich this way. The class can't, because the sum it divides doesn't grow. That's why Marx sets merchants' and money lenders' capital aside for now. Explained by circulation alone, they'd be cheating on both sides.

@chapter Outside circulation?
@art leather-to-boots
Could value grow outside circulation, then? A producer alone can add value by adding labour, turning leather into boots. But the leather's value hasn't expanded itself. New labour was added to it.

@quote text="It must have its origin both in circulation and yet not in circulation." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 5"
Hence the double condition. Moneybags must buy at value, sell at value, and still draw out more than he put in. It must have its origin both in circulation and yet not in circulation.

@art rhodes-leap
Marx closes with a line from Aesop's fable, said to a boaster who claimed a great leap on Rhodes. Here is Rhodes. Jump here.

@words text="Ricardo · the marginalists"
The strongest objection: trade raises total output, and both sides of a voluntary exchange gain in what goods are worth to them. Marx accepts the first as a gain in use-value. The second is a different definition of value, and the argument is only as strong as Marx's.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Profit comes from selling above value@The first|One trader's gain is a gain for all@The second|Gain in use-value is gain in value@And the third"
The first slip is thinking profit comes from selling above value. Every seller is a buyer. The second is counting one trader's gain as a gain for all, when B is poorer by the same ten pounds. And the third is confusing a gain in use-value with a gain in value.
