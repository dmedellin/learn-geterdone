@chapter A line cut in two
@title text="The Working Day" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10" art=thumb
Most people's working day is set by a contract, a law and a habit. So it feels fixed, like the length of an hour. Chapter ten begins by showing that it isn't.

@art day-line
Draw the necessary labour time as a line, A to B, six hours long. Extend it to C by one, three or six hours, and you get three working days: seven, nine and twelve hours.

@math heading="6 hours necessary" items="7 h: 1 / 6 = 16⅔%@seven;;9 h: 3 / 6 = 50%@nine;;12 h: 6 / 6 = 100%@twelve"
The surplus part, B to C, is then one sixth, three sixths and six sixths of the necessary part. So the rate of surplus value is sixteen and two thirds percent for seven hours, fifty percent for nine, and a hundred percent for twelve.

@chapter Determinable, but indeterminate
@art elastic-limits
One end of the line is fixed by the value of labour-power. The other is fixed by nothing in the sale. So the day is determinable, but indeterminate in itself. Its maximum is set by the body's need for sleep and food, and by the worker's intellectual and social wants. Both limits are elastic.

@quote text="Capital is dead labour, that vampire-like, only lives by sucking living labour, and lives the more, the more labour it sucks." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10, §1"
Capital is the past labour in the means of production, which keeps its value and grows only by absorbing living labour. Capital is dead labour, that vampire-like, only lives by sucking living labour, and lives the more, the more labour it sucks.

@chapter The worker speaks
@art worn-out-years
Then the worker speaks, using the market's own logic. You bought my labour-power for a day, and I must be able to sell it again tomorrow. What you gain in labour, I lose in substance.

@math heading="30 years, or 10" items="a day's pay = 1/10,950 of a working life@ten thousand nine hundred;;worn out in 10 years: a day = 1/3,650@three thousand six hundred;;paid: one third of a day's value@a third"
If an average worker can work thirty years, a day's pay is one ten thousand nine hundred and fiftieth of the whole. Work him out in ten years, and you pay that same fraction for what's now one three thousand six hundred and fiftieth. Only a third of a day's value.

@quote text="There is here, therefore, an antinomy, right against right, both equally bearing the seal of the law of exchanges. Between equal rights force decides." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10, §1"
So, Marx concludes, there is here an antinomy, right against right, both equally bearing the seal of the law of exchanges. Between equal rights force decides.

@chapter Hidden in the minutes
@art corvee-fields
Capital didn't invent surplus labour. Athenian, Roman and Norman owners all took it. The difference is visibility. A Wallachian peasant under the corvee worked his own field some days and his lord's on others. In a factory, necessary and surplus labour glide into each other, minute by minute.

@art clock-minutes
So England's factory inspectors counted minutes. A mill that started a quarter hour early, stopped a quarter late, and nibbled thirty minutes from meals gained five hours forty minutes a week. Over fifty weeks, that's twenty seven working days a year. Moments are the elements of profit.

@art night-furnace
Where no law reached, the evidence is hard to read. Children of nine in Nottingham lace, working from the small hours to near midnight. A milliner of twenty dead after twenty six and a half hours of unbroken work. And the relay system kept furnaces running all night, because idle furnaces were capital absorbing no labour.

@chapter The objection
@words text="Leisure for income?"
The objection is serious. Economists argue that workers choose hours, trading leisure for income, and that very long days reflected low productivity: as wages rose, hours fell. Marx doesn't deny that workers agreed. His claim is that the agreement was compelled, and the exchange contains no rule for where the day should end.

@chapter Doubling the rate
@math heading="Twelve hours, then eighteen" items="12 h: (12 − 6) / 6 = 100%@a hundred percent;;15 h: 9 / 6 = 150%@fifteen;;18 h: 12 / 6 = 200%@eighteen"
Hold necessary time at six hours. Twelve hours give a hundred percent. Fifteen hours, a quarter longer, give a hundred and fifty. And eighteen give two hundred. The rate doubles when the surplus part doubles, at eighteen hours, not at twenty four.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The day is fixed by nature or contract@The first|The rate rises in proportion to the day@The second|The corvée as a different exploitation@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the day's length is fixed by nature or contract. Only the necessary part is. The second is thinking the rate rises in proportion to the day. Every added hour falls on the surplus side. And the third is taking the corvee for a different kind of exploitation, when only its visibility differs.
