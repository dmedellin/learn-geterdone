@chapter Two circuits
@title text="The General Formula for Capital" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 4" art=thumb
You know the first circuit from your own life. You sell an old bicycle you no longer need, and spend the money on something you do. Thing, money, thing.

@art corn-to-clothes
Marx's version is a peasant. He sells a quarter of corn for three pounds, and with the three pounds buys clothes. The money, so far as he's concerned, is spent and done with. What he wanted was the clothes, and once he has them, the circuit is over.

@quote text="If I purchase 2000 lbs. of cotton for £100, and resell the 2000 lbs. of cotton for £110, I have, in fact, exchanged £100 for £110, money for money." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 4"
Now turn it round. Marx's merchant begins with money. If I purchase two thousand pounds of cotton for a hundred pounds, and resell the cotton for a hundred and ten, I have, in fact, exchanged a hundred pounds for a hundred and ten, money for money.

@chapter Same acts, other order
@art two-circuits
Both circuits use the same two acts, a sale and a purchase, and nothing new is added to the market. What differs is the order. C, M, C begins with a sale and ends with a purchase. M, C, M begins with a purchase and ends with a sale.

@art money-returns
That inversion changes what moves. In C, M, C the same coin changes hands twice and doesn't come back. In M, C, M the cotton changes hands twice, and the money flows back to where it started. The merchant lets the money go, but only with the sly intention of getting it back. It isn't spent. It's advanced.

@chapter Only quantity
@words text="£100 → cotton → £100" sub="the same for the same"
Now the decisive step. The two ends of C, M, C are different use-values, and that difference is the point. The two ends of M, C, M are money. Swapping a hundred pounds for cotton and the cotton for a hundred pounds is a roundabout way of exchanging the same for the same.

@math heading="The merchant's circuit" items="£100 → cotton → £110@a hundred and ten;;ΔM = M′ − M@M prime minus M;;ΔM = £110 − £100 = +£10@ten pounds"
So the circuit can only be about quantity. Its full form is M, C, M prime, where M prime is M plus an increment. The increment, delta M, is M prime minus M. Here it's a hundred and ten minus a hundred, ten pounds. Marx names it surplus value. For now the name is all there is.

@chapter No natural end
@art miser-and-capitalist
The peasant stops when he has his clothes. But the hundred and ten pounds that comes back is a sum of money just as the hundred was, with exactly the same reason to go out again. So the circulation of capital has no limits.

@quote text="but while the miser is merely a capitalist gone mad, the capitalist is a rational miser." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 4"
Marx compares the hoarder from chapter three. Both chase exchange value without limit, but while the miser is merely a capitalist gone mad, the capitalist is a rational miser. One keeps money out of circulation. The other keeps throwing it back in.

@art golden-eggs
So the subject of the movement is the value, not the man. It's money, then cotton, then money, and it grows as it goes. Value, Marx jokes, lays golden eggs. The capitalist is capital personified, endowed with consciousness and a will.

@chapter The general formula
@art three-forms
The formula covers more than merchants. Industrial capital also buys commodities and sells the result for more money. What happens in the factory doesn't change the form. And interest bearing capital shortens it to M, M prime. That's why Marx calls it the general formula, capital as it first appears in circulation.

@words text="Carriage, storage, risk, waiting?"
A trained economist will object, and it's a good objection. The merchant moved the cotton, stored it and bore the risk. On the ordinary account, his ten pounds pays for those services and for waiting. Marx doesn't answer that here. He only insists the increment be explained, not assumed.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Treating both circuits as one@The first|Reading £110 as two sums@The second|Taking the formula for an explanation@And the third"
The first slip is treating both circuits as one. At zero difference the peasant's circuit has done its job, and the merchant's is pointless. The second is reading the hundred and ten as two sums. It's one sum, ready to go out again. And the third is taking the formula for an explanation.

Your turn. The merchant lays out a hundred pounds on cotton and lets it go for ninety. Name the circuit from what stands at its two ends, and compute delta M. Has the form changed, or only the outcome?
