@chapter Centuries of struggle
@title text="The Struggle for a Normal Working Day" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10" art=thumb
The length of the working day wasn't handed down. It was fought over, and the fight ran in two directions.

@quote text="The establishment of a normal working day is the result of centuries of struggle between capitalist and labourer." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10, §5"
The establishment of a normal working day is the result of centuries of struggle between capitalist and labourer.

@art statute-of-labourers
The first Statute of Labourers, in thirteen forty nine, after the Black Death had made labour scarce, fixed wages and hours by law. Later statutes set the summer day from five in the morning to seven or eight at night. Capital in embryo asked the state for more hours, because it couldn't yet get them by economic pressure alone.

@words text="“It is called the Factory.”"
By seventeen seventy a pamphleteer was proposing a House of Terror for paupers, with twelve hours of net labour. A few years later, Marx says, it was built for the industrial worker himself. It is called the Factory.

@chapter The Factory Acts
@art factory-acts-timeline
With machinery, all bounds of morals and nature, age and sex, day and night, were broken down. Parliament passed five labour laws between eighteen oh two and eighteen thirty three, and voted no money to enforce them. The Act of eighteen thirty three let young persons work twelve hours, and children eight.

@words text="1844 · 1847 · 1848"
In eighteen forty four, women came under the twelve hour limit. Then the Ten Hours Bill passed: eleven hours from July eighteen forty seven, and ten from the first of May eighteen forty eight. When the inspector Leonard Horner asked the workers, about seventy percent were for ten hours.

@chapter The relay system
@art relay-system
Then the employers revolted. The legal factory day was still fifteen hours. Inside it, they shuffled young persons and women from room to room, half an hour here, an hour there, until no inspector could tell who had worked how long. They paid ten hours' wages for twelve or fifteen hours' lordship over labour-power.

@art inspector-clock
The workers' answer led to the compromise of August eighteen fifty: ten and a half hours on weekdays, all between six in the morning and six at night, with common meal times. That ended the relay system, at the price of half an hour a day.

@chapter What a limit does
@math heading="6 hours necessary" items="12 h: (12 − 6) / 6 = 100%@twelve hours;;10 h: (10 − 6) / 6 = ⅔@cut to ten;;relays: 12 h comes back · 100%@relays;;10½ h: (10½ − 6) / 6 = ¾ = 75%@ten and a half"
Now the arithmetic, with six hours necessary and the value of labour-power held fixed. Twelve hours give a hundred percent. Cut to ten, and the surplus falls from six hours to four, a rate of two thirds. Under relays, the only checkable limit is fifteen hours, so the twelve hour day comes back. And ten and a half hours give three quarters.

@art two-hours-off
Cutting two hours of twelve takes a third off the rate, because both hours were surplus labour. And the half hour conceded in eighteen fifty was surplus labour too.

@words text="Wages cut by at least 25%" sub="the lab holds labour-power's value fixed"
That fixed value is a real simplification. The manufacturers cut wages by at least twenty five percent when the hours fell, which changes the necessary time. Marx's point holds for the case he states: at the same value of labour-power, a shorter day is a lower rate.

@chapter A protracted civil war
@quote text="The creation of a normal working day is, therefore, the product of a protracted civil war, more or less dissembled, between the capitalist class and the working class." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10, §7"
The isolated worker, Marx argues, succumbs without resistance. Only as a class could workers compel a law. The creation of a normal working day is, therefore, the product of a protracted civil war, more or less dissembled, between the capitalist class and the working class.

@art eight-hours
Baltimore in eighteen sixty six demanded eight hours, and the International proposed the same at Geneva. Motives were mixed: Tory landowners backed ten hours partly in revenge for Corn Law repeal. The claim is that without the workers' pressure, none of those motives would have produced a law that held.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A limit leaves the rate unchanged@The first|The ten-hour day dates to 1847@The second|The limit on paper is the limit in the mill@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a legal limit leaves the rate where it was. The second is dating the ten hour day to eighteen forty seven, when it came in on the first of May eighteen forty eight. And the third is taking the limit on paper for the limit in the mill.
