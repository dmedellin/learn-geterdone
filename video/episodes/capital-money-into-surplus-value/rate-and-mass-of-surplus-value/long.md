@chapter One worker, then a hundred
@title text="Rate and Mass of Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 11" art=thumb
So far we've measured surplus value per worker. Chapter eleven asks about the total a capital gets, and states three laws.

@art one-and-many
Start with one worker. Necessary labour is six hours, embodied in three shillings, so a day's labour-power is worth three shillings. At a rate of a hundred percent, he yields three shillings of surplus value. A capitalist with a hundred such workers advances three hundred shillings, and gets three hundred.

@math heading="The first law" items="mass of s = variable capital × s/v@variable capital times the rate;;variable capital = workers × value of one labour-power@number of workers"
That's the whole first law. The mass of surplus value is the variable capital times the rate. And the variable capital is the number of workers times the value of one labour-power.

@art mass-rectangle
Picture it as a rectangle. Its width is the number of workers, its height is the surplus from each, and the mass of surplus value is the area. Make it narrower, and you have to make it taller to keep the same area.

@chapter A compound ratio
@quote text="it is determined by the compound ratio between the number of labour-powers exploited simultaneously by the same capitalist and the degree of exploitation of each individual labour-power." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 11"
In Marx's words, it is determined by the compound ratio between the number of labour-powers exploited simultaneously by the same capitalist and the degree of exploitation of each individual labour-power.

@math heading="Same 150s" items="100 workers · 300s · 50% = 150s@a hundred workers;;50 workers · 150s · 100% = 150s@fifty workers"
Because it's a product, either factor can make up for the other. A hundred workers at fifty percent, a nine hour day, advance three hundred shillings and yield a hundred and fifty. Fifty workers at a hundred percent, a twelve hour day, advance a hundred and fifty, and still yield a hundred and fifty.

@art seesaw
So, within certain limits, the supply of labour capital can exploit is independent of the supply of labourers. Fewer workers, worked harder, can give as much.

@chapter The limit
@quote text="The absolute limit of the average working-day—this being by Nature always less than 24 hours—sets an absolute limit to the compensation of a reduction of variable capital by a higher rate of surplus-value" cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 11"
Within certain limits. Here's the second law. The absolute limit of the average working day, this being by Nature always less than twenty four hours, sets an absolute limit to the compensation of a reduction of variable capital by a higher rate of surplus value.

@math heading="Fewer workers, a longer day" items="100 workers, 12 h: 300s · 100% = 300s@three hundred;;50 workers: rate needed 300 / 150 = 200%@two hundred percent;;day: 6 + 2 · 6 = 18 h@eighteen hour;;25 workers: 400% · a 30-hour day ✗@thirty hours"
A hundred workers on a twelve hour day give three hundred shillings. To get that from fifty, the rate needed is two hundred percent. That's six plus twice six, an eighteen hour day. Possible, barely. Halve again to twenty five, and you'd need four hundred percent, a day of thirty hours, which no compensation can reach.

@art day-ceiling
Marx's own figures: five hundred workers at a hundred percent give fifteen hundred shillings a day. A hundred workers can never produce twelve hundred shillings of value, which is twenty four hours each. He says this palpable law will explain much later, when capital cuts its workforce but still wants the greatest mass of surplus value.

@chapter The third law
@art spinner-and-baker
The third law: with the rate given, masses of surplus value vary with the variable capital, whatever the constant capital. So a spinner with much machinery and few hands should make less surplus per pound of capital than a baker with many hands.

@words text="“contradicts all experience based on appearance”" sub="resolved only in Volume III"
Marx says at once that this law clearly contradicts all experience based on appearance. Capitals of the same size earn about the same profit. Volume three supplies the missing steps. Boehm Bawerk argued in eighteen ninety six that its solution contradicts Volume one instead. The course takes up that dispute when the book does.

@chapter Twelve spindles shrink to ten
@art shrinking-spindles
Marx's last example is a Paisley thread maker, who wrote in eighteen forty nine that a ten hour law would make every twelve spindles in his mill shrink to ten. To him, their power to absorb two extra hours of other people's labour was part of what they were worth: dead labour employing the living.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="More workers always means more s@The first|The compensation has no limit@The second|The mass depends on constant capital@And the third"
The first slip is thinking more workers always means more surplus value. The second is thinking the compensation has no limit. The limit is a day. And the third is thinking the mass depends on the constant capital. On this law it doesn't.

@chapter Try it
@words text="80 workers · 3s each · 10-hour day" sub="6 h necessary · mass = ?"
Your turn. Eighty workers, each labour-power worth three shillings, on a ten hour day with six hours necessary. Compute the variable capital, the rate and the mass. Then find the day forty workers would need to yield the same mass, and check it's under twenty four hours.
