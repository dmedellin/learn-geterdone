@chapter The wrong denominator
@title text="The Rate of Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 9" art=thumb
Every company report gives its profit as a percentage of the capital employed. That's the first way anyone measures a surplus. Marx says that, for his question, it's the wrong denominator.

@math heading="Marx's £500" items="C = £410 c + £90 v = £500@five hundred;;product = 410 + 90 + 90 = £590@five hundred and ninety;;s = £90@ninety"
Take his figures. A capital of five hundred pounds is laid out as four hundred and ten of constant capital and ninety of variable. The product is worth four hundred and ten plus ninety plus ninety, five hundred and ninety. The surplus is ninety.

@chapter Set c to zero
@art c-reappears
Two steps follow. Only what's consumed counts, so only the machinery's wear goes into c. And constant capital only reappears. The value actually created is v plus s, a hundred and eighty pounds, not five hundred and ninety.

@art whole-capital
So Marx sets c equal to zero. That's merely a mathematical rule, the same one anyone uses who reckons England's cotton profits after deducting what was paid for American and Indian cotton.

@math heading="Rate of surplus value" items="s / v = 90 / 90 = 100%@ninety over ninety;;not s / C = 90 / 500 = 18%@eighteen percent"
The rate of surplus value is s over v: ninety over ninety, a hundred percent. Not ninety over five hundred, which is eighteen percent, a rate so low it might pleasantly surprise Henry Carey and the other harmonisers.

@quote text="The rate of surplus-value is therefore an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital, or of the labourer by the capitalist." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 9, §1"
The eighteen percent is a real ratio too, the rate of profit, and Volume three is built on it. But it doesn't measure how the day is divided. The rate of surplus-value is therefore an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital, or of the labourer by the capitalist.

@chapter In the working day
@art day-in-two
Now the second reading. The worker spends part of the day producing the value of his own labour-power, necessary labour time, and the rest producing surplus value, surplus labour time. So s over v equals surplus labour over necessary labour. One counts materialised labour, the other living labour.

@words text="1871 mill: 80 / 52 = 153 11/13%" sub="Jacob's acre, 1815: a little over 100%"
Marx works two real examples. A mill of ten thousand spindles in eighteen seventy one pays fifty two pounds a week in wages and makes eighty of surplus: a hundred and fifty three and eleven thirteenths percent. And an English acre of wheat in eighteen fifteen gives a little over a hundred percent.

@chapter The yarn in parts
@art yarn-in-parts
The same proportions show in the product. Go back to the twelve hour day: twenty pounds of yarn worth thirty shillings. Twenty four shillings is constant capital, three variable, three surplus.

@math heading="20 lb of yarn, 30s" items="c: 20 · 24/30 = 16 lb@sixteen pounds;;v: 20 · 3/30 = 2 lb@two pounds carry;;s: 20 · 3/30 = 2 lb@surplus produce"
So twenty times twenty four thirtieths: sixteen pounds of yarn carry the constant capital. Two pounds carry the wages. And two pounds carry the surplus, which Marx calls surplus produce.

@art spinning-hours
Of course every pound was spun, and every pound contains cotton. Marx's image is that the spinner might as well have spun the last four pounds out of air. The manufacturer reads it in time: eight hours for the cotton, an hour and thirty six minutes for wear, an hour and twelve for wages, and the last hour and twelve for surplus.

@chapter The word exploitation
@art two-readings
The word exploitation is where many readers argue, and fairly. In Marx's sense it's a defined ratio. It implies the surplus is unpaid labour only if labour alone creates value. An economist who credits capital reads the same hundred percent as a division of income. The arithmetic is common ground. The name is the dispute.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Dividing by the whole capital@The first|Value of product as value created@The second|The 16 lb as yarn not spun@And the third"
The first slip is dividing the surplus by the whole capital. Count all twenty seven shillings as v and you get about eleven percent. The second is taking the value of the product for the value created. And the third is reading the sixteen pounds as yarn the spinner didn't spin.
