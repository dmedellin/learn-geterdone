@chapter The puzzle
@title text="The Labour Process and the Production of Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume I, Chapter 7" art=thumb
Think of the last thing you bought that was made in a factory. Its price had to cover the materials, the wear on the machines and the wages. If nobody cheated, you'd expect it to cover them exactly. That's the puzzle Marx sets with his spinner.

@chapter Labour in any society
@art architect-and-bee
First, the chapter steps back. Labour is a process between people and nature, and what makes it human is purpose. What distinguishes the worst architect from the best of bees is that the architect builds in imagination before building in reality.

@list heading="The labour process" items="The work itself@the work|The object worked on@object|The instruments@instruments"
Its elements are the work itself, the object worked on and the instruments. Fish in the sea is an object nature supplies. Cotton already grown is raw material. The spindle is an instrument. And at the end, the process disappears in the product.

@art wine-cellar
When a capitalist runs it, two things change. The worker works under his control, and the product belongs to him, just like wine fermenting in his cellar. The methods stay as he found them on the market, for now.

@chapter Six hours
@art cotton-to-yarn
Now count, the way the capitalist does, item by item.

@math heading="Six hours of spinning" items="cotton 10 lb: 10s@ten shillings;;spindle wear: 2s@two shillings;;6 h of spinning: 3s@six hours;;yarn: 10 + 2 + 3 = 15s@fifteen shillings;;advanced: 15s · surplus 0@made nothing"
Ten pounds of cotton, bought at its value, ten shillings. Spindle wear, two shillings. Six hours of spinning add three shillings. The yarn is worth fifteen shillings. And the capitalist, who paid three shillings for a day's labour-power and twelve for the rest, has made nothing.

@quote text="Our capitalist stares in astonishment. The value of the product is exactly equal to the value of the capital advanced." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 7, §2"
Our capitalist stares in astonishment. The value of the product is exactly equal to the value of the capital advanced.

@art abstinence
Then a comic scene. He protests his abstinence: he might have squandered the fifteen shillings. He calls the cotton and spindle a service. He claims his labour of superintendence, while his overlooker and manager try to hide their smiles. Then he laughs, and leaves the whole creed to the professors who are paid for it.

@chapter Two magnitudes
@art two-magnitudes
He knew the answer all along. A day's labour-power is worth three shillings, because half a day's labour keeps the worker for a day. Nothing stops him working a whole day. What labour-power costs and what it creates in use are two entirely different magnitudes.

@math heading="Twelve hours of spinning" items="cotton 20 lb: 20s · wear: 4s@twenty pounds;;12 h of spinning: 6s@twelve hours;;yarn: 20 + 4 + 6 = 30s@thirty shillings;;advanced: 20 + 4 + 3 = 27s@twenty seven;;surplus value: 30 − 27 = 3s@three shillings"
So run twelve hours. Twenty pounds of cotton, twenty shillings, and four of wear. Twelve hours of spinning add six. The yarn is worth thirty shillings. But he advanced twenty plus four plus three, twenty seven. Surplus value: three shillings.

@quote text="The trick has at last succeeded; money has been converted into capital." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume I, Chapter 7, §2"
Every commodity was bought at its value, and the yarn sells at its value. The riddle of chapter five is met: in circulation, where labour-power is bought, and not in circulation, where it's used. The trick has at last succeeded; money has been converted into capital.

@chapter Only necessary time
@art gold-spindle
Only socially necessary time counts, which is why the capitalist watches the work so closely. Use a gold spindle where steel would do, and only the steel one counts. In the lab's waste case, two shillings of cotton beyond the norm adds nothing to the yarn, so the surplus falls to one shilling.

@words text="Böhm-Bawerk: a reward for waiting?"
An economist who prices by marginal product would say the capital goods are productive too. Boehm Bawerk put the return down to time: the capitalist is paid for waiting. Marx's reply is that in the six hour case there's nothing to reward abstinence with, since the product is worth exactly what went in.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The surplus is the machine's contribution@The first|The worker must have been underpaid@The second|Counting every hour and every pound@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the surplus is the machine's contribution. The spindle is there in the six hour case too, and the surplus is nothing. The second is thinking the worker was underpaid. He got the full three shillings. And the third is counting every hour and pound of cotton, not just the necessary ones.
