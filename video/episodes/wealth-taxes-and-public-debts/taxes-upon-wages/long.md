@chapter The question
@title text="Taxes upon the Wages of Labour" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 2, Part II" art=thumb
Tax a labourer's wages by a fifth. You'd think his wage would fall by a fifth. Smith says that, under one condition, it rises instead, and by more than the tax.

@art two-dials
The condition comes from Book One. The wages of common labour are set by two things: the demand for labour, and the ordinary price of provisions. The first decides whether his living is liberal, moderate or scanty. The second decides how much money buys it.

@chapter The rule
@words text="Raise them somewhat higher than the tax"
So while demand and provisions stay the same, a direct tax on wages can have no other effect than to raise them somewhat higher than the tax. The labourer must still be left with the subsistence his old wage bought.

@chapter Ten shillings a week
@math heading="Wage 10s · tax of a fifth" items="new wage: 10 / (4/5) = 25/2 = 12s 6d@twelve and sixpence;;tax: (1/5) · 25/2 = 2s 6d@two and sixpence;;left: 25/2 − 5/2 = 10s@ten shillings free"
Here's Smith's sum. Wages are ten shillings a week, and a tax of a fifth, four shillings in the pound, is laid on them. The wage must rise not to twelve shillings only, but to twelve and sixpence. A fifth of that is two and sixpence, which leaves him ten shillings free.

@art wage-and-tax
That's a rise of two and sixpence on ten, a quarter. The tax is a fifth of the wage as paid, but the wage has risen by a fourth of the old one. Not one fifth part only, Smith says, but one fourth.

@chapter Why the rise outruns the rate
@math heading="Why not twelve shillings?" items="12 − 12/5 = 9.6s@Raise the wage;;new wage = old / (1 − r)@divided;;rise = r / (1 − r)@the rise"
Why not twelve? Because the tax comes off the higher wage, not the old one. Raise the wage to twelve, take a fifth, and he's left with nine point six, short of ten. In general, the new wage is the old one divided by one minus the rate. So the rise is the rate over one minus the rate, always bigger than the rate itself.

@art rise-outruns-rate
Try a tenth. The new wage is ten divided by nine tenths, eleven shillings and a penny and a third, a rise of one ninth. Smith wrote one eighth, which is the rise a tax of one ninth would need. It looks like a slip. His point stands, and the fifth and the fourth are exact.

@chapter Who pays in the end
@art employer-advances
The labourer hands the money over, but he can't properly be said even to advance it. The employer does, by paying the higher wage. Not only the tax, but something more than the tax, comes first from the employer's capital.

@art two-routes
Then the paths divide. A manufacturer charges the rise, with a profit, on the price of his goods, so the consumer pays. A farmer needs more capital for the same labourers, and must keep more of the produce, so he pays less rent. The landlord pays. Smith calls such taxes absurd and destructive.

@art fewer-jobs
Why haven't wages always risen this way? Because, he says, these taxes have generally caused a considerable fall in the demand for labour. Then the labourer bears part of it in lost work. Ricardo later argued the burden falls on profits instead.

@words text="Offices over £100 · 5s 6d in the pound"
The same rule reaches the professions. Their reward keeps a proportion to the lower trades, so a tax on it must raise it above the tax, or the professions would be deserted. Public offices are different, because their pay isn't set by competition. England taxed salaries over a hundred pounds at five and sixpence in the pound, and it was very popular.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the labourer pays because it's in his hand@The first|Expecting the wage to rise by exactly the rate@The second|Applying the rule when demand has changed@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the labourer pays because the tax comes out of his hand. The second is expecting the wage to rise by exactly the rate. And the third is applying the rule when demand has changed. The rule holds demand and provisions fixed.
