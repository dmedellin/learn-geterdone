@chapter The question
@title text="Public Debts" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 3" art=thumb
Why do nations end up in debt, and why does the debt hardly ever come down? Smith's answer starts with one blunt sentence.

@quote text="The want of parsimony, in time of peace, imposes the necessity of contracting debt in time of war." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 3"
The want of parsimony, in time of peace, imposes the necessity of contracting debt in time of war.

@chapter Hoard or spend
@art hoard-and-spend
In a rude society, a great revenue can only feed and clothe people, and nobody lends to a prince who might plunder him. So ancient sovereigns hoarded, as Tartar chiefs still do. In a commercial state, the sovereign has the same frivolous passions as the rich, and his ordinary expense becomes equal to his ordinary revenue.

@art merchant-lender
Then war needs three or four times the peace revenue at once, and new taxes take ten or twelve months to yield. Meanwhile, merchants whose capital passes through their hands several times a year are able and willing to lend. So the state foresees the facility of borrowing, and therefore dispenses itself from the duty of saving.

@chapter Anticipation and funding
@art anticipation-and-funding
Borrowing takes two forms. By anticipation, a tax is pledged for a term of years to repay principal and interest. By perpetual funding, it's pledged forever, for the interest only. Governments kept anticipating again before the first fund was clear, and drifted into funding, because it raises a greater sum. The future liberation of the revenue they leave to posterity.

@chapter The sinking fund
@math heading="Interest from 6% to 5%" items="6 − 5 = 1 saved on each 6@saving one;;1/6 of each annuity@a sixth;;1717: £523,454@seventeen seventeen"
The sinking fund came from cheaper money. When interest fell from six to five per cent, creditors accepted five, saving one on every six of interest, a sixth of each annuity. That surplus became the sinking fund, five hundred and twenty three thousand four hundred and fifty four pounds by seventeen seventeen.

@art sinking-fund
But a sinking fund, though made for paying old debts, makes new ones much easier. It's a fund always at hand to mortgage, and in peace it's the easiest money to divert, since a new tax is felt and a diverted fund isn't.

@chapter The record
@math heading="The debt, in pounds" items="1739: 46,954,623@seventeen thirty nine;;1748: 78,293,313 · about 31.3 million added@seventeen forty eight;;5 January 1775: 129,146,322@seventeen seventy five"
Smith's figures make the case. Seventeen years of the most profound peace paid off only about eight point three million, leaving almost forty seven million in seventeen thirty nine. The war that followed added about thirty one point three million by seventeen forty eight. And by January seventeen seventy five, the debt was a hundred and twenty nine million.

@art debt-staircase
In eleven peaceful years since seventeen sixty three, only about ten point four million was paid. To expect the debt ever to be discharged from savings on the ordinary revenue, he says, would be altogether chimerical.

@chapter Paying it down
@math heading="Debt 129 · 3% · paying 4.87 a year" items="interest, year 1: 3/100 · 129 = 3.87@three point eight seven;;paid off: 4.87 − 3.87 = 1@a million;;cleared in the 54th year@fifty fourth"
In millions, with payments written for the lesson, a debt of a hundred and twenty nine at three per cent costs three point eight seven a year in interest. Pay four point eight seven, the interest plus Smith's million, perhaps, and a million comes off. As the interest falls, more comes off each year. The debt is cleared in the fifty fourth year.

@words text="War loan of 100 → ≈ 222.5 · interest ≈ 6.68 > 4.87"
Now add a war. Pay for six years, then borrow a hundred, Smith's figure for the war begun in seventeen seventy five. The debt is about two hundred and twenty two and a half, and its interest is about six point six eight, more than the whole payment. Now the debt grows, and is never paid off.

@chapter What funding does
@art right-and-left
Smith says money lent to the state is capital turned into revenue. Pay a war by taxes, and private spending just shifts. Pay it by funding, and capital is destroyed. Some said interest on the debt is the right hand paying the left. Smith calls that mercantile sophistry. Later, Ricardo, Barro and Lerner each argued about this in different ways.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Expecting the sinking fund to keep pace@The first|Treating a funded debt as falling due@The second|Reading his reply as about money leaving the country@And the third"
The first slip is expecting a sinking fund to keep pace with the debt. The second is treating a perpetual funded debt as one that falls due. Nothing falls due. And the third is reading Smith's reply to the right hand and the left as being only about money leaving the country.
