@chapter The question
@title text="Taxes upon Consumable Commodities" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 2, Part II" art=thumb
A state can't measure everyone's revenue without an inquisition. So it taxes what people spend instead. Smith asks who finally pays such taxes, and finds a case where doubling a duty halves the revenue.

@words text="Equal and arbitrary, or certain and unequal"
First, the poll tax, a tax per head. If you try to make it equal, it becomes arbitrary and uncertain, since fortunes can only be guessed. If you make it certain, by rank, it becomes unequal, since fortunes differ within a rank. So the state taxes expense, supposing it roughly follows revenue.

@chapter Necessaries
@art shirt-and-shoes
Smith splits goods in two. A necessary isn't only what nature requires, but whatever custom makes it indecent for creditable people, even of the lowest order, to be without. The Greeks and Romans lived comfortably without linen. But a day labourer in Smith's England would be ashamed to be seen without a linen shirt.

@art necessaries-stack
A tax on necessaries operates exactly like a direct tax on wages. The price rises, the labourer's wage must rise with it, and his employers pass it on. And taxes pile up. You pay the tax on your shoes, and part of the tax on the shoemaker's salt and soap and candles too.

@words text="The middling and superior ranks ought always to oppose them"
So the middling and superior ranks, if they understood their own interest, ought always to oppose taxes on the necessaries of life. The final payment falls on them, always with a considerable overcharge. The landlord pays twice, in smaller rent and dearer goods.

@chapter Luxuries
@art luxury-tobacco
Luxuries are different. Taxes on them are finally paid by the consumers of the goods taxed, without any retribution. Tobacco is taxed in England at three times its original price, and hasn't raised wages. Nor has the beer tax. Common labour in London earned eighteen or twenty pence a day before it, and no more after.

@chapter Two and two make one
@math heading="A duty schedule, in pence" items="3d · 120,000 = 360,000@three pence;;6d · 100,000 = 600,000@six pence;;9d · 60,000 = 540,000@nine pence;;12d · 25,000 = 300,000@twelve pence"
Now the customs, with a schedule written for the lesson. Revenue is the duty times the quantity that still pays it. At three pence a pound, a hundred and twenty thousand pounds weight sell, three hundred and sixty thousand pence. At six pence, six hundred thousand. At nine pence, it falls to five hundred and forty thousand. At twelve pence, three hundred thousand.

@art revenue-hump
So the revenue peaks at six pence, and the twelve penny duty, double the rate, brings in half as much. That's Swift's saying, which Smith quotes. In the arithmetic of the customs, two and two, instead of making four, make sometimes only one.

@art smugglers-cove
Why? Heavy duties encouraged smuggling and cut consumption, until the revenue fell below what moderate ones would have brought. They were never meant for revenue, Smith says, but as an instrument of monopoly, taught by the mercantile system.

@chapter Smith's own customs figures
@math heading="Customs, year to January 1755" items="gross: £5,068,000@gross;;less bounties and drawbacks: 5,068,000 − 2,324,600 = £2,743,400@bounties;;less management: 2,743,400 − 287,900 = £2,455,500@management"
Smith's own figures show the leak. In the year to January seventeen fifty five, the customs brought in five million sixty eight thousand gross. Bounties and drawbacks took two million three hundred and twenty four thousand six hundred. Management took two hundred and eighty seven thousand nine hundred. The neat revenue was two million four hundred and fifty five thousand five hundred.

@art customs-leak
Less than half the gross reached the treasury. And the drawbacks bred their own smuggling: goods shipped out for the refund, then landed again somewhere else.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a higher duty always brings in more@The first|Treating necessaries and luxuries alike@The second|Assuming the poor pay taxes on necessaries@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a higher duty always brings in more. The second is treating taxes on necessaries and luxuries alike. And the third is assuming the poor finally pay taxes on necessaries. Smith's answer depends on wages rising to keep the labourer whole, which is the last lesson's condition.
