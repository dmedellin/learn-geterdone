@chapter The question
@title text="Taxes upon Rent and Profit" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 2, Part II" art=thumb
A tax is laid on a farm, a house, or a merchant's capital. Who ends up poorer? Not always the person who hands over the money. For Smith, it comes down to one question. Who can get away?

@art tenant-advances
Start with land. A tax on the rent of land is commonly advanced by the tenant, but the landlord is in all cases the real contributor, because the tenant deducts it from the rent. Land cannot be moved, so the tax stays where it's laid.

@chapter Fixed or variable
@words text="Venice: a tenth of the recorded rent"
A land tax can be fixed at a valuation, like England's, or vary with the real rent, which the French physiocrats called the most equitable of all taxes. Smith prefers the variable one, and praises Venice, where leased land is taxed at a tenth of a rent recorded in a public register.

@math heading="Valued at £1,000 · a fifth · rents fall" items="tax every year: £200@two hundred;;year 4: 200 / 700 = 2/7 ≈ 28.6%@seven hundred;;at a fifth of actual rent: £140@a fifth of"
Now the case England escaped, with rents written for the lesson. A district valued at a thousand pounds and taxed at a fifth pays two hundred a year. Let its rent fall to seven hundred. The tax is still two hundred, two sevenths of the rent, about twenty eight point six per cent. A fifth of the actual rent would be a hundred and forty.

@art falling-rent
That's Smith's warning. A fixed valuation has helped the landlord while rents rose, but in a different state of things it might be hurtful to him. The worry with a variable tax is the other way round: it may discourage improvement. His fix is to rate an improvement at the old valuation until the outlay is repaid.

@chapter The tithe
@math heading="A tithe: one tenth of the produce" items="rich land: rent 5 → 4 · a fifth of the rent@rich;;poor land: rent 2 → 1 · half the rent@poor"
Taxes on produce are really taxes on rent, since the farmer bargains for an abatement. Take two lands, each producing ten. On rich land, rent is five, and the tithe of one leaves four. That takes a fifth of the rent, four shillings in the pound. On poor land, rent is two, and the tithe leaves one. That takes half, ten shillings in the pound.

@art tithe-rich-and-poor
So the tithe, under the appearance of perfect equality, is very unequal. And since the church lays out nothing but shares the profit, it discourages the dearest improvements. It kept the growing of madder confined to the United Provinces for a long time.

@chapter Houses
@art house-and-ground
A house's rent has two parts. The building rent is the interest on what it cost to build, and builders who don't earn the ordinary profit leave the trade. What's left is the ground rent, the price paid for the advantage of the situation.

@math heading="A tax of a fifth on the tenant" items="£60 house: 60 + 12 = £72@sixty pound;;£50 house: 50 + 10 = £60@fifty pound"
Smith's example is a man who can spend sixty pounds a year on housing. Tax the tenant a fifth, and a sixty pound house now costs seventy two. So he takes a fifty pound house, which with ten in tax makes sixty. Fewer people compete for the dearer houses, their rents fall, and the loss comes to rest on the ground rent.

@art smaller-house
So a house tax falls partly on the tenant, who gives up some convenience, and partly on the owner of the ground. It falls heaviest on the rich. And ground rents are a still more proper subject of taxation than houses, being altogether owing to the good government of the sovereign.

@chapter Stock can leave
@art stock-leaves
Profit is harder. The owner's stock is almost always a secret, and it can be moved. The proprietor of stock is properly a citizen of the world. Tax it with a severe inquisition, and it leaves, taking with it the industry it supported. Stock cultivates land. Stock employs labour.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking whoever hands it over pays@The first|Calling the tithe an equal tax@The second|Thinking a house tax falls on the landlord alone@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a tax falls on whoever hands the money over. The second is calling a fixed fraction of the produce an equal tax. And the third is thinking a house tax falls on the landlord alone. It splits between the tenant and the owner of the ground.
