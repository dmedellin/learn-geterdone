@chapter The question
@title text="The Funds of the Sovereign" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 2, Part I" art=thumb
Where does the money for government come from? Before Smith gets to taxes, he asks whether a sovereign could live on funds of his own, and those, he says, must consist either in stock, or in land.

@words text="12d = 1s · 20s = £1"
A word on money first. Smith counts in pounds, shillings and pence. There are twelve pence to a shilling and twenty shillings to a pound, so four shillings in the pound is a fifth.

@chapter A sovereign's stock
@art herds-and-cellar
Stock first. The Tartar chief lived on the increase of his own herds, the earliest and rudest government. Hamburg is said to draw something from a public wine cellar and an apothecary's shop. A state cannot be very great, Smith remarks, whose sovereign has leisure to be a wine merchant.

@math heading="The Bank of England" items="profit: £592,900 a year@five and a half;;interest at 3%: 3/100 · 10,780,000 = £323,400@three per cent;;kept: 592,900 − 323,400 = £269,500@clear"
Then the Bank of England. At a dividend of five and a half per cent, its profit is five hundred and ninety two thousand nine hundred a year. Borrow the capital at three per cent, and the state pays three hundred and twenty three thousand four hundred in interest. It would keep two hundred and sixty nine thousand five hundred clear.

@art trader-and-crown
Smith doesn't dispute the sum. He doubts a government like England's, never famous for good economy, could run the business. No two characters seem more inconsistent than those of trader and sovereign. The East India Company managed its trade well as traders, and as sovereigns had to beg the government to avoid bankruptcy.

@art post-office
The one exception he praises is the post office. The capital needed is small, there's no mystery in the business, and the returns are certain and immediate. It's the only mercantile project successfully managed by, he believes, every sort of government.

@chapter Land
@words text="Revenue above £10 million · land tax under £2 million"
Land was the great fund of ancient republics and kings, when war cost the state almost nothing. Now war is the main expense. Britain's ordinary revenue is above ten million a year, and the land tax at four shillings in the pound falls short of two million.

@art crown-forest
And crown lands, he says, are generally forest, a mere waste and loss of country. Where improved land sells at thirty years' purchase, these might well sell at forty, fifty or sixty.

@chapter Sell and redeem
@math heading="A crown estate yielding £250" items="price at 40 years: 250 · 40 = £10,000@forty years;;debt redeemed at 3%: £300 a year@three per cent;;300 − 250 = £50 better off@better off"
Here's the arithmetic, with an estate written for the lesson. It yields the crown two hundred and fifty pounds a year. At forty years' purchase, it sells for ten thousand. Pay off ten thousand of debt at three per cent, and that's three hundred a year of interest saved. The crown is fifty pounds better off.

@art years-purchase
Watch the direction. More years' purchase means a smaller return. Forty years is two and a half per cent, sixty years one and two thirds. And Smith guesses the crown lands yield not a fourth of what they could, so in private hands the same land might fetch thirty thousand.

@chapter The people lose more
@art rent-and-produce
Then the part to remember. The people's revenue from land follows not the rent, but the produce, and rent is scarce ever more than a third of it. Land paying ten million that could pay twenty has a produce of thirty that could be sixty. The owners lose ten. The country loses thirty.

@words text="Appears to cost nothing; costs society more"
So the crown's rent, which appears to cost nothing to individuals, in reality costs society more than perhaps any other equal revenue. His advice: sell the crown lands by auction, and keep only the parks. A century later Henry George argued the opposite, that the rent of land belongs to the community.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading it as a ban on all state business@The first|Taking more years' purchase as a higher return@The second|Counting the crown's rent as free@And the third"
The first slip is reading this as a ban on every state business. The post office is his exception. The second is taking more years' purchase for a higher return. It's lower. And the third is counting the crown's rent as free revenue.
