@chapter The question
@title text="Bankruptcy Disguised as Debasement" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 3" art=thumb
The last lesson ended with a debt that saving couldn't pay. So what happens to such debts in the end? Smith's answer, on the last pages of the book, is blunt.

@quote text="When national debts have once been accumulated to a certain degree, there is scarce, I believe, a single instance of their having been fairly and completely paid." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 3"
When national debts have once been accumulated to a certain degree, there is scarce, I believe, a single instance of their having been fairly and completely paid. Freedom from them has always come by a bankruptcy, sometimes avowed, but often by a pretended payment.

@chapter A sixpence called a shilling
@art sixpence-shilling
The usual trick is raising the denomination of the coin. Suppose a proclamation calls a sixpence a shilling, and twenty sixpences a pound. A man who borrowed twenty shillings, near four ounces of silver, repays with twenty sixpences, under two ounces.

@math heading="A debt of 128 million" items="owed: 128 · 4 = 512 million ounces@owed;;paid: 128 · 2 = 256 million ounces@paid;;loss: 1 − 2/4 = 1/2@loses"
Now a national debt of about a hundred and twenty eight million, near Britain's own. In silver, it's owed five hundred and twelve million ounces. It's paid with two hundred and fifty six million. The creditor loses half, defrauded of ten shillings in the pound.

@art ounces-owed-paid
And the harm spreads. Every private creditor suffers in proportion, enriching the idle and profuse debtor at the expense of the industrious and frugal creditor. If bankruptcy must come, a fair, open and avowed one is least dishonourable to the debtor, and least hurtful to the creditor.

@chapter The Roman as
@math heading="The Roman as" items="12 oz → 2 oz: 128 · 2/12 = 21⅓ million@twelve ounces;;→ ½ oz: 128 · (1/2)/12 = 5⅓ million@half an ounce"
There's ancient precedent. After the first Punic war, Rome reduced the as from twelve ounces of copper to two. A debt of a hundred and twenty eight million would be paid with a sixth, twenty one and a third million. In the second Punic war it fell to half an ounce, a twenty fourth, about five and a third million.

@art roman-as
Smith thinks the law was probably popular. Poor citizens were constantly in debt to the rich, who lent at exorbitant interest to buy their votes. A smaller as was, in effect, a law to pay debts in part.

@chapter Violence and fraud
@art alloy-mixed
You can debase by weight or by fineness. Raising the denomination is always open. Mixing in alloy, keeping the coin's look, is generally concealed. Both operations are unjust. But a simple augmentation is an injustice of open violence, while an adulteration is an injustice of treacherous fraud.

@chapter A union of the empire
@art union-of-empire
So what can Britain do instead? Smith calls his proposal a new Utopia, less amusing, but no more useless than the old one. Extend British taxes to Ireland and the American colonies, with a fair and equal representation in parliament.

@math heading="The same rate per head" items="10 / 8 = 1.25 a head@ten million;;1.25 · 13 = 16.25 million@thirteen million;;16.25 − 1 = 15.25 · less 9 = 6.25 for the debt@a million"
His estimate. Britain raises ten million on under eight million people, a pound and a quarter a head. The empire has thirteen million. At the same rate, sixteen and a quarter million. Take a million for Irish and colonial government, and Britain's nine million of expense, and six and a quarter million could go to the debt each year.

@chapter The project of an empire
@quote text="It has hitherto been, not an empire, but the project of an empire; not a gold mine, but the project of a gold mine" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 3"
If the colonies won't contribute, the only resource left is less expense. It has hitherto been, not an empire, but the project of an empire; not a gold mine, but the project of a gold mine. It has cost immense expense, without being likely to bring any profit.

@art golden-dream
So realise the golden dream, or awake from it. Smith ends the book telling Britain to accommodate her future to the real mediocrity of her circumstances. It appeared in March seventeen seventy six, as the fighting began. He couldn't know what followed, and historians still dispute his costs.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking debasement pays the debt@The first|Reading open bankruptcy as his advice@The second|Taking the last page as a forecast@And the third"
The first slip is thinking debasement pays the debt. It pays in name only. The second is reading an open bankruptcy as his advice. It's the least bad of bad courses. And the third is taking the last page as a forecast. He offered two ways out, not a prediction.
