@chapter The question
@title text="The Four Maxims of Taxation" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 2, Part II" art=thumb
Before Smith examines a single tax, he lays down four rules for judging one. Three of them sound obvious. The surprise is which one he ranks above the rest.

@art three-springs
He starts from Book One. Every private revenue comes from rent, profit or wages, so every tax must finally be paid from one of them. And many taxes, he warns, are not finally paid from the fund they were meant to fall on. The question for every tax is who hands the money over, and who finally loses it.

@chapter Equality
@art joint-tenants
The first maxim is equality. People should pay in proportion to their abilities, meaning the revenue they enjoy under the protection of the state. It's like the joint tenants of a great estate, each paying for its management in proportion to his interest. It's a rule of proportion, not of equal sums.

@words text="A tax on one sort of revenue only is necessarily unequal"
And a corollary that's easy to miss. Every tax that falls finally on only one of the three sorts of revenue is necessarily unequal, because it leaves the other two alone.

@chapter Certainty
@art tax-gatherer-door
The second is certainty. The time, manner and amount should be plain. Otherwise, everyone is put in the power of the tax gatherer, who can aggravate the tax on anyone he dislikes, or extort a present by threatening to.

@quote text="a very considerable degree of inequality, it appears, I believe, from the experience of all nations, is not near so great an evil as a very small degree of uncertainty" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 2, Part II"
Then the ranking. A very considerable degree of inequality, it appears, I believe, from the experience of all nations, is not near so great an evil as a very small degree of uncertainty. Predictability beats fairness.

@chapter Convenience and economy
@art little-by-little
The third is convenience. Levy a tax when people are most likely to have the means, like a tax on rent payable on rent day. A tax on luxuries is paid little by little, as the goods are bought, and since you may buy or not, it's your own fault if it ever troubles you much.

@art leaky-collection
The fourth is economy. A tax should take out of people's pockets as little as possible beyond what reaches the treasury. It can fail in four ways: a crowd of officers, a check to industry, penalties that ruin evaders, and the vexation of the tax gatherers' visits. The law, he says, first creates the temptation, and then punishes those who yield to it.

@chapter Trying the land tax
@words text="Fixed valuation · never afterwards altered"
Now a tax to test them on. The British land tax was assessed on each district at a fixed valuation, never to be altered. It's perfectly certain. It's paid with the rent, and collected by few officers. And since it doesn't rise with the rent, it doesn't discourage improvement. But it fails the first maxim.

@math heading="Valued at £1,000 · taxed at a fifth" items="tax: 1,000 / 5 = £200 every year@two hundred;;year 5: 200 / 1,800 = 1/9@a ninth;;on actual rents: 200 + 220 + 250 + 300 + 360 = £1,330@actual rent;;saved: 1,330 − 1,000 = £330@saved"
Here are rents written for the lesson. A district valued at a thousand pounds and taxed at a fifth pays two hundred a year, whatever happens. Let rents rise to eighteen hundred over five years, and the tax becomes just a ninth. A tax on the actual rent would have totalled thirteen hundred and thirty. So landlords saved three hundred and thirty.

@art fixed-and-rising
A fixed valuation, though equal at first, necessarily becomes unequal in time, as some districts improve and others decline. Since the revolution, Smith says, it's helped the landlord and hurt the sovereign. That was luck. Had rents fallen, it would have gone the other way.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading equality as equal sums@The first|Assuming equality ranks first@The second|Treating economy as collection cost only@And the third"
The first slip is reading equality as everyone paying the same sum. The second is assuming Smith ranks equality first. He lists it first, and ranks certainty above it. And the third is treating economy as collection cost only. Later, Mill read equality as equal sacrifice, and others as a rule of benefit.
