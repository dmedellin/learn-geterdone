@chapter Dearer beans
@title text="Price Fluctuations of Raw Materials" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 6" art=thumb
You've seen this happen to a coffee shop when coffee beans jump. Either the cup costs more, or the coffee shop earns less on it, or both.

@art coffee-cup
Marx's point goes a step further. Even if the cup's price rises by exactly the dearer beans, the coffee shop's rate of profit falls, because the same surplus is now made on a larger capital.

@quote text="Other circumstances remaining unchanged, the rate of profit falls and rises, therefore, inversely as the price of raw material." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 6"
The last lesson cut c by economy. This one lets the price of c's largest element, the raw material, move by itself.

@chapter Cotton doubles
@art cotton-doubles
Take Marx's four hundred c, a hundred v, a hundred s again, and suppose a hundred of the c is cotton. Now cotton doubles.

@math heading="£100 of cotton doubles" items="c:  400 + 100 = 500;  cost price 600@five hundred;;passed on in full:  price 700,  profit 100@seven hundred;;p′ = 100 / 600 = 1/6@one sixth"
Constant capital becomes five hundred, and the cost price six hundred. If the product sells at its new value, seven hundred, the profit is still a hundred, but on six hundred of capital. That's one sixth, down from a fifth. The rate falls even when the rise is passed on in full.

@words text="Cotton halves: 100 / 450 = 2/9"
It runs the other way too. Let cotton fall by half, so c is three fifty, and the product sells at five fifty. The rate rises to two ninths.

@chapter Passed on, or not?
@quote text="For this reason we note in reality that a rise in the price of raw material is not accompanied by a corresponding rise of the price of the product, nor a fall in the price of the raw material by a corresponding fall of that of the product." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 6"
Marx's reason is the market. A dearer product sells less, so its price is held back.

@art passed-share
So the lab asks how much of the rise reaches the product's price.

@math heading="Cost price £600 after the rise" items="none passed on:  price 600,  profit 0@none of it;;half passed on:  price 650,  profit 50,  p′ = 1/12@half"
Pass on none of it, and the sale is at the old six hundred, against a cost price of six hundred. The profit is nothing. Pass on half, and the sale is six fifty, a profit of fifty, a rate of one twelfth. The profit falls by exactly the part of the rise not passed on.

@art waste-pile
Raw material weighs heavily because it passes its whole value in one turnover, while machinery passes only its wear. And waste rises with its price. With fifteen per cent waste, cotton at three and a half pence loses half a penny a pound. In the famine, at two shillings, the loss was sixpence.

@chapter Stocks gain
@art blackburn-stock
There's a qualification for a capital already at work. When cotton rises, the cotton in stock and the yarn and cloth already made rise in value with it. That can make good the fall in the rate, or even more than make it good. Inspectors saw it in Blackburn at the start of the famine.

@words text="2,000c + 400v + 600s: 25% · 2,000c + 500v + 500s: 20%"
Wages work the same way. Two thousand c, four hundred v and six hundred s is twenty five per cent. If the same workers cost five hundred, it's twenty per cent, and an existing capital must tie up a hundred more just to keep its machinery running.

@chapter The cotton famine
@art idle-mills
Then the history. The American Civil War cut off supply, and cotton that had cost five or six pence a pound went to two shillings. In late eighteen sixty two, about half the cotton workers in Lancashire and Cheshire had no work at all.

@words text="Extended only convulsively"
Marx reads the cycle as built in. Machinery can be multiplied quickly, cotton only at the pace of a harvest. He grants that a high price calls out new supply, and Indian and Egyptian cotton arrived. But it comes late and all at once, so raw material production expands only convulsively, then contracts violently when the price falls.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A rise is always passed on in full@The first|Passed on means the rate is unchanged@The second|A rise always hurts the capitalist@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a raw material rise is always passed on in full. The second is expecting the rate to stay put when it is passed on. It still falls, to one sixth. And the third is concluding a rise always hurts the capitalist, when stocks on hand gain in value.
