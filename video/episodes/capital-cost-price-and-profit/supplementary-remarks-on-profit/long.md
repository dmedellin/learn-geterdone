@chapter The capitalist's refusal
@title text="Supplementary Remarks on Profit" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 7" art=thumb
This chapter is short, and partly unfinished. It grants that a sphere's profit equals the surplus value produced in it. Then it gives the capitalist's reasons for not seeing it that way.

@quote text="Nevertheless the bourgeois will not consider his profit as identical with the surplus-value, that is to say, with unpaid surplus-labor." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 7"
So what follows are the reasons he won't see it.

@art sale-makes-it
The first reason is the one Torrens gave in the first lesson. He forgets the process of production in the process of circulation. The surplus is realised by selling, so it seems to come from selling.

@art blank-page
And there the manuscript stops. Engels notes in brackets that there's a blank at this place, where Marx meant to dwell on the point in detail. You're reading a draft.

@chapter Business ability
@art two-businesses
The second reason is the one the chapter develops. Two businesses each pay a thousand pounds of wages, and get a thousand of surplus value. So the same number of workers are exploited to the same degree. But A works with nine thousand of constant capital, and B with eleven thousand.

@math heading="v = £1,000 · s = £1,000 for both" items="A:  1,000 / (9,000 + 1,000) = 10%@ten per cent;;B:  1,000 / (11,000 + 1,000) = 8⅓%@eight and a third"
A's rate of profit is a thousand over ten thousand, ten per cent. B's is a thousand over twelve thousand, eight and a third per cent. Same exploitation, different rates.

@art manager-desk
The difference may be due wholly to business ability, Marx says. He doesn't deny the ability. His point is about what the capitalist concludes from it.

@quote text="And this fact leads the capitalist to the conviction that his profits are due, not to the exploitation of labor, but at least, in part, to other circumstances independent of that exploitation, particularly to his individual activity." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 7"
A real difference in the rate is read as the source of the profit itself.

@chapter The books and the analysis
@art ledger-and-analysis
The lab puts the two readings side by side, on the first lesson's commodity: four hundred c, a hundred v, a hundred s, sold at its value of six hundred.

@math heading="One sale, two accounts" items="books:  cost price £500,  profit £100,  rate 1/5@the books;;analysis:  s = £100 from v = £100,  s′ = 100%@the analysis"
The books show a cost price of five hundred, a profit of a hundred, and a return of a fifth. The analysis reads the same hundred as surplus value, produced by a hundred of variable capital, at a hundred per cent.

@words text="Every number agrees"
Every number agrees. What differs is what the hundred is called, and what it's taken to be a return on.

@chapter Rodbertus and size
@words text="Bigger capital, proportionally bigger profit?" sub="Rodbertus"
The chapter then turns on Rodbertus, the Prussian landowner and economist, who argued that a larger capital earns a proportionally larger profit, so size can't change the rate. Marx says that's true in only two cases.

@art gold-rescale
The first is a change in the value of gold. A capital of a hundred pounds earning twenty becomes a hundred and fifty earning thirty. Nothing real has changed, only its money name, and the rate stays at a fifth.

@words text="1,000 → 200 · 2,000 → 400 · 500 → 100"
The second is a change of scale with the composition unchanged. Capitals of a thousand, two thousand and five hundred at the same composition earn two hundred, four hundred and a hundred. In every other case, size comes with a change in composition, and the rate moves, as business B's extra two thousand of c showed.

@chapter Might he be right?
@words text="Is the profit his achievement?"
The obvious objection is that the capitalist may simply be right. If capital is productive and management earns its reward, A's higher rate is A's achievement. The chapter doesn't argue that. It explains why he believes it. Whether it's an illusion rests on volume one's account of where the surplus came from.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Sold at value means no profit@The first|Higher p′ means more exploitation@The second|Size never affects the rate@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a capitalist who sells at value makes no profit. Sale at value realises the whole surplus value. The second is reading a higher rate of profit as more exploitation. A and B exploit equally. And the third is assuming size never affects the rate.

@chapter Try it
@words text="£100 earning £20 → re-priced at £150"
Your turn. Gold's value changes so a capital of a hundred pounds earning twenty is now expressed as a hundred and fifty. What's its profit, and its rate of profit? Then compare businesses A and B, and say where they differ.
