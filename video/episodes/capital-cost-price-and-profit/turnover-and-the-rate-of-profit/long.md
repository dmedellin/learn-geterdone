@chapter Engels' chapter
@title text="The Effect of Turnover on the Rate of Profit" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 4" art=thumb
Marx left only the title of this chapter, and Engels wrote it himself. The whole thing is printed in brackets and signed F E. So read it as Engels applying volume two's turnover to volume three's rate of profit, in his own voice.

@art faster-world
He opens with the world he was living in. Production got shorter with the Bessemer and Siemens methods in steel, and dye made from coal tar in weeks. Circulation got shorter with railways, steamships, the telegraph and the Suez Canal. A shipment to Eastern Asia took at least twelve months as late as eighteen forty seven, and now almost as many weeks.

@chapter The pure case
@art twice-and-once
Then the pure case. Capital A is eighty c and twenty v, a hundred in all, at a hundred per cent, turned over twice a year. Capital B is a hundred and sixty c and forty v, two hundred in all, turned over once.

@math heading="s′ = 100%" items="A:  40 of s on 100 advanced = 40%@hundred advanced;;B:  40 of s on 200 advanced = 20%@forty on two hundred"
Both produce forty of surplus value in the year. But A's forty is reckoned on the hundred advanced, not the two hundred turned over, so its annual rate is forty per cent. B's is forty on two hundred, twenty per cent.

@words text="Rates of profit inversely as turnover periods"
With the same composition and the same rate of surplus value, the rates of profit are inversely proportioned to their periods of turnover.

@quote text="Thus we obtain p' = s'n v/C, which is the formula for the calculation of the annual rate of profit." cite="Engels, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 4"
So the annual rate of profit is s prime, times n, times v over C, where n is the number of turnovers of the variable capital.

@chapter Fixed capital stays in C
@art fixed-in-c
Engels' capital one has ten thousand of fixed capital, five hundred of circulating constant capital and five hundred of variable, with v turned over ten times a year.

@math heading="Capital I · s′ = 100%" items="s in a year:  500 · 10 = 5,000@five thousand;;C = 10,000 + 500 + 500 = 11,000@eleven thousand;;annual p′ = 5,000 / 11,000 = 5/11@five elevenths"
In a year, v pays five thousand of wages and produces five thousand of surplus value, on a capital of eleven thousand. That's an annual rate of five elevenths.

@art halved-turns
Turn its v over only five times, and the year's surplus halves to twenty five hundred. The rate falls to five twenty seconds. The ten thousand of machinery hasn't changed, and it's in C all the time.

@words text="Capital III: 6,000c + 5,000v, once → 5/11"
And capital three, with no fixed capital, six thousand constant and five thousand variable turned over once, gets the same five thousand on the same eleven thousand. Ten turnovers against one, and the same rate. Composition still matters.

@chapter A Manchester mill
@art cash-box
Why does this matter in practice? In most cases, Engels says, the capitalist doesn't know the amount of variable capital in his business. Wages and cotton come from the same cash box, and his books show the wages of the year, v times n, not v.

@art spinning-mill
So Engels does it for the Manchester mill of ten thousand spindles from volume one. Each week, twenty pounds of wear, three hundred and fifty eight of materials, fifty two of wages and eighty of surplus value. The machinery is worth ten thousand, and he assumes twenty five hundred of circulating capital.

@math heading="The mill's year" items="v = £318;  52 · 52 = 2,704 of wages → n ≈ 8½@three hundred and eighteen;;s in a year:  52 · 80 = 4,160@forty one sixty;;p′ = 4,160 / 12,500 = 33.28%@thirty three"
Of that, three hundred and eighteen is variable. A year's wages are fifty two times fifty two, twenty seven hundred and four, so the variable capital turns over about eight and a half times. A year's surplus is fifty two times eighty, forty one sixty. On twelve thousand five hundred, that's about thirty three per cent.

@chapter What's disputed
@words text="Why it works is the dispute"
The arithmetic isn't in dispute. Any accountant sees a faster capital earning more on what it advances. What's Marx's and Engels' own is the reason: v working more often. An economist who counts every part of capital as productive credits the circulating constant capital too, which in Engels' examples turns over just as fast.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Turnover only affects circulating capital@The first|Dividing by the capital turned over@The second|More turnovers always means a higher rate@And the third"
The first slip is thinking turnover affects only the circulating capital's profit. The rate is on the whole capital. The second is dividing by the capital turned over, not advanced. And the third is expecting more turnovers always to mean a higher rate.
