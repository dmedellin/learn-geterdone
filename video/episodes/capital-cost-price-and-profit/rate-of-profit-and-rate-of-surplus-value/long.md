@chapter Algebra from Cambridge
@title text="The Rate of Profit and the Rate of Surplus Value" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 3" art=thumb
This chapter is algebra, and it has a history. Engels had a manuscript from the eighteen seventies working out the two rates in equations, and gave it to Samuel Moore, who'd graduated from Cambridge in mathematics.

@art moore-equations
Engels finished the chapter from Moore's summary, and you can feel it in the prose. The analysis, it says, is first carried on purely on the field of mathematics.

@chapter The equation
@math heading="Two factors" items="s = s′ · v@times v;;p′ = s / C = s′ · v / C@Put that into;;p′ : s′ = v : C@as v is to C"
Here's the equation. Surplus value s is the rate of surplus value times v. Put that into the rate of profit, s over C, and you get s prime times v over C. As a proportion, the rate of profit is to the rate of surplus value as v is to C.

@art two-factors
So the rate of profit is the rate of surplus value, scaled down by the share of wages in the capital. If a capitalist advanced only wages, the two rates would be equal. Marx calls that practically impossible.

@chapter Change c alone
@art more-c
First case: hold the rate of surplus value and v fixed, and change c.

@math heading="s′ = 100% in all three" items="80c + 20v + 20s:  p′ = 20%@eighty c;;100c + 20v + 20s:  p′ = 16⅔%@a hundred c;;60c + 20v + 20s:  p′ = 25%@sixty c"
Marx's three capitals: eighty c, a hundred c, and sixty c, each with twenty v and twenty s. Their rates of profit are twenty per cent, sixteen and two thirds, and twenty five. The surplus value doesn't change at all. It's just spread over more or less capital.

@chapter Change s′ alone
@art more-s
Second case: hold the composition fixed and change the rate of surplus value. Marx's capital of eighty c and twenty v, with forty s, has a rate of surplus value of two hundred per cent and a rate of profit of forty. With ten s, fifty per cent and ten.

@words text="Profits high or low as wages low or high" sub="Ricardo, 1817"
With the composition fixed, the rate of profit moves exactly with the rate of surplus value. Marx says this case, wages varying with everything else fixed, is the only one in which Ricardo was right that profits rise exactly as wages fall.

@chapter Both at once
@math heading="Wages fall from 20 to 16" items="80c + 16v + 24s:  s′ = 150%,  p′ = 25%@a hundred and fifty"
Now both. Start from eighty c, twenty v, twenty s, and let wages fall so the same workers cost sixteen. The rate of surplus value becomes a hundred and fifty per cent, and the rate of profit twenty five.

@words text="104c + 16v + 24s · p′ = 20%" sub="c/v: 4 → 13/2"
For the rate of profit to stay at twenty, c would have to rise to a hundred and four. Then c over v goes from four to thirteen halves.

@art offset
The rate of surplus value has risen by half, and the rate of profit hasn't moved. Marx says such an exact offset happens only by accidental coincidence within one country. Between countries, the same rate of profit rests, as a rule, on different rates of surplus value.

@art overshoot
And the offset can overshoot. Marx's capital of ninety c, ten v and fifteen s has a rate of surplus value of a hundred and fifty, and a rate of profit of fifteen. Beside eighty, twenty and twenty, exploitation has risen from a hundred per cent to a hundred and fifty, and the rate of profit has fallen from twenty to fifteen.

@chapter Why it matters later
@words text="Does a rising composition lower the rate?" sub="Tugan-Baranovsky, 1905 · Okishio, 1961"
Later in the book, Marx argues the rate of profit tends to fall as c grows faster than v. Tugan Baranovsky argued a rising composition need not lower it, and Okishio proved a cost cutting technique can't, with the real wage fixed. This chapter doesn't settle that. It supplies the arithmetic both sides use.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Higher s′ must mean higher p′@The first|Percentages show amounts@The second|More c changes the mass of s@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a rising rate of surplus value must raise the rate of profit. It only does if the composition stays put. The second is reading a change in percentages as a change in amounts. And the third is expecting the mass of surplus value to change when only c changes.
