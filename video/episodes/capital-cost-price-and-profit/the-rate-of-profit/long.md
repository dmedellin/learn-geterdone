@chapter One surplus, two rates
@title text="The Rate of Profit" sub="Capital, Volume III, Chapter 2" art=thumb
If you put money in a savings account, you think of the interest as a percentage of all of it. Nobody asks which pound earned it.

@art savings-pot
That's how the capitalist reckons too, and Marx thinks he has a reason. He can't exploit labour without advancing machines and materials, and can't use the machines without hiring labour. So he lumps them all together in his imagination.

@quote text="The rate of surplus-value measured by the variable capital is called rate of surplus-value. The rate of surplus-value measured by the total capital is called rate of profit." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 2"
That's the whole definition. It doesn't introduce a new quantity.

@art two-rulers
Both rates have the same top, s. They differ in what s is divided by. Against v, the wages, it's s prime, s over v. Against the whole capital, c plus v, it's p prime. Because the whole capital is bigger than v, the rate of profit is always smaller.

@chapter Three capitals
@math heading="Marx's capital" items="C = 400 + 100 = £500@five hundred;;s′ = 100 / 100 = 100%@a hundred per cent;;p′ = 100 / 500 = 20%@twenty per cent"
Take Marx's capital from the last lesson: four hundred c, a hundred v, a hundred s. The capital is five hundred. The rate of surplus value is a hundred per cent. The rate of profit is twenty per cent.

@art three-capitals
Now keep the capital at five hundred and the surplus value at a hundred, and just move money between c and v.

@math heading="£500 capital, £100 of surplus value" items="300c + 200v:  s′ = 50%,  p′ = 20%@three hundred c;;400c + 100v:  s′ = 100%,  p′ = 20%@four hundred c;;450c + 50v:  s′ = 200%,  p′ = 20%@four hundred and fifty"
With three hundred c and two hundred v, the rate of surplus value is fifty per cent. With four hundred c and a hundred v, a hundred. With four hundred and fifty c and fifty v, two hundred. The rate of profit is twenty per cent in all three.

@words text="The rate of profit may remain unchanged" sub="and yet express different rates of surplus value"
A worker at the third firm does four times as much unpaid labour per pound of wages as one at the first. The rate of profit can't tell them apart.

@chapter The whole capital
@art machine-denominator
Now add what the first lesson set aside. Marx's instruments were worth twelve hundred pounds, and the rate of profit is reckoned on the whole capital advanced, machines included.

@math heading="With £1,200 of machinery" items="C = 1,200 + 380 + 100 = £1,680@sixteen eighty;;p′ = 100 / 1,680 = 5/84@five eighty fourths;;s′ = 100%@still"
Twelve hundred of machines and three hundred and eighty of materials, plus a hundred of wages, is sixteen eighty. The same hundred of surplus value is now five eighty fourths, under six per cent. The rate of surplus value is still a hundred per cent.

@words text="Cost price: wear only · rate of profit: all of it"
Marx notes that this is the one difference the rate of profit does show: fixed against circulating capital. Fixed capital enters the cost price only by its wear, but the rate of profit counts all of it.

@chapter Which comes first
@words text="Deduce profit from surplus value, not vice versa"
Marx says the transformation of surplus value into profit must be deduced from the rates, starting with the rate of surplus value. Historically, inquiry started from the rate of profit, because that's visible. Surplus value is the invisible essence.

@art cotton-dearer
His illustration is a spinner. What matters for the surplus is the quantity of cotton the labour needs, not its price. Make cotton dearer, and the same surplus labour sits on a larger capital. The surplus is unchanged, and the rate of profit falls.

@quote text="Profit is, therefore, that disguise of surplus-value which must be removed before the real nature of surplus-value can be discovered." cite="Marx, Capital, Volume III, Chapter 2"
That's Marx's conclusion.

@art disguise
Not every economist agrees. Senior called profit the reward of abstinence, and Bohm-Bawerk later explained interest by time preference. On those views, a rate on the whole capital is the natural measure, not a disguise. The lab computes both rates and leaves the dispute open.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="p′ measures exploitation@The first|Profit and surplus differ in amount@The second|Dividing by the cost price@And the third"
The first slip is reading the rate of profit as the degree of exploitation. The second is thinking profit and surplus value differ in amount here. In this part they're the same; only the rates differ. And the third is dividing by the cost price instead of the whole capital advanced.
