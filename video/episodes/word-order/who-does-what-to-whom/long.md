@chapter Endings or order
@title text="Who Does What to Whom" sub="Word Order" art=thumb
The dog bit the man. The man bit the dog. Same words, and only the order tells you who did the biting.

@art endings-vs-order
In many languages, the words can move. In Spanish, a small word marks the man as the one bitten. Russian puts the job in an ending on the noun. Japanese and Korean use a small word after the noun. English, for the most part, does none of this. It keeps the order instead.

@art arrow-action
So in a plain English statement with nouns, the one before the verb is the subject, and the one after is who it happens to. Your own language may not work this way, so say it slowly at first.

@chapter What English kept
@math heading="Pronoun pairs" items="I  ·  me@I and me;;he  ·  him@He and him;;she  ·  her@She and her;;we  ·  us@We and us;;they  ·  them@They and them"
Old English had endings on nouns, and almost all of them are gone. What's left is a small set of pronoun pairs. I and me. He and him. She and her. We and us. They and them.

@art pronoun-pairs
The first of each pair is the subject. The second is who or what the action happens to. So he saw them can't mean the reverse. Her is the one to watch: it also means belonging, as in her book.

@chapter A rule a machine can test
@words text="subject pronoun → its verb" sub="I · he · she · we · they"
Because the pronoun carries the role, there's a rule that needs no reading of meaning. A subject pronoun is followed by its verb. A machine can look for he, and read the next word.

@words text="you · it" sub="the same in both roles, so left out"
You and it look the same whether they're the subject or not. So they can't be scored this way, and they're left out.

@chapter The score
@math heading="Pride and Prejudice" items="passage, 936 words: 72 of 80  ·  90.0%@In seventy-two;;whole novel, quoted: 5,990 pronouns  ·  88.7%@eighty-eight point seven"
On a passage of nine hundred and thirty-six words from Pride and Prejudice, there are eighty subject pronouns. In seventy-two of them, the next word is a verb. That's ninety percent. Over the whole novel, counted once, it's eighty-eight point seven percent.

@art four-groups
It's easy to read the other tenth wrongly, as a failure rate, or as noise. It's neither. It's a few things, and each one has a name.

@chapter The four groups
@list heading="The misses, whole novel" items="Modifier between · 63%@a modifier|Word not in the list · 16%@not in the list|Speech tag · 15%@speech tag|Other · 6%@other"
In the whole novel, a modifier sits in between in sixty-three percent of the misses. The next word is not in the list in sixteen percent. A speech tag is fifteen percent. And six percent are other.

@math heading="Examples" items="they both knew  ·  we all agreed  ·  I almost envy you@They both knew;;I interrupt  ·  I don't know@I interrupt;;said he  ·  returned she@said he"
They both knew. We all agreed. I almost envy you. A small word sits between, and the verb is still the next main word. I interrupt, and I don't know, are words the tool's list doesn't hold. And said he, returned she, are speech tags.

@art speech-tag
A speech tag only happens beside quoted speech. It's a habit of telling a story, not an error. And it's an old one. In speech today, you say he said.

@chapter What was not found
@words text="no pronoun in a place English does not allow" sub="among the cases read"
Here's the result worth knowing. Among the cases that were read, there was no sentence where a subject pronoun stood in a place English doesn't allow. Every apparent exception was one of the groups.

@art old-vs-modern
Why a novel from the eighteen tens? Two modern documents have about six point one times fewer subject pronouns per thousand words. Formal modern writing is mostly nouns. So if you only read formal writing, read stories and speech too.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Copying said he as a new word order@The first|Taking they both knew as a break@The second|Moving words the way your own language would@the third"
The first slip is copying said he as a new word order. The second is taking a modifier, like they both knew, as a break in the rule. And the third is moving the words the way your own language would. The pronoun form only saves you if you keep the order.

@chapter Try it
@list heading="Read who does what" items="Look for a pronoun first@Look for a pronoun|No pronoun? Use the order@use the order|Allow a small word between@Allow a small word|Check for quoted speech@quoted speech"
Your turn. Take any sentence. Look for a pronoun first. If there's no pronoun, use the order: the noun before the verb is the subject. Allow a small word in between. And check for quoted speech.
