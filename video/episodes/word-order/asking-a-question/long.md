@chapter The rule
@title text="Asking a Question" sub="Word Order" art=thumb
Every learner is taught how to ask a question in English. It's an old rule and a real one, and you should use it. But let's also see how much of real writing it covers.

@art verb-jumps
Here's the rule. A helping verb goes before the subject. If there's no helping verb, add do. And if there's a question word, it goes first. Can you go? Did you go? Where did you go?

@chapter Step by step
@math heading="Statement to question" items="she can go  →  can she go?@she can go;;she has gone  →  has she gone?@she has gone;;she went  →  did she go?@she went;;she went where  →  where did she go?@where did she go"
Look for a helping verb, and move it in front of the subject. She can go becomes can she go. She has gone becomes has she gone. If there's none, add do. She went becomes did she go. And she went where becomes where did she go. The main verb goes back to its bare form, and do takes the tense.

@words text="who went?" sub="the question word is the subject: nothing moves"
The question word goes first. But when the question word is itself the subject, nothing moves. Who went?

@art languages-differ
Many languages ask with a small word at the end, like Mandarin or Japanese, or by the voice alone. In Spanish or Russian, the subject and verb may swap with no help word. English moves a word. That's why the rule is worth stating.

@chapter The score
@math heading="90 questions from Pride and Prejudice" items="52 follow the rule  ·  57.8%@fifty-two;;whole novel, quoted: 55.4%@fifty-five point four"
The rule was scored on ninety questions from Pride and Prejudice. It covers fifty-two of them. That's fifty-seven point eight percent. Over the whole novel, counted once, it's fifty-five point four. Too low to call the rule good, and too high to call it wrong.

@art spread-of-misses
So what do the other four in ten look like? Here the misses aren't a small set. They're a spread, and the lesson is in reading them.

@chapter Five kinds of miss
@math heading="38 misses" items="joining word first  ·  16  ·  42.1%@sixteen;;question word, no helping verb after  ·  7@seven;;a pronoun first  ·  4@four;;a small word of address first  ·  2@two;;something else  ·  9@nine"
There are thirty-eight misses. Sixteen begin with a joining word, forty-two point one percent. Seven begin with a question word and no helping verb after it. Four begin with a pronoun. Two begin with a small word of address. And nine are something else.

@art five-trays
The whole novel, counted once, found a joining word first in forty-five percent of its misses. Ninety lines are a sample, so the numbers differ a little, but the picture is the same.

@quote text="And what is your success?" cite="Pride and Prejudice"
A joining word stands first. And what is your success? But are you pleased, Jane? A check at the front misses these.

@art joining-first
But in most of them, the rest of the question follows the rule. And do you really know all this? Only a few don't: but why all this secrecy has no verb at all.

@quote text="What say you, Mary?" cite="Pride and Prejudice"
What say you, Mary? is a form older English used without do. And in what made you so shy of me, the question word is the subject, so nothing moves.

@quote text="I must ask whether you were surprised?" cite="Pride and Prejudice"
This one is a statement with a question mark on it. Then there's oh, why is not everybody as happy? The oh stands in front, and the rule follows. And Miss Bennet, do you know who I am? begins with a name.

@chapter No questions in reports
@art empty-page
Two modern documents, a court opinion and a government news story, were searched for a question mark. In seventeen hundred and twenty-three words, there are none. Formal writing hardly holds a question at all.

@words text="Read stories and plays too"
So a learner who reads only reports and textbooks will almost never see the form they need most when they talk. The next lesson scores the same rule on a play.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="You know him? for every question@The first|Do you can go? ✗  Can you go? ✓@The second|Dropping the rule because of 57.8%@the third"
The first slip is asking by tone alone, for every question. You know him? is fine between friends, but learn do you know him? as the plain form. The second is adding do with a helping verb: do you can go? And the third is dropping the rule because of the score.

@chapter Try it
@words text="She went." sub="make it a question"
Your turn. Make she went a question. There's no helping verb, so add do, in the past: did. And went goes back to its bare form, go. Did she go?
