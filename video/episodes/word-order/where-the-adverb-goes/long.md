@chapter A favourite place
@title text="Where the Adverb Goes" sub="Word Order" art=thumb
Ask a learner to say I always drink tea, and you may hear I drink always tea, or always I drink tea. Both are understood. But neither is what a speaker would choose.

@art middle-slot
Words that tell how often, like always, usually, often, sometimes, never and ever, are called frequency adverbs. English has a favourite home for them: the middle of the verb phrase.

@chapter Three places
@math heading="The middle of the verb phrase" items="before the main verb  ·  she always knew@Before the main verb;;after the first helping verb  ·  she has always known@After the first helping verb;;after be  ·  she is always late@After be"
In the middle, there are three places. Before the main verb: she always knew. After the first helping verb: she has always known. And after be: she is always late.

@art three-places
Notice that the word never goes between the verb and its object. The verb and what it acts on stay next to each other, and the frequency word slips in before them.

@art be-after
Be works differently. With be, the word comes after: she is always late, they were never ready. With every other main verb, it comes before. That's the one most learners miss.

@words text="Your language may differ" sub="the rule is the same for everyone"
A speaker of Spanish may find the first place close to home. A speaker of Japanese or Korean, where the verb comes last, may expect the word near the front of the clause. The rule is the same for everyone.

@chapter Step by step
@list heading="Put the word in place" items="Find the verb phrase@Find the verb phrase|With be: after it@With be|With a helping verb: after the first one@With a helping verb|No helping verb: before the main verb@no helping verb"
Find the verb phrase: the main verb and any helping verbs before it. With be, put the word after it. With a helping verb, put the word after the first one: they would never agree. With no helping verb, put it before the main verb: he often asked.

@chapter The score
@math heading="Pride and Prejudice" items="120 printed lines: 85 in the middle  ·  70.8%@eighty-five;;always: 15 of 16  ·  sometimes: 7 of 16@always is in the middle"
On one hundred and twenty lines from Pride and Prejudice, the word sits in one of the three places eighty-five times, seventy point eight percent. Always is in the middle in fifteen lines of sixteen. Sometimes only seven of sixteen, because it so often opens or closes a clause.

@words text="whole novel, quoted: 472 of 577 · 81.8%"
Over the whole novel, counted once, it's four hundred and seventy-two of five hundred and seventy-seven. That's eighty-one point eight percent.

@chapter What the misses were
@list heading="105 misses, whole novel" items="Verb not in the tool's list · 51@fifty-one|Inside a phrase · 23@twenty-three|At the end · 10  ·  at the start · 7@ten were at the end|Other · 14@fourteen"
That leaves one hundred and five misses in the novel. Fifty-one had a verb the tool's list didn't hold, so it couldn't find the middle. Twenty-three were inside a phrase. Ten were at the end and seven at the start. And fourteen were other.

@art phrase-unit
Never in my life is a fixed unit. The rule is about single words, not phrases. And the fourteen other include uses of still and already that have nothing to do with how often.

@art end-and-start
So only seventeen are plainly a different place, at the end or the start. English allows that, and it often adds weight to the word. Most of the rest aren't failures of place at all.

@chapter On this page
@math heading="35 misses on the printed lines" items="they always contrived  ·  who still resides@they always contrived;;as soon as@as soon as;;more than usually insolent@more than usually insolent"
The thirty-five misses on the printed lines are the same groups in small. They always contrived, and who still resides, are in the right place. The scan just doesn't know the verb. Three are as soon as. Five put the word before an adjective, like more than usually insolent.

@art few-in-reports
The two modern documents have just two of these words in seventeen hundred and twenty-three words. The novel has about four point seven per thousand. The middle place is a rule of speech and stories more than reports.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="she knew always ✗@The first|she knew always the answer ✗@The second|she always is late ✗  she is always late ✓@the third"
The first slip is putting the word at the end: she knew always. The second is putting it between the verb and its object: she knew always the answer. The verb and its object stay together. And the third is forgetting that be works differently.

@words text="She is late always · She is always late · Always she is late" sub="which one follows the rule?"
Your turn. Which follows the rule? She is late always. She is always late. Always she is late. It's after be: she is always late.
