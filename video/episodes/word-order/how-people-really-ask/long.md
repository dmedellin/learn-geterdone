@chapter Written to be said
@title text="How People Really Ask" sub="Word Order" art=thumb
The question rule covers fifty-seven point eight percent of the questions in a novel. But a novel is written to be read. What happens to the rule on a text that's written to be said?

@art stage
Take The Importance of Being Earnest, a play by Oscar Wilde, first staged in eighteen ninety-five. It isn't recorded speech. Wilde made up every line. But it's the nearest thing to talk a page can carry, and it asks a great many questions.

@math heading="Question marks per 1,000 words, quoted" items="the play  ·  13.1@thirteen point one;;the novel  ·  3.9@three point nine;;two modern documents  ·  none@none"
Counted once over each whole text, the play has thirteen point one question marks for every thousand words. The novel has three point nine. And the two modern documents have none.

@chapter The score
@math heading="256 questions from the play" items="89 follow the rule  ·  34.8%@eighty-nine;;the novel: 52 of 90  ·  57.8%@On the novel"
The play has two hundred and fifty-six questions of two words or more. The rule holds for eighty-nine of them. That's thirty-four point eight percent. On the novel, it held for fifty-two of ninety. The rule didn't change. The questions did.

@art kinds-sorted
That leaves one hundred and sixty-seven misses. They're not mistakes. They sort into kinds, by their first words.

@chapter The kinds
@math heading="167 misses" items="joining word first  ·  35  ·  21.0%@thirty-five;;a pronoun first  ·  27@twenty-seven;;question word, no helper straight after  ·  24@twenty-four;;a word of address first  ·  20@twenty;;no verb at all  ·  14@fourteen;;something else  ·  47@forty-seven"
Thirty-five begin with a joining word, twenty-one percent. Twenty-seven begin with a pronoun. Twenty-four begin with a question word that has no helping verb straight after it. Twenty begin with a word of address, like a name. Fourteen have no verb at all. And forty-seven are something else.

@chapter Six lines, six kinds
@math heading="From the play" items="And when was the engagement actually settled?@And when was;;Gwendolen, will you marry me?@Gwendolen, will you"
And when was the engagement actually settled? A joining word comes first, and then the rule follows. Gwendolen, will you marry me? starts with a name, and then it's a full question. Neither one breaks the rule. They just start with a word the rule doesn't mention.

@art name-first
A name, oh, or well can stand in front. Well, what shall we do? Miss Prism, you are, I trust, well? What follows may be a full question, or a statement.

@words text="How old are you?" sub="follows the rule: the count reads only old"
How old are you? follows the rule too. Are comes before you. But the count reads only the word straight after how, and old isn't a helping verb. Twelve of the twenty-four question word lines are like this.

@words text="You don't mean to say Gwendolen refused you?" sub="a statement with a question mark"
You don't mean to say Gwendolen refused you? This has the order of a statement. In print, only the question mark asks. On the stage, the voice would rise.

@art rising-voice
Twenty-seven lines start with a pronoun like this. I beg your pardon? is in the play three times. And in nine of them, a short tag at the end asks as well: you will marry me, won't you?

@math heading="Short pieces" items="Your guardian?@Your guardian;;A hand-bag?@A hand-bag"
Your guardian? Two words, no verb, a thing repeated back to the speaker. A hand-bag? is the same kind of thing. The count doesn't call it no verb, because hand and bag are on its verb lists.

@art repeated-back
A short piece like this works on the stage because someone has just said it. Said alone, it asks nothing. So use the full form when nothing came before.

@chapter How to sort a question
@list heading="Five steps" items="Read the first word@Read the first word|Set aside a name, oh or a joining word@Set aside|Pronoun first? A statement@pronoun comes first|No verb? A short piece@no verb|Look past a phrase after the question word@Look past"
Read the first word. If it's a helping verb, or a question word and a helping verb, it follows the rule. Set aside a name, oh, or a joining word, and read again. If a pronoun comes first, it's a statement. If there's no verb, it's a short piece. And look past a phrase after the question word.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading 34.8% as proof the rule is wrong@The first|Saying a hand-bag? with nothing before it@The second|Counting how old are you? as a break@the third"
The first slip is reading the low score as proof the rule is wrong. The second is copying a short piece without the talk around it. And the third is counting a question word and a phrase as a break in the rule. How old are you? follows it.

@chapter Keep the rule
@art two-tools
Keep the rule. It's how you ask a question that has to stand alone. Then learn to hear the other kinds. A name first, a joining word, a pronoun with a rising voice, a few words repeated back. They're not mistakes. They're most of what you'll be asked.
