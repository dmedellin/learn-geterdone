@chapter Who speaks for the church?
@title text="Eternal Life and the Church" sub="Leviathan, Chapters 38 and 39" art=thumb
In the sixteen forties, bishops, preachers, a Parliament, a king and the Scottish Presbyterians each claimed to speak for the church. The Scots held there were two kingdoms, and in the church the king was a member, not a master. Hobbes makes the opposite case.

@chapter Greater rewards than life
@words text="Greater rewards than life · greater punishments than death"
He starts with rewards. A commonwealth can't stand where anyone but the sovereign can give greater rewards than life, or greater punishments than death. Eternal life and eternal torment are exactly that. So what Scripture means by them is a political question.

@art eden-tree
His reading is distinctive, and he says so. Adam, if he hadn't sinned, would have had eternal life on earth, as the tree of life shows. Christ recovered that life for believers, and the texts about Zion and the new Jerusalem place it on earth, after the resurrection.

@art valley-fire
Of the wicked, he finds no text that gives any individual an eternal life in torment. Scripture speaks of a second death. The fire, the worm and the darkness are metaphors tied to places: the valley of Hinnom, Sodom, Egypt's three days of darkness.

@words text="Propounded, maintaining nothing"
He says he propounds this, maintaining nothing, and submits it to the Bible as his commonwealth authorises it. Critics like Bishop Bramhall and Clarendon took it as a denial of the immortal soul. Others have held his reading on Scripture alone, with no political reason.

@chapter What a church is
@art greek-assembly
Chapter thirty nine turns to the word. In the Greek cities, an ecclesia was an assembly of citizens called forth to hear the magistrate. Called by lawful authority, it was a lawful church. Called by tumult, a confused one.

@art crowd-without-authority
In Scripture, church means many things: a house, all Christians, the elect. But only a congregation can be one person. Without authority from a lawful congregation, whatever a crowd does is the act of each person present, not of all together.

@quote text="I define a CHURCH to be, A company of men professing Christian Religion, united in the person of one Soveraign; at whose command they ought to assemble, and without whose authority they ought not to assemble." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 39"
So he defines a church as a company of men professing Christian religion, united in the person of one sovereign, at whose command they ought to assemble, and without whose authority they ought not.

@art one-sovereign-two-names
Two things follow. There's no universal church on earth that all Christians must obey, since there's no power to which all commonwealths are subject. And a church that can command or judge is the same thing as a commonwealth of Christian men. Temporal and spiritual are two words brought in to make men see double.

@chapter Testing the definition
@math heading="Three letters" items="C: professes Christianity@C means;;S: one commonwealth, one sovereign@S;;W: acts by the sovereign's authority@W;;church = C and S and W@all three"
The lab turns the definition into a rule. C means the company professes Christianity. S, its members are one commonwealth under one sovereign. W, it acts by that sovereign's authority. A church needs all three.

@math heading="Christians alone" items="Christian commonwealth: C = 1, verdict 1: agrees@commonwealth;;crowd, no authority: C = 1, verdict 0: too broad@crowd;;all Christians under a pope: C = 1, verdict 0@pope;;heathen commonwealth: C = 0, verdict 0: agrees@heathen;;2 of 4@two of four"
Now try a looser definition, Christians alone. A Christian commonwealth: it agrees. A crowd of Christians with no authority: the formula admits it, the verdict refuses it, so it's too broad. All Christians under a pope: admitted again, wrongly. A heathen commonwealth: agrees. Two of four.

@words text="Roman reading: 3 of 4, too narrow"
Hobbes's three letters agree with all four. But count the universal church as a church, as the Roman preset does, and his definition gets three of four, too narrow. So the dispute is over the verdicts, not the logic.

@chapter The other side
@art pagan-emperor
A Catholic, a Presbyterian or an Independent would reply that the definition settles by a word what's really in dispute. The first Christians were a church under pagan emperors, with their own head and discipline, whatever sovereigns did.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Any Christian assembly is a church@The first|Hobbes denies sin is punished@The second|The table settles the dispute@And the third"
The first slip is thinking any Christian assembly counts. It must be united in one sovereign. The second is reading the hell chapter as denying sin is punished. He denies eternal torment, not punishment. And the third is thinking the table settles it. The cases are readings.
