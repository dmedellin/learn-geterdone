@chapter What makes us wonder
@title text="Miracles and Their Use" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 37" art=thumb
Ask a friend if they believe in miracles, and you'll usually hear about wonders: a recovery nobody expected, a coincidence too good to be chance. Hobbes starts there, then tightens the word.

@list heading="Two things make men wonder" items="It is strange@strange|No natural cause can be imagined@natural means"
There are only two things, he says, that make men wonder at an event. That it's strange, and that we can't imagine a natural means for it.

@art speaking-horse
If a horse or a cow should speak, it would be a miracle. A man begetting a man isn't, though we know no more how that's done, because it's usual. And the first rainbow ever seen was a miracle, because it was the first. Rainbows now aren't.

@art eclipse
So the same thing may be a miracle to one man and not to another. The ignorant take an eclipse for a supernatural work. Those who can foretell the hour don't.

@chapter What a miracle is for
@quote text="A MIRACLE, is a work of God, (besides his operation by the way of Nature, ordained in the Creation,) done, for the making manifest to his elect, the mission of an extraordinary Minister for their salvation." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 37"
A miracle is a work of God, beyond his operation by way of nature, done to make manifest to his elect the mission of an extraordinary minister. So the creation and the flood were admirable works, but not miracles in this sense. They weren't done to credit any prophet.

@chapter The magicians of Egypt
@art rod-and-serpent
What about the magicians who turned rods into serpents? Hobbes says words have no effect on anyone who doesn't understand them. When a rod seems a serpent, nothing is enchanted. It's the spectator who's wrought upon. The enchanter has deceived a man, which is no miracle, but a very easy matter to do.

@art confederates
Stars foreknown, a juggler's tricks, ventriloquism. And confederacy. There is nothing, however impossible, that's impossible to be believed. Two men conspiring, one to seem lame and one to cure him, deceive many. Many conspiring, with the rest to bear witness, deceive many more.

@chapter The lab: one witness
@math heading="Priors, for illustration" items="miracle 1/100 · trick 49/100 · mistake 1/2@one report in a hundred;;witness says so: 9/10 · 9/10 · 1/10@nine times in ten"
So the lab weighs a reported wonder. The lesson supposes one report in a hundred is a miracle, forty nine a trick, and half are mistakes. A miracle or a trick would produce a witness nine times in ten. An honest mistake only one time in ten.

@math heading="One witness" items="miracle: 1/100 · 9/10 = 9/1000@nine in a thousand;;trick: 49/100 · 9/10 = 441/1000@four forty one;;mistake: 1/2 · 1/10 = 50/1000@fifty;;miracle share: 9/1000 ÷ 1/2 = 9/500@nine over five hundred"
Multiply. The miracle gets nine in a thousand, the trick four forty one, the mistake fifty. They total a half. So the miracle's share is nine over five hundred, one point eight percent. It's barely moved from one percent.

@art witness-scale
What the witness ruled out was the honest mistake, not the trick, because a trick produces witnesses as readily as a miracle does. Three witnesses only reach about two percent. And when the confederates always bear witness, the miracle falls to about one and a half percent. None of these rates are Hobbes's. He gives no probabilities.

@chapter Who judges
@words text="See it done, and consider whether it be really done"
Hobbes's advice is to see a pretended miracle done, and use every means to consider whether it really was, and whether no man could do it by natural power. In doubtful cases, we go to God's lieutenant, the head of the church.

@art public-reason
Today, he says, the question is no longer whether what we see is a miracle, but whether the report is true or a lie. And on that we follow the public reason, the reason of God's supreme lieutenant. But a private man keeps a liberty, because thought is free, to believe or not in his heart.

@words text="Hume: unless its falsehood were more miraculous"
A century later, Hume argued that no testimony is enough unless its being false would be more miraculous than what it reports. That's a different argument. A Bayesian would put the point in the prior, which in the lab is a number you can change.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Any wonder is a miracle@The first|More witnesses make it likely@The second|The probabilities are Hobbes's@And the third"
The first slip is calling any wonder a miracle. It has to credit a messenger. The second is thinking more witnesses make a miracle likely. They don't touch the trick. And the third is reading the lab's numbers as Hobbes's.
