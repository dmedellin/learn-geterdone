@chapter God and man
@title text="Power Ecclesiastical and What Is Necessary for Salvation" sub="Leviathan, Chapters 42 and 43" art=thumb
Picture a Christian in sixteen forties England. The king says one thing, Parliament another, the preacher a third, and each claims God's authority. Hobbes says the most frequent pretext of civil war has been the difficulty of obeying at once both God and man.

@words text="Obey both, without forfeiting life eternal?"
When two commands conflict and one is God's, God's must be obeyed. The trouble is knowing which is God's. So Hobbes asks what's necessary for salvation. If the sovereign's command can be obeyed without losing eternal life, then not obeying it is unjust.

@chapter Ministers teach
@art fishers-of-men
First, Chapter forty two asks what power a church's ministers have. Their commission was to preach, teach and baptise. So preachers have not magisterial, but ministerial power. Christ's kingdom isn't of this world, and his ministers are fishers, not hunters, of men. Compulsion is excluded.

@art schoolroom
And faith depends on arguments, not commands. Paul says, we have no dominion over your faith. So the apostles and ministers are our schoolmasters, not our commanders, and their precepts aren't laws, but wholesome counsels, unless a minister also holds civil authority.

@chapter The hard case
@art house-of-rimmon
What if a sovereign forbids belief in Christ? That has no effect, since faith is a gift of God no one can give or take away by rewards or torture. And if he commands you to profess what you don't believe? Profession is an outward act. Elisha let Naaman the Syrian go in peace, when he bowed in the house of Rimmon.

@art inward-outward
That action, Hobbes says, is not his, but his sovereign's. This is the passage that most infuriated his opponents. But read it as it is. It doesn't say conscience is nothing. It says conscience is inward, and the outward act belongs to the one who commands it.

@art peter-rock
The rest of Chapter forty two answers Cardinal Bellarmine, the strongest defender of the Pope's claim. Thou art Peter, and upon this rock, is a play on Peter's name, Hobbes says. The rock is the article that Jesus is the Christ. And if a pastor may compel kings, the Pope would be King of Kings.

@chapter What salvation needs
@quote text="All that is NECESSARY to Salvation, is contained in two Vertues, Faith in Christ, and Obedience to Laws." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 43"
Then Chapter forty three gives the answer Part Three has been building. All that is necessary to salvation is contained in two virtues: faith in Christ, and obedience to laws.

@list heading="Two virtues" items="Obedience: a serious endeavour to obey@Obedience|Faith: that Jesus is the Christ@one article"
Obedience means a serious endeavour to obey, since God accepts the will for the deed. And the only article of faith that Scripture makes simply necessary is one article: that Jesus is the Christ. Other articles, he says, are contained in it.

@chapter The lab's argument
@math heading="Bowing in the house of Rimmon" items="S ⟺ (F and G)@saved if and only if;;(C and G) → F@leaves the faith;;C,  G@commands;;∴ F, ∴ S: 16 rows, valid@sixteen rows"
The lab writes it in letters. S, saved. F, holds the faith. G, obeys his sovereign. C, the sovereign commands an outward act. Saved if and only if faith and obedience. Obeying an outward command leaves the faith. He commands, you obey. So you keep the faith, and you're saved. Sixteen rows, no counterexample.

@art covenant-banner
Now drop the premise that obeying leaves the faith, as a Covenanter would. One row survives: commanded and obeyed, faith false, not saved. For the Scottish Covenanters, Christ was head of the church, and confessing before men was part of faith. Add a further article that salvation needs, and again one row breaks it.

@chapter Who judges?
@words text="No contradiction between God's laws and a Christian commonwealth's"
So whoever obeys his Christian sovereign is not hindered from believing or obeying God. Could the king err in drawing consequences? Yes. But who else would judge? A private man, the Pope, or an apostle, who might also err? Under an infidel sovereign, faith is inward. You may suffer for it, but you shouldn't make war.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Hobbes says give up conscience@The first|The sovereign cannot err@The second|Valid defeats the Covenanters@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Hobbes asks you to give up conscience. Belief can't be commanded. The second is thinking he says the sovereign can't err. He says no one else may judge. And the third is reading validity as the Covenanters' defeat. They deny a premise.
