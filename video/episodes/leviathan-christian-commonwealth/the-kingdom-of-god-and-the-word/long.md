@chapter A lot turns on a word
@title text="The Kingdom of God and the Word of God" sub="Leviathan, Chapters 35 and 36" art=thumb
Say the kingdom of God is the church's spiritual rule over believers, and you've said some power besides the king governs Christians. Divines, Hobbes says, mean either heaven or grace in the heart. He finds something else in Scripture.

@quote text="by the Kingdome of God, is properly meant a Common-wealth, instituted (by the consent of those which were to be subject thereto) for their Civill Government" cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 35"
By the kingdom of God is properly meant a commonwealth, instituted by the consent of its subjects, for their civil government: their conduct toward God their King, toward one another in justice, and toward other nations in peace and war.

@chapter Israel's covenant
@art covenant-mountain
The evidence is mostly one passage of Exodus. If Israel keeps God's covenant, they'll be a peculiar people, a holy nation. All nations are God's by power, Hobbes reads it, but you shall be mine by your own consent and covenant. That reading, he says, gives too much light to Christian kings about their right of church government.

@art rejected-king
When the elders demanded a king, the Lord told Samuel, they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me. So God had been king. And Christ was put to death as a claimant to the title, King of the Jews. Thy kingdom come would be pointless if that kingdom had never been interrupted.

@art holy-vessels
From the kingdom comes holiness. What belongs to the king is public, so what belongs to God, king of Israel, is holy: the Sabbath, the temple, the tithes, the priests. A sacred thing is the king's, and who the king is decides who may consecrate.

@chapter Testing prophets
@art many-prophets
Chapter thirty six turns to the word of God and to prophets. Anyone who claims to teach men the way to happiness pretends to govern them, and is worth suspecting of ambition and imposture. So everyone must examine a prophet before obeying him. And the odds are poor.

@quote text="they were all false Imposters, but onely one Michaiah." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 36"
Ahab asked four hundred prophets, and they were all false impostors, but only one, Micaiah. Hobbes adds that impostors gain more by one casual event, wrested to their purpose, than they lose by any number of failings.

@chapter What one hit is worth
@math heading="One fulfilled prophecy" items="prior: 1/401 true, 400/401 false@one in four hundred and one;;chance of the sign: 1 if true, 1/10 if false@one in ten;;Bayes factor: 1 ÷ 1/10 = 10@ten;;posterior: 1/41@forty one"
The lab takes the lesson's count, four hundred prophets and Micaiah besides, so one in four hundred and one. A true prophet's word always comes to pass. A false one's comes true by chance, or by being wrested, one in ten. The factor is ten. Prior odds of one to four hundred become ten to four hundred, which is one in forty one.

@art balance-of-signs
So the sign made him ten times more likely to be true, and still only one chance in forty one. These numbers are the lesson's, not Hobbes's. They show what his two remarks imply: one hit is weak evidence, and a failure is decisive.

@math heading="The other presets" items="two signs: factor 100 → 1/5@two independent;;vague: factor 10/9 → 1/361@vague;;failed: true prophet's chance 0 → 0@fails"
With two independent prophecies fulfilled, the factors multiply to a hundred, and the chance is one in five. If the prophecy's vague, so a false prophet fulfils it nine times in ten, the factor is ten ninths, and the chance is one in three sixty one. And if it fails, the true prophet is ruled out.

@chapter The sovereign prophet
@art heart-and-tongue
The last step is Hobbes's rule. Every subject must try a prophet by his own reason, but must also look to the sovereign prophet, who for Christians is the sovereign. A critic says that's a freedom the sovereign can take away. Hobbes would answer that the freedom is of the heart, since thought is free, and the deference is of the tongue.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="The kingdom of God is only heaven@The first|A fulfilled prophecy is proof@The second|The base rate is a statistic@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the kingdom of God means only heaven. The second is reading a fulfilled prophecy as proof. On these numbers, he's still more likely false. And the third is taking four hundred and one as a measured rate. It's a reading of one story.
