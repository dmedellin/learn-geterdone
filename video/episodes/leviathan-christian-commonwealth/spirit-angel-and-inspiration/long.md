@chapter A sum that doesn't add up
@title text="Spirit, Angel and Inspiration" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 34" art=thumb
Hobbes said in Chapter five that to reason is to add and subtract names. Before he infers anything from Scripture, he fixes what its words mean. And one phrase, he finds, is a sum that doesn't add up.

@quote text="Substance incorporeall are words, which when they are joined together, destroy one another, as if a man should say, an Incorporeall Body." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 34"
Substance incorporeal are words which, when they are joined together, destroy one another, as if a man should say, an incorporeal body.

@chapter Body and substance
@art body-fills-room
Here's why. A body is whatever fills, or occupies, some room or imagined place, and is a real part of the universe. Bodies change, and thought of that way they're called substances. So substance and body signify the same thing.

@art breath-and-air
But Hobbes admits the ordinary use. People call body only what they can see or feel, and call air and breath spirit, since the Latin spiritus means breath. In that sense an incorporeal substance isn't nothing. It's a thin invisible substance, with the same dimensions as grosser bodies. The trouble is incorporeal meaning no body at all.

@chapter Spirit and angel
@list heading="Spirit means" items="A subtle, fluid, invisible body@subtle|A ghost, an idol of the imagination@ghost|Figures: a disposition, a gift, zeal@figurative"
So spirit properly means either a subtle, fluid, invisible body, or a ghost, an idol of the imagination. Beyond that, there are figurative senses: a disposition, a gift of wisdom, a sudden zeal.

@art ghost-in-mirror
Keep the two proper senses apart. A ghost seen in a dream or a looking glass is an idol of the brain, which is nothing. When the Spirit of God moved on the waters, it's a wind. Other signification of spirit, he says, I find nowhere any.

@art pillar-of-cloud
An angel means a messenger. What makes one is not its shape but its use, to signify God's presence. A pillar of cloud is an angel as much as a man with wings would be.

@words text="Angels substantial and permanent"
At first Hobbes took angels to be only apparitions of the fancy. Then the New Testament extorted from his feeble reason a belief in angels substantial and permanent. On his definitions that makes them bodies, however thin. What can't be shown is that they're in no place.

@chapter Inspiration
@art barrels
Inspiration, taken properly, would be blowing thin air into a man, like filling a bladder. So in Scripture it's a metaphor. God inspired the breath of life means he gave life. And God's graces are virtues, not bodies to be poured into men as into barrels.

@chapter The lab's reckoning
@math heading="Is a substance incorporeal?" items="substance = body = extended + real@substance;;incorporeal = unextended@incorporeal;;sum: extended + real + unextended@together;;extended and unextended cannot be added: absurd@absurd"
The lab does the reckoning. Substance is body, and body is extended and real. Incorporeal is unextended. Put them together, and you have extended and unextended, which can't be added. So a substance is incorporeal is absurd. Extended and unextended are the lesson's simple names, not Hobbes's own terms.

@math heading="Nine claims" items="true: 3@three true;;false: 2@two false;;absurd: 3@three absurd;;insignificant: 1@one insignificant"
The preset holds nine claims, and on the definitions as written there are three true, two false, three absurd, and one insignificant. A subtle spirit is a body: true. A ghost is a body: false, but not absurd. And inspiration is infusion is insignificant, since neither word is defined.

@words text="The verdicts are the lab's sorting"
One caution. Those four verdicts are the lab's own sorting. For Hobbes, a false universal claim is itself absurd, and an undefined name and a contradiction are both insignificant sounds. So the lab shows the difference between not following and cancelling out, not where Hobbes draws the line.

@chapter The other side
@art penetrable-spirit
Some contemporaries read this as an attack on religion. From inside, it targets a word of the Schools, not Scripture. God is a spirit in a sense we don't understand. The strongest reply came from Henry More and the Cambridge Platonists: a spirit may fill room and still not be a body, because it's penetrable. Then Hobbes's first definition is the premise in dispute.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Hobbes says there are no angels@The first|The lab's verdicts are Hobbes's@The second|The definitions are the lab's discovery@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Hobbes says there are no angels. He believes in permanent ones. The second is taking the lab's verdicts for Hobbes's. And the third is reading the definitions as the lab's discovery. They're Hobbes's, and one of them carries nearly all the result.
