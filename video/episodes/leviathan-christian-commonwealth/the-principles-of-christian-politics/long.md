@chapter God told me so
@title text="The Principles of Christian Politics" sub="Leviathan, Chapter 32" art=thumb
Why does a book about sovereignty suddenly need a theory of prophets? Think of England when Hobbes wrote. Through a civil war and a king's execution, people on every side said the Spirit had told them what to do.

@art voice-in-crowd
Hobbes has spent two Parts showing that peace needs one sovereign judge. Part Three has to face the one claim that seems to outrank him: God has spoken to me, and I must obey God rather than man.

@chapter Reason stays in the room
@words text="Above reason, never contrary to it"
Until now he's argued from nature alone. Now he must reason from revelation too. But we're not to renounce our senses and experience. They're talents to trade with, not to be folded up in the napkin of an implicit faith. Much in Scripture is above reason. Nothing in it is contrary to reason.

@art captive-will
And when Scripture bids us captivate our understanding, that means submitting the will to obedience, not handing your intellect to another man. What you sense and believe isn't in your power to change, so it can't be commanded. What can be commanded is that you don't contradict, and that you act accordingly.

@chapter Who can check a revelation?
@art dreamer
Here's the problem. If a man says God spoke to him in a dream, he's saying he dreamed that God spoke. A vision is a dream between sleeping and waking. God can do all this. But he obliges no one to believe the man who claims it, who, being a man, may err, and may lie.

@words text="400 prophets · only Micaiah was true"
And prophets contradict one another. Of four hundred the king of Israel consulted before the war at Ramoth Gilead, only Micaiah was a true one. If one prophet can deceive another, what certainty is there, except by reason?

@chapter Two marks
@quote text="there be two marks, by which together, not asunder, a true Prophet is to be known." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 32"
Hobbes answers out of Scripture. There be two marks by which, together, not asunder, a true prophet is to be known. One is doing miracles. The other is teaching no religion but the one already established.

@art serpent-rods
Why both? Because the Egyptian sorcerers' works, though not so great as Moses's, were great miracles. And Deuteronomy says a wonder worker who teaches strange gods is to be put to death. In Israel, Hobbes adds, revolting from God meant revolting from your King.

@chapter The lab's test
@art two-keys
So the test is two keys in one lock. A miracle alone doesn't open it, and neither does sound doctrine alone.

@math heading="Three letters" items="A: he is to be acknowledged@A means;;M: he has done a miracle@M;;D: he teaches the established religion@D;;rule: A ⟺ (M and D)@if and only if"
The lab writes the test as a conjunction, which is the lesson's reading. A means he's to be acknowledged. M, he's done a miracle. D, he teaches the established religion. And the rule: A, if and only if M and D.

@math heading="The sorcerers" items="premises: A ⟺ (M and D);  M;  not D@premises;;A needs D, and D is false@needs D;;not A in every row: valid@every row"
Take the sorcerers. The premises are the rule, M, and not D. Three letters give eight rows. A needs D true, and D is false. So not A holds in every row that fits. No counterexample. Valid.

@words text="Miracle alone: one counterexample" sub="A false, M true, D false"
Now give only the miracle, and ask for A. One row, with A false, M true and D false, fits the premises and breaks the conclusion. That row is the sorcerer again. Doctrine alone fails the same way. Neither mark suffices asunder.

@chapter Miracles have ceased
@art empty-pulpit
Then Hobbes's conclusion for his own century. Seeing miracles now cease, we have no sign left to acknowledge the pretended revelations of any private man. In the lab's last preset, with no miracle, nobody can be acknowledged, whatever he teaches.

@words text="What counts as established?"
Critics objected at once: the apostles and the Reformers taught against the religion of their day. Hobbes answers by fixing what's established: for Israel, the law as Moses gave it, and for Christians, the one article that Jesus is the Christ. Whether that satisfies is what the sixteen forties fought about.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="A miracle alone proves the prophet@The first|Captivity means giving up reason@The second|God can't speak to a man@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a miracle alone proves the prophet. The second is reading captivity of the understanding as giving up reason. And the third is thinking Hobbes says God can't speak to a man. He can. The limit is on what a report can make others owe.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Sound doctrine, no miracle" sub="what follows?"
Your turn. A man teaches exactly the established religion, but has done no miracle. Write the premises, find the counterexample row, and then check your answer against what Hobbes says of such a man.
