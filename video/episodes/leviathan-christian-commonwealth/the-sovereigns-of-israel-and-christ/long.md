@chapter Why obey Moses?
@title text="The Sovereigns of Israel and the Office of Christ" sub="Leviathan, Chapters 40 and 41" art=thumb
Why would anyone obey Moses? The Israelites had seen the plagues and stood at Sinai, so surely they obeyed because God chose him. Hobbes sees a problem. They had to know God chose him, and the only one who said so was Moses.

@chapter Abraham first
@art abraham-household
The chapter starts with Abraham, the father of the faithful. God spoke only to him, and his family's wills were included in his. So those God hasn't spoken to must take his commandments from their sovereign. And only the sovereign can say what is, or isn't, the word of God. Inward belief falls under no law. The obligation is to outward profession.

@chapter Four grounds, three crossed out
@art mountain-and-people
Moses is harder. He had no father's power to inherit. So what bound the people? Not God's command, because God didn't speak to them directly, but through Moses himself.

@quote text="His authority therefore, as the authority of all other Princes, must be grounded on the Consent of the People, and their Promise to obey him." cite="Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapter 40"
And not Moses's own witness, since even Christ said, if I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true. His authority therefore, as the authority of all other princes, must be grounded on the consent of the people, and their promise to obey him.

@words text="Speak thou with us, and we will hear"
Hobbes finds that promise at Sinai. The people said to Moses, speak thou with us and we will hear, but let not God speak with us, lest we die. By that they obliged themselves to obey whatever he delivered as God's command.

@chapter The lab: by elimination
@math heading="Five letters" items="R: the people were obliged to obey Moses@R says;;D: God's direct command obliged them@D;;H: they were obliged as Abraham's heirs@H;;W: Moses's own witness obliged them@W;;C: their consent and promise did@C"
The lab writes this with five letters. R says the people were obliged to obey Moses. D, God's direct command obliged them. H, they were obliged as heirs to Abraham. W, Moses's own witness bound them. And C, their consent and promise did.

@math heading="Moses preset" items="R → (D or H or W or C)@one of four;;not D,  not H,  not W,  R@crossed out;;∴ C: 32 rows, no counterexample@thirty two rows"
If they were obliged, it was by one of four grounds. Three are crossed out, and they were obliged. So it was consent. Five letters give thirty two rows, and none is a counterexample. The argument is valid.

@art missing-ground
Drop the premise excluding inheritance, and one row is a counterexample. Or add signs and wonders as a ground, with nothing to exclude it, and again one row breaks it. So everything rests on the list being complete. Hobbes folds miracles into the people's opinion, which could change. The lab shows that this has to be said.

@chapter Israel's history
@art crown-and-mitre
After Moses, sovereignty passed to Eleazar the high priest. Under the judges, everyone did what was right in his own eyes. Then the people asked for a king, and God consented. From then to the captivity, the supremacy of religion was in the same hand as the civil sovereignty. Solomon thrust out a high priest, and dedicated the temple himself.

@words text="Prophets rebuked kings" sub="Isaiah and Hezekiah · Rutherford, Lex, Rex, 1644"
A Presbyterian answered at once: prophets rebuked kings. Hobbes grants that no king was uncontrolled, but says that doesn't put the right in the prophets. Samuel Rutherford's Lex, Rex, in sixteen forty four, read the same history as showing kings hold power by covenant, under God's law. That was the disagreement of the Civil War.

@chapter The office of Christ
@list heading="Three parts of Christ's office" items="Redeemer@redeemer|Teacher@teacher|King, in the world to come@king"
Chapter forty one gives Christ's office three parts: redeemer, who pays the ransom of sin, teacher, who proclaims himself the Christ, and king, under his Father, in the world to come.

@art three-offices
The redeemer pays the ransom of sin, which is death, as the scapegoat and the sacrificed goat did together. The teacher prepares men by teaching and miracles. The king's reign is still to come.

@art tribute-coin
On earth, Hobbes argues, he wasn't king, since his kingdom was not of this world. He told men to obey those who sat in Moses's seat, and to pay tribute to Caesar. Nothing he taught diminished the civil right of the Jews or of Caesar. So his ministers have no power to command now.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Hobbes says Moses was a fraud@The first|Valid means true@The second|Christ's kingdom is here and now@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Hobbes calls Moses a fraud. He says Moses spoke with God. His question is what bound the people. The second is treating validity as truth. And the third is thinking Christ's kingdom is here and now. It begins when he returns.
