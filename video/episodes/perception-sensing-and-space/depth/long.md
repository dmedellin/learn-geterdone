@chapter Is depth visible?
@title text="Depth" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 2" art=thumb
The section on depth begins with something Berkeley and the intellectualists agree on, though they agree on little else. Depth, they say, is not visible.

@art flat-retina
Berkeley, in his New Theory of Vision of seventeen oh nine, argued that the retina receives only a flat projection, so depth has no way of being recorded. Analytical reflection reaches the same verdict for other reasons: distance exists only for a subject who synthesizes it.

@quote text="Traditional ideas of perception are at one in denying that depth is visible. … In both cases depth is tacitly equated with breadth seen from the side, and this is what makes it invisible." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Space, p. 297"
Opposed doctrines, one assumption. In both cases, depth is tacitly equated with breadth seen from the side, and this is what makes it invisible.

@chapter Breadth seen from the side
@art side-spectator
Spelled out: depth is really a row of points, like breadth, and I'm just badly placed. A spectator off to the side would take in at a glance the objects that, for me, hide one another. What hides depth from me shows it to him as breadth.

@words text="For God, who is everywhere, breadth is immediately equivalent to depth."
To treat depth like that, the subject has to leave his place and think himself everywhere. For God, who is everywhere, breadth is immediately equivalent to depth. Yet the chapter grants Berkeley one thing: depth plainly belongs to the perspective. It's the most existential dimension.

@chapter The classical theory
@art eye-triangle
Now the classical theory in its own voice. The distance is the height of a triangle, with its base and base angles given to me. The base is the distance between the eyes. The angles come from how much they converge.

@art blind-sticks
The picture goes back to Descartes, where the eyes are like a blind man's two sticks, running together as the object comes near. The Cartesians called this a natural geometry. Berkeley replaced the geometry with signs learned by experience.

@words text="The signs presuppose what they are meant to give"
The chapter's reply: the signs presuppose what they're meant to give. I can read convergence as a sign only if I already picture my eyes, my body and the world in one objective space. Natural geometry is a myth, in Plato's sense.

@chapter The lab computes it
@math heading="Near preset, eyes 6.5 cm apart" items="50 cm: 6.5 / 50 = 13/100 rad@at fifty;;60 cm: 6.5 / 60 = 13/120 rad@at sixty;;disparity: 13/100 − 13/120 = 13/600 ≈ 0.0217 rad@disparity"
The lab computes the classical theory, with the angle as a length over a distance. Eyes six point five centimetres apart. A point at fifty gives thirteen hundredths of a radian. A point at sixty gives thirteen over a hundred and twenty. The disparity is thirteen over six hundred, about nought point nought two one seven.

@math heading="Farther off" items="500 and 600 cm: 13/6000, a tenth@ten times;;500 and 510 cm: 13/51000@same ten centimetre;;both at 50 cm: 0@both at fifty"
Move both points ten times farther, and the disparity falls to a tenth: thirteen over six thousand. The same ten centimetre interval five metres off gives thirteen over fifty one thousand. With both at fifty, it's nought.

@words text="there is disparity only for a subject who tries to fuse monocular phenomena"
These are the figures the theory says the mind uses. But nobody has them. I'm not aware of my eyes converging when I see something far off. And there is disparity only for a subject who tries to fuse monocular phenomena, a gaze already reaching for one thing.

@chapter Motives, not signs
@words text="Convergence and apparent size are neither signs nor causes of depth"
The Gestalt psychologists concluded that convergence is a cause instead, like a law of physics. The chapter rejects that too. Convergence and apparent size are neither signs nor causes of depth. They're present the way a motive is present in a decision.

@art journey-motive
A death motivates my journey, because it's a situation that calls for my presence. And in deciding to go, I make the motive valid. The relation runs both ways.

@art road-parallel
The sides of a road running to the horizon aren't given as converging, or as parallel. They're parallel in depth. Is a man smaller at two hundred yards than at five? Only if I isolate him and measure. Otherwise he's the same man, seen from farther away.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="We see depth by calculating convergence@calculating|Convergence causes depth@a cause of depth|The far man is seen smaller, then corrected@then corrected"
Don't say we see depth by calculating from convergence; the lab computes the calculation nobody performs. Don't make convergence a cause of depth either. And don't say the far man is seen smaller and then corrected. He offers the gaze less to hold.
