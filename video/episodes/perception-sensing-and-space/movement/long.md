@chapter A stone across the garden
@title text="Movement" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 2" art=thumb
Throw a stone across a garden. For a moment it becomes an indistinct, meteor like streak, then a stone again where it lands. Now think about it clearly, and it comes apart.

@art stone-arc
The stone isn't changed by moving. It's the same stone I held and the same I find on the ground. Movement is just a change in its relations to its surroundings. So there's no movement without one moving body, no movement without a landmark, and no absolute movement.

@chapter Zeno returns
@art zeno-arrow
And yet that idea negates movement. Zeno argued that a flying arrow, at each instant, fills a space equal to itself, so it's at rest at every instant. Bring in a moving body identical throughout, and Zeno's arguments become valid once more.

@words text="How does movement ever begin for me?"
Infinitely small instants don't help. Whatever two positions you take, the passing from one to the other can't be conceived in one identical body. Thinking clearly about movement, I don't understand how it ever begins for me.

@chapter Two lines of light
@quote text="If we present to a subject alternately two lines of light A and B, the subject sees a continuous movement from A to B, then from B to A, then again from A to B and so on, even though no intermediate position or indeed the extreme positions be given for themselves; we have one single line ceaselessly moving back and forth." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Space, pp. 313–14"
Max Wertheimer flashed two lines of light in alternation. His subjects saw one line moving back and forth, though nothing lit the space between. We have one single line ceaselessly moving back and forth.

@art two-lights
Speed up or slow down the alternation, and the line is held at one place, then jumps. Go further, and you see two lines. The perception of positions varies inversely as that of movement.

@art rod-between
Put a short rod between the lights, and the movement passes over it without ever completing it. We have not a passage of the line, but a pure passage. Real movement's the same. Watch workmen throwing bricks: you see the arm at the start and the end, never between.

@chapter The lab's bands
@math heading="Lights 5 cm apart" items="20 ms: 5 / 20 = 1/4 cm per ms → seen together@twenty milliseconds;;60 ms: 5 / 60 = 1/12 cm per ms → motion@sixty;;250 ms: 5 / 250 = 1/50 cm per ms → in succession@two hundred and fifty"
The lab uses thresholds from later textbooks, not from Merleau-Ponty: below about thirty milliseconds, seen together, above about two hundred, one after the other. Lights five centimetres apart, twenty milliseconds: a quarter centimetre per millisecond, seen together. At sixty: a twelfth, motion. At two hundred and fifty: a fiftieth, in succession.

@words text="the speed of a body nobody sees"
Notice what the speed is a speed of. It's how fast a body would have to cross the gap. That's the objective idea of movement turned into arithmetic. In the experiment, nothing crosses the gap at all.

@chapter Logician and psychologist
@art vanishing-watch
The logician, Paul Linke, answers. All movement must be movement of something. If my watch vanished from the table and reappeared in the next room, nobody would say it had moved. The psychologist replies that movement is a dynamic phenomenon, not built from visual contents.

@words text="identical in them, not beneath them" sub="the mobile entity"
The chapter says both are right. The logician's right to demand something that moves, wrong to make its identity explicit. The mobile entity is not identical beneath the phases of movement. It is identical in them. I go to pick up the stone because I saw it one in its flight.

@art grey-bird
As the circle before Euclid had no diameters, the mobile entity has only a style. The bird crossing the garden is, while it moves, merely a greyish power of flight.

@chapter A phenomenon of levels
@words text="Like top and bottom, motion is a phenomenon of levels"
Movement is given in a field. On a ship along the coast, either the coast flows by, or the ship glides. Playing cards in a train, I see the next train move off, even when it's mine that starts. Like top and bottom, motion is a phenomenon of levels.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Motion is one identical thing in every position@every position|The chapter sides with the psychologist@siding with|Anchorage makes motion relative@relative"
Don't say motion is one identical thing passing through every position. Don't read the chapter as siding with the psychologist; both are vindicated. And don't conclude motion is relative. Once I'm lodged in a setting, motion appears as an absolute.
