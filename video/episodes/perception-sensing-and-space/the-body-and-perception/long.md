@chapter Turning a die
@title text="The Theory of the Body Is Already a Theory of Perception" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, introduction" art=thumb
Pick up a die and turn it slowly in your fingers. The face that was square on to you narrows and slides away, and the next one widens until it's square on in its turn.

@art die-turning
You never have all six faces at once. And yet you never doubt you're holding a cube with six equal faces. So where is that cube? In the die, in your idea of a cube, or in something your hand and eye are already doing?

@quote text="Our own body is in the world as the heart is in the organism: it keeps the visible spectacle constantly alive, it breathes life into it and sustains it inwardly, and with it forms a system." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, introduction, p. 235"
That's the sentence that opens Part Two, The World as Perceived. The body keeps the visible spectacle alive, and with it forms a system.

@chapter A walk round a flat
@art flat-rooms
The introduction's own case is a walk round a flat. The views you get, room after room, appear as views of one flat only because you know each is the flat seen from one spot or another, and because you're aware of your body keeping its identity as you move.

@art flat-plan
A plan on paper doesn't escape this. It's only a more comprehensive perspective, the flat seen from above. You can gather the views into it only because one and the same embodied subject can look from one place after another.

@chapter The objector's cube
@words text="It will perhaps be objected…" sub="the objector's voice, not Merleau-Ponty's"
Then he lets an objector speak, and be careful here, because the rest of that paragraph is the objector's voice, not his. From my body's point of view, the objector says, I never see the six sides as equal, even with a glass cube. But the cube in reality has six equal sides.

@words text="the views are occasions for conceiving the cube"
The changing views are only the occasion for conceiving the whole cube, the structure that explains them. My body becomes one moving object among others. This is intellectualism: thought fixes what the cube is, and the views are data it interprets.

@chapter The reply
@art enclosed-room
The reply starts with the definition. You can put six, side and equal into a formula, but the words enclose and between get their meaning from our bodies. A space is enclosed between the sides of a cube as we're enclosed between the walls of a room.

@quote text="The cube with six equal sides is not only invisible, but inconceivable" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, introduction, pp. 235–239"
In space itself, with no one perceiving, there's no inside and no outside. So to conceive the cube at all, you take up a position, and from that moment you see it in perspective. The cube with six equal sides is not only invisible, but inconceivable.

@words text="a limiting idea" sub="whereby I express the material presence of the cube"
What's left is a positive claim. The six equal sides are the limiting idea whereby I express the material presence of the cube, the one before my eyes and under my hands. Its sides aren't projections of it. They're sides.

@chapter What the lab computes
@math heading="Turned view: 3 0 4" items="9 + 0 + 16 = 25, length 5@nine plus;;right 3/5, front 4/5@three fifths"
The lab computes parallel projection, ordinary geometry. Take the turned view, along three, nought, four. Nine plus nought plus sixteen is twenty five, so the length is five. The right side is drawn at three fifths of its area, the front at four fifths.

@math heading="Corner view: 1 2 2" items="1 + 4 + 4 = 9, length 3@one plus;;right 1/3, above 2/3, front 2/3@one third;;no face drawn at 1@no face"
Now the corner, along one, two, two. One plus four plus four is nine, so the length is three. Three faces: the right at one third, the top and the front at two thirds each. No face is drawn at its true area.

@art cube-projection
But notice how the lab gets these figures. It holds a cube with six equal faces in advance, from no point of view, and computes every view from it. That's the objector's procedure. The figures are what that procedure computes, not what anyone sees.

@chapter One act, two facets
@art crossed-fingers
The introduction then gives cases. Cross two fingers, touch a marble with both, and you feel two marbles. The crossed position is one the fingers can't reach by their own movement, so they can't explore one object together. To perceive one marble and to use two fingers as one organ are the same thing.

@quote text="The theory of the body schema is, implicitly, a theory of perception." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Part Two, introduction, pp. 235–239"
Every external perception is at once a perception of my body, and the other way round. Hence the programme of Part Two. The theory of the body schema is, implicitly, a theory of perception.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The equal faces are the corrected view@corrected view|The body here is the physiologist's body@the physiologist's body|The reply makes the cube subjective@subjective"
Don't take the six equal faces for the corrected view; that's the objector. Don't hear the body as the physiologist's body of nerves; it's the body schema. And don't think the reply makes the cube subjective. The cube is already there, showing itself through the views.
