@chapter A glass falls
@title text="The Unity of the Senses" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 1" art=thumb
A glass slips off the edge of the table. Before it lands, you know how it'll sound. And when it breaks, the sound doesn't arrive beside the glass. It comes out of the glass you're watching.

@art glass-falling
You didn't see a shape, hear a ring, and then decide they belonged together. The rest of Sense Experience holds two claims at once. Each sense is distinct. And the senses communicate.

@chapter Touching is not seeing
@words text="All senses open on one space?"
Intellectualism doesn't really talk about senses at all. For it, all senses must open on one space, since space is the form of any object. The chapter agrees every sense is spatial, but for its own reason: sensing is a coexistence, and so already makes a space.

@art cataract-sight
The cases are Marius von Senden's, people born blind and given sight by a cataract operation. A patient marvels at visual space, and beside it touch seems poor. He sees colours as we smell an odour. One, blind for eighteen years, tries to touch a ray of sunlight.

@words text="touching is not seeing" sub="yet he reaches out for what he sees"
And yet he reaches out for the objects shown to him, which makes no sense if touch opened on no space. So the chapter holds both halves. There's a space strictly tactile, and touching is not seeing.

@chapter The separate quality
@art white-papers
What about a colour that belongs to sight and nothing else? Picture sheets of white paper, some in sunlight, some in shadow. As a whole they all look white. Look at the shaded ones through a card with a small window, and they turn into a grey or steely blue stuff.

@words text="Synaesthetic perception is the rule"
So the separate quality is the product of an analytic attitude, the one I take up when I'm afraid of being wrong. Beneath it is a layer before the senses divide, where a flute's sound can give a bluish green colour. Synaesthetic perception is the rule.

@chapter Through the thing
@quote text="The senses intercommunicate by opening on to the structure of the thing. One sees the hardness and brittleness of glass," cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Sense Experience, pp. 266–7"
Now the passage. The senses intercommunicate by opening on to the structure of the thing. One sees the hardness and brittleness of glass, and when it breaks with a tinkling sound, the sound is conveyed by the visible glass.

@art steel-twig-cobbles
One sees the springiness of steel. One reads a twig's elasticity in its jerk as a bird leaves it. One hears the hardness of cobbles in the rattle of a carriage. And with eyes closed one feels the texture of a bent steel bar.

@chapter The lab's two codings
@math heading="Sense-bound coding" items="seen: transparent;;touched: smooth, cold;;tapped: rings;;dropped: tinkles@tinkles;;core: none@the core"
The lab codes the glass seen, touched, tapped and dropped. In the sense bound coding, each sense gives only its own qualities: transparent, smooth and cold, rings, tinkles. What's in all four? Nothing, so the core is none.

@math heading="The chapter's coding" items="each adds: hard, brittle@hard and brittle;;core: hard, brittle@core"
In the chapter's coding, each variant also has hardness and brittleness, seen, heard and felt. Now the core is hard and brittle.

@art two-codings
The intersection can't go wrong. Everything depends on the coding. The empty core isn't a finding. It's just what the sense bound coding put in, and the chapter's descriptions put hardness in what each sense gives.

@chapter Two eyes, one finger
@words text="Venus: Morning Star and Evening Star?"
But what kind of unity is it? It's been said the sonata I hear is the one Helen Keller touches, the common ground of two appearances, as Venus is of the Morning Star and the Evening Star. Then perception would be an incipient science.

@art double-finger
The chapter's answer is binocular vision. Fix your gaze far off, and a near finger is double. Look at it, and the two images merge into one. If the unity were a thought, it would come as soon as I noticed. In fact I wait for the focusing.

@words text="The senses translate each other without any need of an interpreter"
So the body is a synergic system, a ready made system of equivalents from one sense to another. The senses translate each other without any need of an interpreter. In a dubbed film, the ears go to the soundless words on the screen.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Each sense has its own world and the mind joins them@the mind joins|Touching is not seeing means touch has no space@no space|Synaesthesia is a rare anomaly@rare"
Don't say each sense has its own world and the mind joins them. Don't read touching is not seeing as meaning touch has no space. And don't take synaesthesia for a rare anomaly. It's the separate quality that's the special case.

@chapter Try it
@math heading="Find the core" items="hard brittle clear;;hard cold;;hard rings;;hard brittle tinkles"
Your turn. Four variants: hard, brittle, clear. Hard, cold. Hard, rings. Hard, brittle, tinkles. What's the core? Then say which coding the chapter describes. Next lesson: a room in a tilted mirror that suddenly rights itself.
