@chapter Looking up
@title text="The Anonymous Subject of Sensing" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 1" art=thumb
Lie on your back on a clear day and look up. You didn't decide to see blue, the way you might decide to take a job. You looked, and the blue was there.

@art sky-gaze
Yet you weren't a camera either. You had to look, and let your gaze go out into the sky and stay there. If sensation is communion, the chapter asks, then who is it that senses?

@chapter Two answers on the table
@list heading="Two answers" items="Empiricism: an impression undergone@For empiricism|Intellectualism: a quality posited@For intellectualism"
Two answers were already on the table. For empiricism, a sensation is an impression undergone, a state of the organism. For intellectualism, it's a quality posited by a thinking subject. Then it's ridiculous to say I see with my eyes, since my eyes are just things in the world.

@words text="How could I ever have been ignorant of it?"
The chapter's question back is short. If I don't really see with my eyes, how could I ever have been ignorant of it, if my thought is transparent to itself? Saying it's still I who conceive myself as embodied is a purely verbal solution.

@chapter Coexistence
@art give-and-take
So sensation is redefined as coexistence. Between the one who senses and the sensible, neither simply acts while the other suffers. Apart from the probing of eye or hand, the sensible is only a vague beckoning.

@art spasm-attitude
Werner reports that a subject who tries to see blue while holding the bodily attitude of red feels a kind of spasm, which stops when he takes up blue's attitude. And yet the body's attitude alone never makes it see blue.

@quote text="The sensible gives back to me what I lent to it, but this is only what I took from it in the first place." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Sense Experience, pp. 246–252"
The sensible sets the body a muddled problem, and the body takes it up. The sensible gives back to me what I lent to it, but this is only what I took from it in the first place.

@chapter Not a hole but a fold
@words text="I am the sky itself as it is drawn together and unified"
Back to the sky. I don't set myself over against it or spread an idea of blue on it. I give myself up to it. The sky is not a mind, you'll say. The astronomer's sky isn't. But the perceived sky isn't made of parts outside each other.

@art fold
And the one who senses needn't be a pure nothing. He's a repository of natural powers. Against Hegel's phrase, not a hole in being, but a hollow, a fold, made and able to be unmade.

@chapter One perceives in me
@quote text="So, if I wanted to render precisely the perceptual experience, I ought to say that one perceives in me, and not that I perceive. … I am no more aware of being the true subject of my sensation than of my birth or my death." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Sense Experience, p. 250"
Here's the passage. I ought to say that one perceives in me, and not that I perceive. And I'm no more aware of being the true subject of my sensation than of my birth or my death.

@art personal-act
Seeing blue isn't like deciding to devote my life to mathematics. Personal acts create a situation, and perception expresses a given one. I can't experience my own birth or death, and each sensation is a birth and a death, with its origin before me.

@words text="another self which has already sided with the world"
In it I feel a self that isn't the one I answer for, but another self which has already sided with the world: the life of my eyes, hands and ears. The chapter calls them natural selves.

@art visual-field
And sensation can be anonymous because it's incomplete. The self that sees is specialized, open on one sector of being. To have a visual field is to have access to a system of visible beings, with no effort, limited by a horizon of what's not seen.

@chapter The lab's table
@list heading="Three observations" items="I do not decide to see blue: y, n@I don't decide|The sensible beckons until taken up: n, y@beckons|Sentient and sensible give and take: n, n@give and take"
The lab codes three observations, empiricism first. I don't decide to see blue: empiricism absorbs it, intellectualism doesn't. The sensible beckons until it's taken up: empiricism fails, intellectualism absorbs. Sentient and sensible give and take: both fail.

@math heading="All preset" items="each: 1 of 3 absorbed, 2 failed@each account;;survivors: none@survivors;;split: the first two@the split"
So each account absorbs one of three and fails two. The survivors tile prints none. The split, where one absorbs and the other fails, is the first two. Both fail the third, and that's where the chapter's own account stands, in prose, not scored.

@words text="passivity preset → empiricism survives" sub="one observation is not the argument"
Keep observation one alone, and empiricism survives. That's why a single case isn't the chapter's argument. The coding is the lesson's reading, too, so treat it as an interpretation.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="If one perceives in me, there's no subject@no subject|Anonymous means passive@passive|The sky has a mind@a mind"
Don't say there's no subject; it's placed below the personal one. Don't hear anonymous as passive; it means not chosen and not answered for. And don't read I am the sky itself as saying the sky has a mind.
