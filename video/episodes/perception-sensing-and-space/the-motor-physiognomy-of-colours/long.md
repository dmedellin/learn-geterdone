@chapter Red and green
@title text="The Motor Physiognomy of Colours" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 1" art=thumb
Sense Experience opens by asking what a sensation is. Its first answer comes from patients whose arms move differently under red and under green.

@art arm-red-green
Kurt Goldstein and Rosenthal reported on patients with diseases of the cerebellum or the frontal cortex. In a healthy person, a colour of this kind hardly affects movement. In these patients, red favours stretching the arm outwards, and green bending it back towards the body.

@art arm-rest
Their arm feels at rest further from the body than a healthy person's, and green brings it nearer. With a green field, a patient measures a length accurately with a finger. With red, he overshoots.

@list heading="A definite motor value" items="Red, yellow: abduction, away from the body@Red and yellow|Blue, green: adduction, towards it@blue and green"
Each colour always acts with the same tendency. Red and yellow favour abduction, movement away from the body's midline. Blue and green favour adduction, movement towards it. Drawn by the world, or withdrawn to its centre.

@chapter Neither state nor thought
@art fauna-distant
Why does this matter? Objective thought takes the world as ready made. The empiricist describes sensations as one might describe the fauna of a distant land, forgetting he's perceiving too. Intellectualism names the thinking subject he looks from, but it also takes the world ready made.

@quote text="Sensations, ‘sensible qualities’ are then far from being reducible to a certain indescribable state or quale; they present themselves with a motor physiognomy, and are enveloped in a living significance." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Sense Experience, p. 243"
So the chapter returns to sensation. Its first clause rejects the opponents' idea, a quale. The second gives the finding: a motor physiognomy, like the character a face presents at once.

@chapter Two exits closed
@words text="first the colour, then the movement?"
It's long been known that stimuli start small movements around a quality. The usual view keeps the two apart: first the colour, then, as its effect, the movement. That's what the chapter overturns.

@list heading="Two exits closed" items="Not light as physics describes it@Not light|Not a quality clearly seen@Nor a quality"
Not light as physics describes it, because a blue produced by contrast, with nothing physical behind it, has the same motor halo. Nor a quality clearly seen, because red may accentuate my reactions without my being aware of it.

@quote text="Blue is that which prompts me to look in a certain way" cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Sense Experience, pp. 240–246"
A colour is a field, an atmosphere offered to the eyes and the whole body. For Kandinsky green asks nothing of us. For Goethe blue yields to the gaze and red invades the eye.

@art two-sides
What's left is that the colour and the body's way of moving are one thing, seen from two sides.

@chapter Before it is seen
@art faint-light
Heinz Werner showed colours too faint or too brief to be seen. One subject felt a slipping downwards in his body, and so knew it could only be blue, though he saw no blue. Another clenched his teeth, and so knew it was yellow.

@chapter The lab's gap
@math heading="Red and green, pure quality" items="predicted: 0 − 0 = 0 cm@nought minus;;reported: 4 + 2 = 6 cm@four outward;;gap: 6 − 0 = 6 cm@the gap"
The lab computes the opponents' prediction. A pure quality has no motor effect, so the arm stays at nought under both colours: nought minus nought is nought. The reports, illustratively, put it four outward under red and two inward under green, six centimetres apart. So the gap is six.

@words text="The whole of what is reported is gap" sub="centimetres illustrative; the chapter gives directions"
Those centimetres are illustrative; the chapter reports directions, not measurements. The arithmetic is trivial, and that's its point. The usual view calls the gap a second fact. The chapter says red is already the amplification of our motor being.

@chapter A form of communion
@art sleeper-breath
So who senses? Think of a sleeper. I breathe slowly to summon sleep, and suddenly the rhythm I was keeping up becomes my very being. In the same way, I look, expecting a sensation, and suddenly the sensible takes hold of my gaze.

@words text="sensation is literally a form of communion"
His second comparison is the sacrament. The sensible is a way of being in the world, offered from a point in space and taken up by a body able to take it up. The quality is lived, not inspected.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="The colour is seen first, then moves the body@seen first|The arm's deviation is the physiognomy@deviation|Communion is mysticism@mysticism"
Don't say the colour is seen first and then moves the body. Don't take the arm's deviation for the physiognomy; it's the evidence. A healthy arm hardly deviates and the colour still has its character. And communion isn't mysticism. It's a comparison.

@chapter Try it
@words text="4 cm outward · 2 cm inward" sub="predicted? reported? gap?"
Your turn. The arm starts at nought, goes four outward under red and two inward under green. Find the predicted difference, the reported one, and the gap. Then say why the chapter won't call that gap an effect of a colour already seen.
