@chapter Beneath known space
@title text="Lived Space" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 2" art=thumb
So far the chapter has dealt with space as a disinterested subject might know it. Its last section widens the question, from the perception of space to the spatiality of existence itself.

@art paris-style
In the everyday, natural attitude, I don't have perceptions. Paris, for me, isn't a collection of views, or the law that unites them. It's a style, like the one a person shows in gesture, gait and voice.

@words text="walls 150 yards apart" sub="under mescalin, he still takes food with his hand"
The cases show what lies beneath. Under mescalin, approaching objects seem to grow smaller, and the walls of a room seem a hundred and fifty yards apart. Yet the subject still takes food with his hand. Perception as knowledge is intact.

@chapter Night
@quote text="Night is not an object before me; it enwraps me and infiltrates through all my senses, stifling my recollections and almost destroying my personal identity. … it is pure depth without foreground or background, without surfaces and without any distance separating it from me." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Space, p. 330"
Then night, from the psychiatrist Eugene Minkowski. Night is not an object before me. It enwraps me, and it is pure depth without foreground or background, with no distance separating it from me.

@art night-enwraps
When the world of clear objects is abolished, our perceptual being evolves a spatiality without things. A shout or a distant light peoples the night only vaguely. It's from the heart of the night that I'm united with it, and the distress some feel there is our contingency brought home.

@chapter Dreams and myth
@art dream-bird
Rising and falling are common themes in dreams, and they can be related to breathing or to desire. But that doesn't get far, because dreamed rising and falling aren't in visible space. The dreamer's great bird, shot and falling into a small heap of charred paper, falls with the tide of his existence.

@art encampment
For people who live in a world of myth, the tribal encampment isn't located by a landmark. It's the landmark of all landmarks. And when I arrive in a village for my holidays, it becomes the centre of my life, until news from Paris makes me feel an exile there.

@words text="lived distance"
Minkowski calls this lived distance. It can leave me room, or be too small and too great at once. One of Fischer's patients, before a mountain landscape, feels a second sky, black and boundless, penetrate the blue sky of evening.

@chapter The objection
@words text="confused appearances of one true space?"
Now objective thought, at strength. The dreamer must wake to know what he dreamed, and then his anxiety is a respiratory obstruction in the chest. Ask the patient where his second space is, and he conjures it away. So the lived spaces are confused appearances of one objective space.

@art brush-window
The sharpest reply is a patient of Binswanger's. He feels a brush near his window coming nearer and entering his head, and never stops knowing it's over there. Look at the window, and he still sees it. He has no quarrel with explicit perception.

@words text="not their critical powers but the structure of their space"
Either the one who experiences something knows what he experiences, and the patient must be taken at his word, or he doesn't, and the philosopher's own certainty may be an illusion. What protects the sane isn't their critical powers, but the structure of their space.

@chapter The lab's table
@list heading="Three cases" items="Night envelops me: n, n@night envelops|The brush, in his head and at the window: y, n@the brush|The dream rises and falls: n, y@the dream"
The lab codes three cases, empiricism first. Night envelops me: both fail, since there's nothing in it to attach a feeling to, and its thinking starts from nowhere. The brush: empiricism absorbs it, intellectualism fails. The dream: association fails, intellectualism places it in the chest.

@math heading="All preset" items="each: 1 of 3 absorbed, 2 failed@each account;;survivors: none@survivors;;split: the brush and the dream@split"
So each account absorbs one of three and fails two. The survivors tile prints none, and the split names the brush and the dream. Night defeats both, and the chapter's own account of it stays in prose.

@chapter Existence is spatial
@art tuileries-stone
Is this each person shut in a private space? No. Myth opens on possible objects, the dream uses waking space, and understanding myth is not believing in it. Rest your eyes on one stone of the Tuileries wall, and the square disappears. Beneath human space, there's always a natural one.

@words text="space is existential; … existence is spatial"
So, in the chapter's words, we have said that space is existential. We might just as well have said that existence is spatial. Human spaces are built on natural space, which isn't geometrical space.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Lived space distorts real space@a distortion|The chapter believes the myth@believes|These cases say nothing about normal perception@normal perception"
Don't call lived space a distortion of real space. Don't think the chapter believes the patient, or the myth. And don't think these cases say nothing about normal perception. They're extreme so a structure everywhere can be seen.
