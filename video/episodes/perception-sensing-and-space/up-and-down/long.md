@chapter A tilted room
@title text="Up and Down" sub="Phenomenology of Perception · Part Two, chapter 2" art=thumb
The chapter on space begins with up and down, and with two experiments. One turned the visual world upside down for a week. In the other, a tilted room suddenly rights itself.

@quote text="If we so contrive it that a subject sees the room in which he is, only through a mirror which reflects it at an angle at 45° to the vertical, the subject at first sees the room ‘slantwise’. … After a few minutes a sudden change occurs: the walls, the man walking about the room, and the line in which the cardboard falls become vertical." cite="Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Space, p. 289"
This is Max Wertheimer's mirror, tilted at forty five degrees to the vertical. At first the room looks slantwise. After a few minutes a sudden change occurs, and the walls, the man walking, and the falling cardboard become vertical.

@art tilted-mirror
At first the man seems to lean as he walks, and a piece of cardboard falling down the door frame falls obliquely. The chapter values one feature. The righting is sudden, and needs no exploration by movement.

@chapter Kant's alternative
@art two-spaces
Kant drew a strict line between space and the things in it. So either I don't reflect, and take space vaguely as the setting of things. Or I reflect, and find a single power that traces all directions. Spatialized space, or spatializing space. Is that alternative forced?

@chapter Stratton's spectacles
@art inverting-glasses
George Stratton wore spectacles that inverted the image on the retina. In a week long experiment, things looked inverted at first. From the second day the landscape wasn't inverted but his body felt wrongly placed. From the third day to the seventh, the body gradually righted itself.

@list heading="Stratton, 1897" items="Actions found their target from day five@the fifth day|Sounds placed only when their source was seen@Sounds|Spectacles off: things looked queer@came off"
Actions found their target from the fifth day. Sounds were placed correctly only when their source was also seen. And when the spectacles came off, things looked not inverted but queer, and he reached with the wrong hand.

@chapter Two readings rejected
@words text="“Inverted” or “upright”, in themselves, obviously have no meaning."
The first reading says the visual world is turned through a hundred and eighty degrees, and associations slowly rename the top. But this is unintelligible. Head and feet in the image could give the field a direction only if they had one themselves. Inverted or upright, in themselves, have no meaning.

@words text="each axis placed by another" sub="a mind that sees from nowhere"
Intellectualism fares no better. If up and down are axes a mind chooses, each axis is placed by another, for ever. And a mind that sees from nowhere couldn't even grant that the world looked upside down.

@chapter The spatial level
@art anchoring-room
Hence the spatial level. Before the experiment, perception has a level, and the mirrored room looks tilted. The spectacle induces another level, and the whole field is upright again. Walls and doors act as anchoring points, drawing the vertical to themselves.

@words text="My body is wherever there is something to be done."
The level isn't the body's own orientation. What counts is the body as a system of possible actions. In the mirror, the reflected room calls up a subject who could live in it. The level is a certain gearing of my body to the world.

@chapter The lab's model
@math heading="Wertheimer preset" items="period 1: 45 − 0 = 45°@period one;;period 2: 45 − 0 = 45°@period two;;period 3: level jumps, 45 − 45 = 0°@period three;;periods 4, 5: 0°@four and five"
The lab's model is the lesson's own: perceived tilt is image tilt minus level. Period one, forty five minus nought is forty five. Period two, the same. At period three the level jumps to forty five, so forty five minus forty five is nought. Periods four and five, nought.

@math heading="Stratton preset" items="180°, 90°, 45°, 45/2°, 45/4°@a hundred and eighty;;never reaches 0°@never reaches"
For Stratton, the image is turned a hundred and eighty, and the level moves half the remaining way each period: a hundred and eighty, ninety, forty five, forty five over two, forty five over four. It never reaches nought. The halving is the lesson's illustration, not Stratton's figures.

@art key-change
Wertheimer's change is a jump, the level re anchored as a whole. Stratton's is systematic, yet piecemeal. I go from one system to another without the key to either, like someone who knows no music singing a tune he's heard in another key.

@chapter Space precedes itself
@art inverted-face
For a thinking subject, a face upside down is the same face. For a perceiver it's unrecognizable. Look long at someone lying on a bed from its head, and the face becomes terrifying. To invert an object is to deprive it of its significance.

@words text="space always precedes itself"
And a level always presupposes an earlier one. Space always precedes itself. My first hold on the world resumes a pre personal tradition. When levels give way, the result is giddiness and nausea.

@chapter Mistakes
@list heading="Don't say" items="Up is the top of the retinal image@retinal image|The level is the body's own orientation@own orientation|The subtraction is the chapter's theory@subtraction"
Don't say up is the top of the retinal image; the room came upright with the image unchanged. Don't confuse the level with the body's own orientation. And don't take the model's subtraction for the chapter's theory.
