@chapter The question
@title text="Total, Average and the Repugnant Conclusion" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
When you compare outcomes with the same people, adding up welfare is fairly simple. But what if the outcomes contain different numbers of people? Then total welfare and average welfare come apart, and each one costs you something.

@chapter Three at ten
@art three-tall-bars
Start with three people, each at welfare ten. Call that A. Its total is thirty, and its average is ten. These are good lives.

@words text="ε = 1/10" sub="a life barely worth living"
Now imagine a population whose members all live at a level called epsilon, a small positive number standing for a life barely worth living. Let epsilon be one tenth.

@art crowd-of-short-bars
A crowd of n people at one tenth has a total of n times one tenth. That's tiny for a small crowd. But it grows with every person you add, so once n is large enough, it beats thirty.

@chapter Finding the crowd
@math heading="The smallest crowd that beats A" items="A = 10, 10, 10:  total 30@A has;;n people at 1/10:  total n · 1/10@n people;;n = 300:  total 30, ties with A@three hundred;;n = 301:  total 301/10, beats A@three hundred and one;;n* = 301@the smallest"
A has a total of thirty. n people at one tenth have a total of n times one tenth. At three hundred, the total is thirty, a tie. At three hundred and one, it's thirty point one, which beats A. So the smallest winning crowd, n star, is three hundred and one.

@words text="12 at 1/10 total 6/5" sub="it is the 301 that win"
The lab lists at most twelve people on a side, so it shows twelve as a sample. Twelve at one tenth total six fifths, and lose to the three at ten. It's the three hundred and one that win.

@art repugnant-balance
So under total welfare, the three hundred and one are better than the three, though every one of them has a life of little value. That's the conclusion Parfit called repugnant. For every population at ten, some larger population at a lower positive level has a bigger total.

@chapter Mere addition
@art mere-addition
It follows from a premise that sounds harmless. Mere addition says adding people whose lives are worth living, without making anyone else worse off, doesn't make an outcome worse.

@math heading="A plus two at 2" items="total:  34 against 30, B wins@thirty four;;average:  10 against 34/5, A wins@ten against"
In this case B is A plus two people at welfare two. Total welfare ranks B above A, thirty four against thirty, and agrees with the premise. Average welfare ranks A above B, ten against thirty four fifths. So it denies the premise. Two new people with good enough lives made things worse.

@chapter The cost of averaging
@art one-tall-many-short
That's how averaging dodges the conclusion, and it isn't free. Take one person at eleven against twelve people at ten. Total prefers the twelve, a hundred and twenty to eleven. Average prefers the one person, eleven to ten. And the same holds for a million people at ten. One person at eleven beats any crowd at ten.

@words text="No free exit" sub="accept total's verdict, average's, or find another rule"
There's no free exit here. Accept the total's conclusion, accept the average's, or look for a rule that avoids both. The next lessons tune the weighting, and none of them is announced as the answer.

@art zero-line
One more thing. The scale carries a stipulation. Barely worth living means a level just above zero, and the computation depends on where zero lies. The lab takes epsilon as given, and it can't tell you whether a life at epsilon is worth living.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Assuming averaging avoids the problem@The first|Reading the conclusion as requiring misery@The second|Treating n* as a prediction@And the third"
The first slip is assuming averaging avoids the problem. It trades one repugnant verdict for others. The second is reading the conclusion as requiring misery. The people at epsilon have lives worth living, if barely. If epsilon were zero or less, no crowd would be large enough. And the third is treating n star as a prediction. Change the scale or epsilon, and it moves.

@chapter Try it
@words text="ε = 1/5" sub="find n*, then compare the averages"
Your turn. Pick a different epsilon, say one fifth, and find the least crowd whose total beats the three at ten. Then compare their averages. Decide which premise you'd give up, and say what you would then have to accept.
