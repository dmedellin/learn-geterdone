@chapter Two promises
@title text="Moral Dilemmas and Deontic Consistency" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
You've promised Ana to be at her door at six. You've also promised Bo to be at his, on the other side of town, at the same hour. Each promise gives you an obligation, and you can't keep both.

@art two-doors
Philosophers who call this a moral dilemma mean something stronger than that it's hard. They mean you ought to do each, you can't do both, and neither obligation overrides the other. There's no further reason that makes one of the oughts lapse.

@chapter Five sentences
@math heading="The set" items="1.  a:  ought to keep the promise to Ana@one, a;;2.  b:  ought to keep the promise to Bo@two, b;;3.  (a and b) → c:  agglomeration@three;;4.  c → d:  ought implies can@four;;5.  not d:  you cannot keep both@five"
Write it as five sentences. One, a, you ought to keep the promise to Ana. Two, b, you ought to keep the promise to Bo. Three, if a and b, then c, you ought to keep both. That's agglomeration: oughts combine. Four, if c then d, you can keep both. That's ought implies can. And five, not d. You can't keep both.

@art five-blocks
The lab finds no assignment of truth values that makes all five true. One, two and three give c. With four, that gives d. And five says not d. Take any one sentence away, though, and a model exists. So the smallest inconsistent set is all five. None of them is idle.

@words text="Not ignorance" sub="every sentence is settled, and the set still fails"
That's what a dilemma is, in this sense. A set that can't be true together. It's a fact about the principles and the situation, not about what the agent doesn't know.

@chapter Four exits
@list heading="Four ways out" items="Williams: give up agglomeration@Williams|Give up ought implies can@A second camp|One ought lapses: the orthodox view@A third|You can keep both after all@The fourth"
Each way of restoring consistency is held by someone. Williams keeps both oughts, and gives up agglomeration. A second camp keeps agglomeration and gives up ought implies can. A third denies one of the oughts. When obligations conflict, one lapses. That's the orthodox view. The fourth says you can keep both after all, by a plan not yet considered.

@art remorse-weight
Williams's argument rests on remorse. The remorse you owe, whichever promise you break, shows the obligation you set aside didn't lapse. So from, you ought to keep each, it doesn't follow that you ought to keep both. The combined obligation is never formed.

@chapter Marcus's point
@art unlucky-world
Marcus adds a point the lab makes visible. Delete sentence five, and the other four have a model. So the principles were never inconsistent with each other. The clash needed the world to supply not d. On her view, a dilemma is a consistent code meeting an unlucky situation, so there's a further obligation to arrange your life so such situations are rare.

@chapter The price of each exit
@list heading="What each exit costs" items="No agglomeration: what does failing one mean?@Reject agglomeration|No ought implies can: oughts stop guiding@Reject ought implies can|Drop an ought: the guilt has no object@Drop one"
Reject agglomeration, and it's unclear what a person is doing who fails one. Reject ought implies can, and an obligation no longer guides action, since it can demand the impossible. Drop one of the oughts, and the guilt you feel has nothing to be about. The lab shows each exit is consistent. It doesn't say which price is lowest.

@art scales-of-exits
And notice this. Standard deontic logic builds in agglomeration and ought implies can, so it makes dilemmas inconsistent by design. That's itself a position. Writing the set out lets it be stated, rather than assumed.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a dilemma is not knowing what to do@The first|Thinking the set shows the situation is impossible@The second|Expecting the lab to pick the exit@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a dilemma is not knowing what to do. You know exactly what each promise requires. The second is thinking an inconsistent set shows the situation is impossible. You did make two promises. The repair is to give up a sentence. And the third is expecting the lab to pick the exit.

@chapter Try it
@art drop-one
Try it with the five sentences. Delete each one in turn, and check that a model appears every time. Then choose the sentence you'd give up, and name its price. A dilemma is real only if every exit costs something someone won't pay.
