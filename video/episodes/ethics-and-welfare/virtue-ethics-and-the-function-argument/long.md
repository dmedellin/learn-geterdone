@chapter The argument
@title text="Virtue Ethics and the Function Argument" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
Virtue ethics asks what kind of person to be, before it asks what act to do. Its oldest argument about the good life is Aristotle's function argument. It sounds airtight when you hear it told. Let's see whether it is.

@art flute-and-eye
A flute player's good lies in playing the flute well. An eye's good lies in seeing well. A thing's good is performing its function well. What's distinctive of human beings, shared with neither plants nor animals, is reasoning. So the human good is reasoning well.

@chapter Three letters
@list heading="Three letters" items="f: reasoning is the human function@f is|g: the human good is reasoning well@g is|r: reasoning sets humans apart@and r is"
Write three sentence letters. f is, reasoning is the human function. g is, the human good is reasoning well. And r is, reasoning is what sets human beings apart from other living things.

@math heading="As usually told" items="f → g@if f then g;;r@and r;;∴ g@The conclusion is g"
The argument as told gives two premises: if f then g, and r. The conclusion is g.

@chapter The counterexample row
@math heading="The row that breaks it" items="f = F,  g = F,  r = T@f false;;f → g:  true, its antecedent is false@the first premise;;r:  true,  g:  false@the conclusion"
The lab builds the eight rows and finds one with f false, g false, and r true. On that row, reasoning distinguishes humans, but it isn't the human function. So the first premise is true, because its antecedent is false. And the conclusion fails.

@art missing-link
Nothing in the stated premises connects what's distinctive of humans to what's their function. That makes it an enthymeme, an argument that relies on a premise it doesn't state. Making the premise explicit doesn't make the argument sound. It shows a critic where to look.

@chapter The first missing premise
@words text="r → f" sub="whatever sets a kind apart is its function"
The missing premise is, if r then f. Whatever sets a kind apart is its function. Add it, and the lab finds no counterexample row. The argument is valid. Aristotle supplies something like it, saying a function is what a thing does that nothing else does.

@art laughing-test
That's the premise to press. Laughing also sets humans apart, and few would say that laughing well is the human good. So distinctiveness alone looks too weak to fix a function.

@chapter The second gap
@math heading="Stripped down" items="r,  r → f  ∴  g@Strip the argument;;row:  r = T,  f = T,  g = F@the row;;missing:  f → g@Here if f then g"
There's a second gap on the other side. Strip the argument to r, and if r then f, and ask whether it reaches g. It doesn't. The row with r true, f true, and g false is a counterexample. Here if f then g is the missing premise.

@art function-to-good
That premise is the bridge from what a thing does to what's good for it. It's the step the is and ought lesson asks everyone to price. A defender says that for a thing with a function, being good just is performing it well. A critic asks why we should care about the function of a kind we belong to.

@chapter A tool and a person
@art knife-and-person
The premise sounds compelling for a knife. Its function was given by whoever made it, and a good knife cuts well. It sounds less compelling for a person, whose function, if there is one, wasn't assigned. The lab can say the argument is valid with the premise, but not whether it holds.

@words text="The mean: no structure to test" sub="so the lesson leaves it alone"
And the lab doesn't reach further. The doctrine of the mean, practical wisdom, and how character forms by habit have no premises and conclusion to test. Any widget offered for them would be decorative, so the lesson leaves them alone.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Assuming a great philosopher's argument is valid as written@The first|Treating the added premise as free@The second|Reading the lab's silence on the mean as a verdict@And the third"
The first slip is assuming a great philosopher's argument is valid as written. The second is treating the added premise as a repair that costs nothing. The argument is now exactly as strong as that premise. And the third is reading the lab's silence on the mean as a verdict on the doctrine.

@art argument-steps
