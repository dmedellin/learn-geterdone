@chapter The shape
@title text="Slippery Slopes and Small Differences" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
This step makes no moral difference. Neither does the next, and so on. So no step does, and we may go all the way. That's a slippery slope. It's often dismissed as a fallacy, without saying which kind. Let's write one out and test the dismissal.

@chapter Forty weeks
@words text="F(k): k weeks' notice is adequate" sub="F(40) is plainly true"
Let F of k be, k weeks' notice is adequate notice. Forty weeks is plainly adequate, so F of forty is a premise.

@math heading="The chain" items="F(40)@F of forty;;F(40) → F(39)@if F of forty;;F(39) → F(38)@then F of thirty nine;;F(1) → F(0)@all the way down;;∴ F(0)@no notice at all"
One week less never makes a moral difference, so for each step there's a premise. If F of forty, then F of thirty nine. If F of thirty nine, then F of thirty eight. And so on, all the way down to F of one, then F of zero. The conclusion is F of zero: no notice at all is adequate.

@art staircase
Each of those is a tolerance conditional. It says one small step doesn't change whether a vague property holds. A slope is a start, plus a chain of them.

@chapter Classical logic
@art domino-chain
Under the classical treatment, all forty conditionals are true, the start is true, and the conclusion is true. The argument is valid. So if you find the conclusion absurd, you must give up a premise. The question is which one. That's the whole dispute, and it's why fallacy is the wrong word.

@chapter Three other treatments
@art cutoff-step
A sharp cutoff says adequacy stops at some exact point, here twenty four. F of twenty four is true and F of twenty three is false. So exactly one conditional is false, and the conclusion is false.

@art blurred-band
A borderline range says there's no fact about where adequacy stops, only admissible places to draw the line. With the range sixteen to thirty two, the cutoff can fall anywhere from seventeen to thirty two. So sixteen conditionals are true on some sharpenings and false on others, neither true nor false outright.

@words text="Super-false" sub="false on every sharpening"
No single conditional is false. But the claim that every step is tolerated is false on every sharpening. The lab calls that super false, and the conclusion is super false too.

@art fading-bars
Degrees give each conditional a value just under one, thirty nine fortieths. Chaining forty of them guarantees nothing, and the conclusion gets zero. Each step costs a fortieth of the property. Too little to notice, and enough, forty times, to lose it all.

@chapter Where the slope is sound
@math heading="A sound slope" items="F(k):  k is smaller than a million@k is smaller;;start:  F(10,000)@ten thousand;;∴ F(0):  true@zero is smaller"
Now let F of k be, k is smaller than a million, starting at ten thousand. Each step down keeps it smaller, so every premise is true. And the conclusion, zero is smaller than a million, is true. Same chain. What differs is that the premises are all true. The form was never the difference.

@chapter Conceptual and causal
@art causal-slope
The lab models the conceptual slope, where a property is said to survive each step. It doesn't model the causal slope, where allowing one step makes the next more likely. That's a claim about how people and institutions behave, and it needs evidence. The argument against such a policy is an empirical claim.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking a slope is always a fallacy@The first|Counting a small difference as zero@The second|Expecting the lab to pick the treatment@And the third"
The first slip is thinking a slippery slope is always a fallacy. The chain is valid. A slope fails when a premise does. The second is counting each step's difference as zero, when it's only small. Small differences add up. And the third is expecting the lab to say which treatment is right. That's a view about vagueness.

@chapter Try it
@list heading="Forty weeks, four ways" items="classical: all true, conclusion true@classical|cutoff 24: one false@cutoff|range 16 to 32: super-false@range|degrees: each 39/40, conclusion 0@degrees"
Run the forty week chain four ways. Classical keeps everything true. A cutoff at twenty four makes one conditional false. A range from sixteen to thirty two makes the universal claim super false. And degrees give each step thirty nine fortieths. Then choose the premise you'd give up, and say what it costs.
