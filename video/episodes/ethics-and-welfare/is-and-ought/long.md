@chapter The question
@title text="Is and Ought" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
Can a list of facts ever prove what someone ought to do? It feels like it should. But there's a gap in that step, and once you see its shape, you'll know exactly where to look in any moral argument.

@art fact-stack
An argument crosses the is ought gap when every premise describes how things are, and the conclusion says how they ought to be. Hume's remark was that such arguments usually slip an ought in between the last description and the conclusion, without announcing it.

@chapter Jones and the debt
@list heading="Three sentences" items="p: Jones promised to repay Smith@Let p be|q: Smith has not been repaid@q be|o: Jones ought to repay Smith@and o be"
Take Jones, who promised to repay Smith. Let p be, Jones promised to repay Smith. Let q be, Smith has not been repaid. And o be, Jones ought to repay Smith.

@words text="p, q ∴ o" sub="it sounds entirely natural"
The argument is p and q, therefore o. It sounds entirely natural. Notice one thing about its shape, though. The letter o appears in the conclusion and in no premise at all. That's the formal shape of the gap.

@chapter The counterexample row
@art counterexample-row
So build the truth table. Three letters give eight rows. The lab checks all eight and finds exactly one on which both premises are true and the conclusion is false.

@math heading="The one bad row" items="p = T,  q = T,  o = F@p true;;premises both true@both premises hold;;conclusion false@the conclusion fails"
It's the row with p true, q true, and o false. On that row both premises hold, and the conclusion fails. Every other row has a false premise, so it can't refute anything.

@words text="These premises do not force this conclusion" sub="not: o is false · not: there are no duties"
It's easy to hear this as something stronger, so state the limit. The row doesn't show that o is false, or that there are no duties, or that morality is opinion. It shows only that these premises do not force this conclusion. Nothing is being claimed about Jones.

@chapter The bridge
@art gap-bridge
To close the gap, you add a bridging premise, one that connects the facts to the duty. Here it's this: if p and q, then o. If Jones promised to repay and hasn't repaid, then Jones ought to repay.

@math heading="With the bridge" items="(p ∧ q) → o@if p and q;;p, q, (p ∧ q) → o  ∴  o@add it;;no counterexample row: valid@no counterexample"
In symbols, if p and q, then o. Add it as a third premise, and the lab finds no counterexample row. Every row that makes p, q and the bridge true also makes o true. The argument is valid. But the bridge isn't a description of anyone's behaviour. It's a claim that bears an ought.

@chapter Three replies
@list heading="Three replies" items="Moralist: a moral principle@The moralist|Searle: promising is an institution@Searle replies|Skeptic: the bridge just moved@The skeptic"
Each reply deserves full strength. The moralist accepts the bridge as a moral principle, argued for on its own grounds. Searle replies that promising is an institution. Saying the words in the right conditions is, by what the word means, undertaking an obligation. The skeptic answers that this only relocates the bridge. Why act inside the institution at all?

@math heading="Searle's derivation" items="u: the words of a promise, in the right conditions@Let u be;;m: Jones made a promise@m be;;u,  u → m,  m → o  ∴  o@The premises are"
Let u be, Jones uttered the words of a promise in the right conditions. Let m be, Jones made a promise. The premises are u, if u then m, and if m then o. The conclusion is o, and the lab says it's valid.

@art relocated-bridge
But is if m then o a fact about what promising is, or a moral claim in disguise? That's exactly what the lab can't tell you. To it, both are just sentence letters.

@chapter What the bridge costs
@art price-scale
So the bridge has a price, three times over. It has to be argued for, because validity gives it no support. It has to be defended for every case it covers, not only for Jones. And it can be denied without inconsistency by someone who grants every fact.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading Hume as saying moral claims are false@The first|Treating the bridge as a harmless extra@The second|Taking valid as the end of the matter@And the third"
The first slip is reading Hume as saying moral claims are false. With the bridge, o follows. The second is treating the bridge as a harmless extra. It does the work, so a critic should look there first. And the third is taking valid as the end of the matter. Validity never checks the premises.
