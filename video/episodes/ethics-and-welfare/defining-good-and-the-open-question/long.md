@chapter The question
@title text="Defining Good" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
What does good mean? Pleasant, perhaps. Or wanted. Either answer is a definition, and a definition makes a claim about every case you can describe. So you can test it, case by case.

@chapter Moore's open question
@art closed-open
G. E. Moore started from a contrast. If a bachelor is an unmarried man is a real definition, then asking of an unmarried man whether he's a bachelor is a closed question. No competent speaker can sensibly doubt it.

@words text="It's pleasant. But is it good?" sub="an open question"
But ask of a pleasant thing whether it's good, and the question is open. Someone who asks it isn't confused. So good doesn't simply mean pleasant.

@art case-table
The lab doesn't run Moore's test. It runs the method the test motivates, the method of cases. Describe a case, settle its verdict before consulting any definition, and then see which definitions agree.

@chapter Five cases
@list heading="Four conditions" items="P: pleasant@P for pleasant|D: desired@D for desired|A: authentic, beliefs about the life are true@A for authentic|H: harmless to others@H for harmless"
A proposed definition is a formula in four conditions. P for pleasant. D for desired. A for authentic, meaning the person's beliefs about the life are true. And H for harmless to others. A formula fits a case when it's true exactly when the case is good.

@art experience-machine
Here's the most famous one. The experience machine gives you pleasant experiences that you want, but they aren't real. Your beliefs about your life are false.

@list heading="The case table" items="A good life: good@a good life|A hard-won project: good@a hard won project|The experience machine: not good@the experience machine|The satisfied sadist: not good@the satisfied sadist|The unwanted cure: good@the unwanted cure"
There are five cases. A good life, which has all four conditions, is good. A hard won project, which isn't pleasant, is good. The experience machine, which isn't authentic, is not good. The satisfied sadist, who isn't harmless, is not good. And the unwanted cure, neither pleasant nor desired, is good.

@chapter Testing hedonism
@math heading="Hedonism: good = P" items="good life: P = 1, good → fits@the good life;;machine, sadist: P = 1, not good → too broad@too broad;;project, cure: P = 0, good → too narrow@too narrow;;1 fit in 5@one case"
Hedonism says good means pleasant, the formula P. It's true of the good life, which fits. It's also true of the machine and the sadist, which the table says aren't good. That's too broad. And it's false of the project and the cure, which are good. Too narrow. It fits only one case.

@art two-directions
Those are the two ways to fail. Too broad means the definition says good where the table says not, so the repair is a stricter condition. Too narrow means it says not where the table says good, so the repair is a weaker one. A definition can fail both ways at once.

@words text="good = D" sub="too broad: machine, sadist · too narrow: cure"
Desire fares differently. Good means desired, the formula D, fits the good life and the project. But it's too broad on the machine and the sadist, and too narrow on the cure. A different shape of failure, from different cases.

@chapter A candidate
@math heading="Pairs search" items="A ∧ H@It finds A and H;;fits all five cases@fits all five"
The lab then searches pairs of conditions joined by and or or. It finds A and H: good when the life is authentic and harms no one. It fits all five cases.

@art next-case
That's a candidate, and a modest one. A sixth case could break it. And a philosopher would still ask whether it passes the open question.

@chapter Rejecting a verdict
@words text="Machine: good?" sub="hedonism: 1 fit → 2 fits, 3 failures"
What if you reject a verdict? The hedonist can say the machine life is good. Change that verdict, and hedonism fits two cases instead of one. It still fails on the other three. Rejecting a verdict is allowed, but it's a specific move with a specific price, not a shrug about taste.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Calling a counterexample a matter of taste@The first|Reading a fit as what good means@The second|Mixing up the two directions@And the third"
The first slip is calling a counterexample a matter of taste. The disagreement is about one specific cell. The second is reading a fit as the discovery of what good means. It's a candidate only. And the third is mixing up the two directions, which sends the repair the wrong way.

@chapter Try it
@art scales-of-cases
Try the worked example yourself. Put hedonism against the five cases, row by row, and mark each result: fits, too broad, or too narrow. Then flip the machine's verdict, and count what hedonism gains, and what it still loses.
