@chapter The question
@title text="Kant and the Universalisability Test" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
The trolley lesson ended with a forbidden list typed by hand. It struck an act, but never said why. Deontological theories try to say why. Kant's first test is one such account, and you can give it a checkable structure.

@words text="Act only on a maxim you can will as a universal law" sub="not a forecast of consequences"
Kant says to act only on a maxim you can at the same time will to be a universal law. The usual gloss is, what if everyone did that? And that's where the trouble starts, because it sounds like a forecast of consequences. It isn't.

@chapter The maxim
@art maxim-card
A maxim is the rule an agent acts on, stated with the act, the circumstance and the aim. Here's the one to test. When I need money, I will promise to repay it, knowing I won't, in order to be given it.

@chapter The game
@art ten-players
Model promising as a game with ten players. Each one either keeps promises, cooperating, or makes false ones, defecting. Let k be the number of the other nine who keep their word.

@math heading="Payoffs, k of the other 9 honest" items="honest:  C(k) = 2@An honest promiser;;liar:  D(k) = 5k/9@A liar;;k = 0:  liar 0@nothing;;k = 9:  liar 5@every promise is"
An honest promiser gets two, whatever the others do. A liar gets five k over nine. That's nothing when no one else is honest, because no promise is believed. And it's five when everyone else is honest, because every promise is.

@chapter Two figures
@math heading="The two figures" items="alone:  liar 5, honest 2,  gain 3@when alone;;all lie:  liar 0, honest 2,  gain −2@when everyone"
The test reads two numbers. First, the liar's gain when alone. All nine others are honest, so the liar gets five, where honesty gets two. A gain of three. Second, the same choice when everyone makes it. No one is honest, so the liar gets zero, against the two honesty yields. A difference of minus two.

@art collapsed-practice
That zero is a contradiction in conception. Adopted by everyone, the practice the act depends on couldn't exist, so the act couldn't achieve its aim. A promise is believed only where promises are usually kept. Where everyone lies, there's nothing left to exploit.

@chapter Not a forecast
@words text="Highest total: 7 honest, 3 liars" sub="a sum of welfare does not condemn the maxim"
Here's why it isn't a forecast. A forecast asks whether a world of universal lying is worse. But in this table, total welfare among the ten is highest with seven honest and three liars, because an honest promiser loses nothing to a liar. So a sum of welfare doesn't condemn the maxim. Kant's test does, for a different reason. The liar can't do what the lie was for.

@chapter Queue-jumping
@art queue-line
Here's a second example.

@math heading="Cutting in line" items="wait:  2 + 2k/9@a waiting person;;jump:  1 + 5k/9@a jumper;;alone:  jumper gains 2@Alone;;all jump:  1 against 4@When all jump"
Cutting in line pays only where others wait. A waiting person gets two plus two k over nine, and a jumper gets one plus five k over nine. Alone, the jumper gains two. When all jump, the jumper gets one, where the orderly queue gave four. Nobody's left to be ahead of.

@art tax-pool
The tax case is less clean. Evading a tax that funds something everyone uses pays whatever the others do. But a world without the tax is perfectly conceivable. Kant's second failure fits better, a contradiction in the will. The evader can't coherently will a world without the public good, because the evader wants it.

@chapter The limits
@art narrow-maxim
Kant didn't compute anything. The lab does, only by treating the maxim's point as a payoff you supply. And it can't choose how general the maxim should be. I will lie to this person on this day passes any such test, and most critics begin there.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the test as a forecast@The first|Treating the two figures as a verdict@The second|Forgetting that the maxim fixes the verdict@And the third"
The first slip is reading the test as a forecast of consequences. The second is treating the two figures as a verdict. Which failure they show depends on why the second figure matters. And the third is forgetting that how you state the maxim fixes the verdict.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Liar among nine honest" sub="alone: +3 · all: −2"
Rework the liar yourself. Write the honest payoff and the liar's payoff as k changes, and read the two figures. Then name the practice the lie exploits, and say why the second figure means the rule can't be a universal law.
