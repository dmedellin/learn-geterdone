@chapter The question
@title text="Double Effect, Means and Side Effects" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
Two acts can cause the same harm and bring about the same good. One doctrine says the first can be permissible, and the second can't. The difference is whether the harm is the means to the good, or just a side effect. Here's a way to test that.

@list heading="Double effect" items="The act itself is permissible@the act itself|The bad effect is not the means@the bad effect|The good is proportionate to the harm@and the good"
The doctrine of double effect, usually traced to Aquinas, says an act with a good and a bad effect can be permissible when three things hold. The act itself is permissible. The bad effect isn't intended as a means to the good. And the good is proportionate to the harm. This lesson takes just one clause, the second.

@chapter The switch, as equations
@list heading="Five variables" items="L: the lever is pulled@L for|M: a man is on the side track@M for|E: the trolley goes onto the side track@E for|H: the man is hit@H for|F: the five survive@and F for"
Take the switch case from the trolley lesson. L for the lever is pulled. M for a man is on the side track. E for the trolley goes onto the side track. H for the man is hit. And F for the five survive.

@art lever-switch
Pull the lever, and the trolley turns.

@math heading="The switch" items="E = L@E equals L;;H = E and M@H equals;;F = E@F equals E"
The model has three equations. E equals L. H equals E and M. And F equals E. In the actual case, everything is true.

@art flip-switch
Now the test. Set the harm to false, and leave the rest of the model alone. F equals E, and E hasn't changed, so the five still survive. The man being hit isn't a cause of the five surviving at all. They live because the trolley left their track. His death happens on the way.

@chapter The loop
@art loop-model
Now the loop. The side track curves back to the main one, so the trolley would reach the five anyway, unless something stops it. And the man's body does.

@math heading="The loop" items="E = L@the same;;H = E and M@the same;;F = E and H@third one changes"
The first two equations are the same. The third one changes. Now F equals E and H. The five survive only if the trolley turns, and the man is hit.

@art flip-loop
Flip H here, and F becomes false. The five die. So the harm is a but for cause of the good. It's a means. Same lever, same body count, same outcome for the five. The two cases differ in one equation.

@words text="Means: the good depends on the harm" sub="side effect: the good comes about anyway"
That's the lab's sense of means. A harm is a means to a good when the good depends on it. Set the harm to false, hold everything else, and the good fails. It's a side effect when the good comes about whether or not the harm occurs.

@chapter Two bombers
@art two-bombers
The same test separates two bombers. A strategic bomber destroys a munitions factory, and civilians next to it die. The war is shortened because the factory is gone. A terror bomber kills the same number of civilians, in order to break morale. The war is shortened because morale breaks. Same deaths, same benefit, different verdicts.

@chapter Three limits
@list heading="What the lab can't say" items="Whether the act is permissible, or proportionate@It tests one clause|Whether a means is worse than a side effect@central claim|Whether the equations describe the case fairly@the equations"
There are three limits. It tests one clause only. It can't say that a means is worse than a side effect, which is the doctrine's central claim, and the one most disputed. Many people judge the loop permissible. And the equations are a description. Redescribe the switch so the man's weight slows the trolley, and the verdict changes, with no change in anyone's conduct.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the lab reads intentions@The first|Treating a side effect as a harm that doesn't matter@The second|Reading the verdict off the case, not the model@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the lab reads intentions. Causal models contain no mental states. The second is treating a side effect as a harm that doesn't matter. The man still dies, and proportionality is where that's argued. And the third is reading the verdict off the case, instead of the model you wrote.

@chapter Try it
@art equation-blocks
Try it with both models. Write the three equations, flip H, and read whether F changes. Then rewrite the strategic bomber so the war is shortened only by the civilian deaths, and watch the verdict move.
