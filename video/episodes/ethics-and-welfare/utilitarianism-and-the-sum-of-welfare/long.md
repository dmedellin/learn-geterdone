@chapter The rule
@title text="Utilitarianism and the Sum of Welfare" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
Here's utilitarianism stated as plainly as possible. Add up each person's welfare, and prefer the larger total. It's a rule that turns a whole distribution into one number. Let's see what that number notices, and what it doesn't.

@art welfare-list
An outcome is a list, one welfare number per person. The numbers are stipulated by the example. Whether welfare can really be measured like that is a real question, and the lab doesn't answer it. Keep the people in the same order in both lists, so you can read off the changes.

@chapter Two outcomes
@math heading="Add each list" items="A = 10, 10, 10:  total 30@Under A;;B = 1, 30, 2:  total 33@Under B;;B ranks above A@larger total"
Compare A, ten, ten, ten, with B, one, thirty, two. Under A, the total is thirty. Under B, it's thirty three. So the rule ranks B above A, because the larger total wins. That's the whole of the rule.

@words text="Add. Don't average, weight or sort." sub="larger total ranks higher · equal totals tie"
Notice what you didn't do. You didn't average, weight, or sort anything. You added each list and compared the sums. A larger total ranks higher, and equal totals tie. Later lessons change exactly these choices, so it's worth seeing the plain version first.

@chapter Who gains, who loses
@math heading="From A to B" items="first:  10 → 1,  −9@The first;;second:  10 → 30,  +20@the second;;third:  10 → 2,  −8@the third;;net:  +3@net"
Now look person by person. The first goes from ten to one, minus nine. The second goes from ten to thirty, plus twenty. The third goes from ten to two, minus eight. The net change is plus three, which is just the difference of the totals.

@art two-down-one-up
So two of the three people are much worse off under B, and the rule prefers it anyway. The person left with one is the price of the person with thirty.

@words text="The greatest good of the greatest number" sub="as a rule of aggregation: one aim, the sum"
Bentham's slogan, the greatest good of the greatest number, makes that sound impossible, because it sounds like two aims. As a rule of aggregation, there's one: the sum. When the two pull apart, as here, the number of people helped doesn't count. Bentham himself came to drop the second clause.

@chapter A transfer
@art swap-bars
Next, compare five and fifteen with fifteen and five. The two people have simply swapped welfare. Each list totals twenty, so the rule calls them equal. A sum forgets who has which number. Two lists with the same numbers in a different order always have the same total, so the rule is indifferent to how welfare is spread.

@words text="Same total, any spread" sub="from the badly off to the well off, or the reverse"
The same indifference covers any redistribution that leaves the sum alone, whether it takes from the badly off to give to the well off, or the reverse.

@chapter A sacrifice
@math heading="A sacrifice" items="A = 10, 10, 10:  total 30@ten, ten, ten;;B = 0, 15, 16:  total 31@zero, fifteen;;the rule prefers B@prefers B"
Now compare ten, ten, ten with zero, fifteen, sixteen. One person is left with nothing, and the total rises from thirty to thirty one. The rule prefers B.

@art empty-bar
That person has nothing at all, and the rule still counts B as better.

@art diminishing-returns
These are the cases critics press, and the replies deserve full strength. A utilitarian can say that in practice, resources have diminishing returns in welfare, so sacrifices like this are rare. That's a claim about how often the sum favours them. It leaves the verdict on these numbers where it was. Another reply accepts the verdict, and holds that the intuition against it should be revised.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the slogan as two aims@The first|Expecting the total to favour fairness@The second|Reading the arithmetic as an argument@And the third"
The first slip is reading the slogan as two separate aims. The rule has one. The second is expecting the total to favour fairness. A sum is the same for every ordering. And the third is reading the arithmetic as an argument for or against the theory. The numbers are stipulated. The argument lives in those stipulations, not in the sum.
