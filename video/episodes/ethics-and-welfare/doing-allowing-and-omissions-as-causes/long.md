@chapter The question
@title text="Doing, Allowing and Omissions as Causes" sub="Ethics and Welfare" art=thumb
Many people hold that killing is worse than letting die. The commonest support is a claim about cause. The person who pushes causes the drowning, while the person who watches only fails to prevent it. That claim is easy to test.

@words text="The but-for test" sub="does not care whether the cause is an event or an absence"
It's easy, because the but for test doesn't care whether a cause is an event or an absence. Flip the candidate cause, recompute, and see whether the effect changes. That works just as well for something not done.

@chapter The lifeguard
@art lifeguard-pool
Let L be, the lifeguard is in reach. W, the lifeguard is willing. R, the swimmer is rescued. And D, the swimmer drowns. The rescue happens only if the lifeguard is both in reach and willing. The swimmer drowns when there's no rescue.

@math heading="The model" items="R = L and W@R equals;;D = not R@D equals;;actual:  L = 1,  W = 0@In the case;;so  R = 0,  D = 1@So R is"
R equals L and W, and D equals not R. In the case as it happened, the lifeguard is in reach and unwilling. L is one and W is zero. So R is zero, and D is one.

@art flip-rescue
Now take the rescue not performed as the candidate cause. Set R to one, and D becomes zero. So the omission is a but for cause, in exactly the sense a struck match is a cause of a fire.

@words text="An omission is a value, not a gap" sub="a variable whose actual value is 0"
An omission is the absence of an act that was available. In the model, it's just a variable whose actual value is zero. You test it like any other: flip it, and recompute.

@chapter Does this prove too much?
@art out-of-reach
The obvious objection is that this proves too much. Everyone has failed to rescue every swimmer in the world. But availability is an equation, not a footnote. If the lifeguard is out of reach, L is zero, and flipping W changes nothing. Of everything not done, the lab selects what was available.

@chapter Two lifeguards
@math heading="Two lifeguards, both unwilling" items="either would do:  R = W1 or W2@If either;;flip one:  rescue,  so each is but-for@each omission;;both needed:  R = W1 and W2@If instead both;;flip one:  nothing,  a joint cause@a joint cause"
Now two lifeguards. If either would do, R is W one or W two. Both are unwilling, and flipping either one alone produces a rescue. So each omission is a but for cause. If instead both are needed, as when two people must carry a boat, flipping one changes nothing. The lab reports a joint cause. Neither is a but for cause alone, and each is a cause together with the other.

@art two-rescuers
So the thought that neither is responsible, because the other could have gone, isn't one the structure supports.

@chapter The shallow pond
@art shallow-pond
A child is drowning in a pond, shallow enough to wade into, at the cost of ruined shoes. Flip whether you wade in, and both the drowning and the shoes flip. The lab shows the death depends on your choice. Whether the shoes outweigh it isn't something it computes.

@chapter Doing versus allowing
@list heading="If pushing is worse, why?" items="The pusher initiates the harm@initiates|Duties not to harm outweigh duties to aid@duties not to harm|The cost to the rescuer differs@the cost"
A pusher's act is a but for cause too. So if pushing is worse than watching, the reason isn't that one is a cause and the other isn't. Candidates are that the pusher initiates the harm, that duties not to harm are stronger than duties to aid, or that the cost to the rescuer differs. Each is a premise about what matters, and none of them is computed here.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking an omission can't be a cause@The first|Spreading responsibility until it vanishes@The second|Treating the causal verdict as the moral one@And the third"
The first slip is thinking an omission can't be a cause. The second is spreading responsibility until it vanishes, just because someone else could have helped. And the third is treating the causal verdict as the moral one. The lab shows the drowning depended on the omission, not that the omission was wrong.
