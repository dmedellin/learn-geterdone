@chapter The question
@title text="Multiplying Polynomials" sub="Polynomials and Factoring" art=thumb
There's one rule for multiplying any two polynomials, however long they are: distribute until no brackets remain. Everything else, including FOIL, is bookkeeping for that rule.

@chapter Term times term
@math heading="Coefficients multiply, exponents add" items="(3x²)(4x⁵) = 12x⁷@three x squared;;(−2x³y)(5xy⁴) = −10x⁴y⁵@minus two x cubed y"
Start small. Three x squared times four x to the fifth is twelve x to the seventh. The coefficients multiply, and the exponents add. Minus two x cubed y times five x y to the fourth is minus ten x to the fourth y to the fifth.

@art adding-powers
The x exponents give three plus one, four, and the y exponents give one plus four, five. Each variable is tracked on its own. The powers add because x squared times x to the fifth is two x's times five x's, which is seven x's.

@chapter Term times polynomial
@math items="3x(2x² − 5x + 4) = 6x³ − 15x² + 12x@three x times"
Three x times two x squared minus five x plus four is six x cubed minus fifteen x squared plus twelve x.

@art reach-every-term
That's the distributive law directly. The three x reaches every term inside the bracket, the same discipline as the minus sign when you subtract.

@chapter Polynomial times polynomial
@art product-grid
To multiply two polynomials, multiply each term of the first by each term of the second, and add the results. A polynomial with m terms times one with n terms makes m times n products before any collecting. Count them first, and you'll know when you've finished.

@math heading="(x + 4)(x − 7)" items="First   x · x = x²@first;;Outer   x · (−7) = −7x@outer;;Inner   4 · x = 4x@inner;;Last   4 · (−7) = −28@last;;x² − 3x − 28@collect"
For two binomials that's two times two, four products, remembered as FOIL. First, x times x is x squared. Outer, x times minus seven is minus seven x. Inner, four times x is four x. Last, four times minus seven is minus twenty-eight. Then collect, to get x squared minus three x minus twenty-eight.

@art foil-runs-out
FOIL is worth knowing as a memory aid, and worth distrusting as a method, because it only describes the two by two case. A binomial times a trinomial has no outer and inner to speak of. It has six products. The counting rule survives where the acronym doesn't.

@chapter A worked example
@math heading="(2x − 3)(x² + 4x − 5)" items="degree 1 + 2 = 3 · leading term 2x³@one plus two;;2 × 3 = 6 products@six products"
Predict before you multiply. The degree is one plus two, three, and the leading term is two x times x squared, two x cubed. And two terms times three terms means six products owed.

@math heading="Distribute one term at a time" items="2x · x² = 2x³   2x · 4x = 8x²   2x · (−5) = −10x@two x across;;−3 · x² = −3x²   −3 · 4x = −12x   −3 · (−5) = +15@minus three across"
Take two x across the trinomial: two x cubed, eight x squared, minus ten x. Then minus three across it: minus three x squared, minus twelve x, plus fifteen. Each on its own line.

@math items="2x³ + (8 − 3)x² + (−10 − 12)x + 15@collect;;2x³ + 5x² − 22x + 15@the answer"
Now collect down the columns. Eight minus three, and minus ten minus twelve. So the answer is two x cubed plus five x squared minus twenty-two x plus fifteen.

@art collect-columns
That's why each term gets its own line. Six products written on two lines are far easier to check than six products written in a row.

@words text="At x = 1: (−1)(0) = 0 and 2 + 5 − 22 + 15 = 0" sub="constant term: (−3)(−5) = 15"
It starts two x cubed, as predicted. The constant term is always the product of the two constants, minus three times minus five, fifteen. And at x equals one, both the factors and the answer give zero.

@chapter Degree, predicted
@art degree-sum
The product's degree is the sum of the degrees. Degree three times degree four gives degree seven, always. The leading terms multiply to give the leading term, and two nonzero numbers can't multiply to zero, so it never cancels.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Multiplying the exponents instead of adding@The first|Trusting FOIL beyond two binomials@The second|Forgetting to collect@The third"
The first slip is multiplying the exponents. x squared times x to the fifth is x to the seventh, not x to the tenth. That's the rule for a power of a power. The second is trusting FOIL beyond two binomials. Count m times n instead. The third is forgetting to collect. Minus seven x plus four x still has to become minus three x.

@chapter Try it
@words text="(x + 2)(2x² − x + 3)" sub="degree 3 · leading term 2x³ · 6 products"
Your turn. x plus two, times two x squared minus x plus three. Degree three, leading term two x cubed, and six partial products. Write all six, collect, and test at x equals one.

@math items="2x³ + 3x² + x + 6@two x cubed;;at x = 1: both give 12@give twelve"
You should get two x cubed plus three x squared plus x plus six, and both forms give twelve at x equals one. If your x squared term is missing, one of the middle products was skipped.
