@chapter The question
@title text="The Perceptual Span" sub="Reading Faster · How the Eyes Read" art=thumb
How far does one glance reach? Some speed reading courses say a trained eye takes in a whole line at once. This lesson measures how far your eyes identify letters from one fixation, and sets that against what eye tracking found in reading.

@art flash-row
The drill runs twenty flashes. Each starts with a plus sign where your eyes rest. Then a row of fifteen random letters appears for a hundred and fifty milliseconds, and a mask of hash signs covers it. Then three positions are marked, one at a time, and you pick the letter that was there from four, or say you didn't see it.

@chapter Too short to look
@words text="150 ms < 175 to 200 ms" sub="the time needed to start a saccade"
Why a hundred and fifty milliseconds? Because starting a saccade takes about a hundred and seventy five to two hundred. The flash is over before your eyes could move to it. So everything you report was seen from the one fixation on the centre.

@art marked-later
And the positions are marked only after the flash has gone. You can't know in advance which will be asked, so your answers sample what was available everywhere in the row.

@chapter Scoring by position
@math heading="Scoring" items="positions −7 … +7@minus seven;;accuracy = right / asked@letters right there;;span = farthest position with accuracy ≥ 1/2@at least one half"
Answers are scored by position, from minus seven on the left to plus seven on the right. Accuracy at a position is the letters right there over the times it was asked. Your span is the farthest position on each side where accuracy was at least one half.

@words text="A guess is right 1 time in 4" sub="about 4 asks per position"
One caution. With four choices, a pure guess is right one time in four. And twenty flashes of three marks ask each position only about four times on average. So one lucky answer can move a position by a quarter.

@chapter Where identification ends
@math heading="Typical preset, right side" items="+3: 6/10 = 60% ≥ 1/2@plus three;;+4: 4/10 = 40% < 1/2@plus four;;span: left 3, right 3@left three"
The typical preset is a reader whose every position was asked ten times. At plus three, six of ten: sixty percent, at least a half. At plus four, four of ten: below a half. So identification ends between them. The left reads the same, so the span is left three, right three. The strong preset, with six of ten at four, reads left four, right four.

@art symmetric
Notice both spans are symmetric. A row of random letters has no direction to read in, and no words to recognise, so nothing pulls attention one way. That's what makes this a different span from the one in reading.

@chapter The span in reading
@art moving-window
In nineteen seventy five, McConkie and Rayner measured the reading span with a moving window. As a reader's eyes moved, a computer changed the text outside a window around each fixation. When the window was wide enough that reading went on at normal speed, it held everything the reader was using.

@list heading="The reading span (Rayner 1998)" items="3 to 4 letters left@three to four|14 to 15 letters right@fourteen to fifteen|words identified within 7 to 8 right@seven to eight"
The answer is about three to four letters to the left of the fixation, and fourteen to fifteen to the right. Words are identified only within about seven to eight letters to the right. Beyond that, the reader picks up mostly the lengths of words.

@art lopsided
The lopsidedness is attention, not the eye. For readers of Hebrew, which runs right to left, the span is larger to the left. The span is also smaller on hard text, and somewhat larger in faster, more skilled readers. So it does move a little.

@chapter A whole line?
@art long-line
What it doesn't do, on any evidence, is reach a whole line. A line of sixty five characters is about eight word identification spans long. 

@words text="65 characters ≈ 8 word-identification spans"
If your drill came out like the typical preset, past three or four letters either side, even letters you were trying to catch were mostly guesses.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking in a line at a glance@The first|Reading the drill's span as the reading span@The second|Trusting one position's score@And the third"
The first slip is believing a trained eye takes in a line at a glance. The second is reading the drill's span as the reading span. Symmetric left three, right three doesn't contradict the reading figures. It answers a different question. And the third is trusting one position's score. Read the pattern across positions.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Run the twenty flashes" sub="keep your eyes on the +"
Your turn. Run the twenty flashes. Keep your eyes on the plus sign, and when you didn't see a letter, say so rather than guess. Then write your span next to three to four left and fourteen to fifteen right, and say in a sentence why yours is roughly symmetric.
