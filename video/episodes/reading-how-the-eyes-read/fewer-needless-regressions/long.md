@chapter The question
@title text="Fewer Needless Regressions" sub="Reading Faster · How the Eyes Read" art=thumb
Some backward glances repair understanding. Others just follow a mind that drifted off the line. The useful question isn't how to stop looking back. It's whether the looking back that follows drifting can be made rarer.

@chapter Repair and drift
@art repair-vs-drift
A repair regression goes back because the sense didn't hold: a word misread, or a sentence that turned.

@art drift-path
A lapse is different. The eyes kept moving while attention went elsewhere, and rereading is how you notice. You've lost your place, so you go back to find it.

@words text="A teaching device" sub="the studies measured regressions, not their reasons"
Be clear about one thing. Splitting regressions this way is a teaching device. The studies measured regressions, not their reasons. And the last lesson showed the repairs carry comprehension, so training away every look back trains away the repairs as well.

@chapter The drill
@art two-readings-count
So there are two readings. Reading A at your natural pace, with no pacer, logged as a test. Reading B behind the pacer at your hold target, logged as a condition. As you read, keep a count of the times your attention left the text: you lost your place, or reread for no reason.

@art tally
After each reading, before the questions, the lab asks for that number. The pacer's advocates expect it to be smaller behind the pacer, because a moving highlight is harder to drift away from than a still page. It's worth having that experience for yourself.

@words text="A self-report" sub="you count only the lapses you noticed"
But the count is a self report. You count the lapses you noticed, and some pass unnoticed. Your eyes regressed many more times than that, mostly without your noticing.

@chapter The size of the prize
@math heading="The budget, with illustrative shares" items="3/20 back: 15/17 fixations, 225 ms → ≈ 266.7@three twentieths;;7/100 back: (3/4) / (93/100) = 25/31 fixations@seven hundredths;;25/31 × 255 ≈ 205.6 ms → ≈ 291.8@two ninety one point eight"
The eye movement budget puts a size on it. Say a reader's regressions are three twentieths of fixations: that's fifteen seventeenths of a fixation a word, and about two sixty six point seven words a minute. If steadier attention brought the share down to seven hundredths, it's twenty five thirty firsts of a fixation, and about two ninety one point eight.

@art prize-not-promise
That's about twenty five words a minute, at the same comprehension, if the regressions cut were all lapses. The shares are invented for the example. Nobody knows yours. Treat the figure as the size of the prize, not a promise of it.

@chapter A worked pair
@math heading="Pacer-steadies preset" items="A: 240 × 5/6 = 200 · 4 lapses@four lapses;;B: 264 × 5/6 ≈ 220.0 · 1 lapse@one lapse;;+20 < one question's worth@The difference is about twenty"
Here's a reader who read A at two forty words a minute, five of six right, an effective rate of two hundred, and counted four lapses. Then B behind the pacer at two sixty four, five of six again, about two twenty, and one lapse. The difference is about twenty, less than one question's worth at these rates.

@words text="4 lapses against 1" sub="the part worth noting, as a self-report"
So the effective rates don't show a result. The lapse count, four against one, is the part worth noting, as a self report. And B's rate was the pacer's, so the lab doesn't set it against your baseline range.

@chapter What a pacer can do
@art pacer-limits
A pacer can do something for drift. It gives the eyes somewhere to be. What it can't do is widen the window a fixation takes in, or make a regression that repairs the sense unnecessary.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Every look back is wasted time@The first|The lapse count measures regressions@The second|Crediting the pacer with B's rate@And the third"
The first slip is treating every look back as wasted time. The second is reading the lapse count as a measure of regressions. And the third is crediting the pacer with B's rate. The comparison that means something is effective rate, and the difference has to exceed one question.

@chapter Try it
@words text="A: 240 wpm, 5 of 6 · B: 264 wpm, 5 of 6" sub="effective rates? a result?"
Your turn. Work it out for yourself. Reading A, two forty words a minute, five of six. Reading B, two sixty four, five of six. What are the effective rates, and is the difference a result? You should get two hundred and two twenty, and no: one question at these rates is forty to forty four.
