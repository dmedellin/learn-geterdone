@chapter The question
@title text="Training the Periphery" sub="Reading Faster · How the Eyes Read" art=thumb
Speed reading courses love a particular eye exercise. You get faster at it, often quickly. The question is whether that makes you a faster reader, and how you'd know if it did.

@art schulte-grid
It's called a Schulte table: a five by five grid of the numbers one to twenty five, in random order. You keep your eyes on the centre as well as you can, and click one, two, three and on to twenty five. The lab times it, and counts wrong clicks. You do three tables in a row. Then a reading at your natural pace, logged as a test.

@chapter The claim
@art wide-awareness
The table is a staple of speed reading courses, as a way to train what the eyes take in away from the centre. The tradition's claim is that this wider awareness carries over into reading, so the eyes take in more of each line.

@chapter Three tables
@math heading="One reader, one day" items="first: 62 / 25 = 2.48 s per cell@sixty two;;third: 41 / 25 = 1.64 s per cell@forty one;;2.48 − 1.64 = 0.84 s per cell@point eight four"
Here's one reader. Their first table took sixty two seconds: sixty two over twenty five is two point four eight seconds a cell. The third took forty one: one point six four a cell. That's point eight four seconds a cell faster, about a third off the time in three tries.

@art practice-curve
That's the kind of gain practice brings to any new task. Practice improves the practised task. So what the faster table shows is that you got faster at finding numbers in a grid.

@chapter Transfer
@words text="Transfer has to be shown, not assumed"
Whether it makes you faster at a different task, here reading, is called transfer. And it has to be shown, not assumed.

@words text="The evidence for transfer is weak" sub="Rayner and colleagues, 2016"
Rayner and colleagues' twenty sixteen review found the evidence for that kind of transfer from eye exercises weak. Reviews of brain training games have mostly reported the same pattern: better at the game, little change elsewhere.

@chapter Test it on yourself
@art two-series
You can test it on yourself. Do a few tables before some of your test readings, and write each table time beside the test's effective rate in a log note. Over several weeks, two lines will form.

@words text="Tables faster, tests inside the range" sub="no transfer, for you, so far"
If the table times fall and the test rates stay inside your baseline range, the exercise hasn't transferred, for you, so far.

@art confounds
And if both improve, that's not yet transfer either. The Daily Drill has been running the whole time, and it trains reading directly. The passages differ from week to week. And a reader keeping a careful log is also paying more attention.

@words text="Weeks with tables · weeks without" sub="the Daily Drill held the same"
The comparison that would separate them is a stretch of weeks with the tables, and a stretch without, with the Daily Drill held the same.

@chapter Even the table time
@art search-click
Even the table time isn't simple to read. A faster table could come from better search, quicker clicking, or more seen away from the centre. And the Perceptual Span lesson showed how quickly letter identification falls off a few positions out. The time alone can't say which.

@chapter The routine
@list heading="About ten minutes" items="Three tables, eyes on the centre@Three tables|Then the test reading@test reading|Both in one note@one note|Read the two series every few weeks@every few weeks"
Three tables, eyes on the centre, and write down the times. Then the test reading, always last, and note its effective rate. Put both in one note with the date. And read the two series every few weeks: did the tables fall, did the tests leave your range, and what else changed?

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Faster exercises mean faster reading@The first|Two rising lines as cause and effect@The second|Treating the table time as a reading@And the third"
The first slip is believing faster eye exercises make reading faster. The second is reading two rising lines as cause and effect. And the third is treating the table time as a reading. It's logged as other, so it never enters your baseline or tests. Only the reading is a reading.

@chapter Try it
@words text="60 s → 35 s · 230 → 260 over six weeks" sub="what does it show?"
Your turn. Over six weeks your table time fell from sixty to thirty five seconds, and your test effective rate rose from two thirty to two sixty. What does that show? Think about what else ran in those weeks. Both improved, which is consistent with transfer, but has other explanations.
