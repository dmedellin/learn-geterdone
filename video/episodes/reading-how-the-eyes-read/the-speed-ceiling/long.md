@chapter The question
@title text="The Speed Ceiling" sub="Reading Faster · How the Eyes Read" art=thumb
A thousand words a minute. Twenty five thousand. You'll see claims like these. Here's a line of arithmetic that settles most of them before any drill: turn the claimed rate into the characters each fixation would have to take in.

@chapter From a rate to a glance
@math heading="Characters each fixation must advance" items="time per word = 60,000 / rate@sixty thousand over the rate;;fixations per word = time per word / (fixation + saccade)@divide that;;characters per fixation = 6 / fixations per word@six over;;needed = rate × 6 × (fixation + saccade) / 60,000@put together"
A rate fixes the time per word: sixty thousand over the rate, in milliseconds. Divide that by the time one fixation and its saccade take, and you have fixations per word. Then six over that is the characters each fixation must advance. Put together, it's the rate, times six, times the stop, over sixty thousand.

@chapter Two thresholds
@art two-spans
Now set that against two thresholds. Words are identified within about eight characters of the fixation. And the perceptual span reaches about fifteen to the right. Needing more than eight means taking in words that aren't identified. Needing more than fifteen means taking in text that isn't seen.

@chapter Three claims
@math heading="225 ms + 30 ms, 6 a word, no regressions" items="500 × 6 × 255 / 60,000 = 12.75@five hundred;;1,000 → 25.5 characters@a thousand;;25,000 → 637.5 characters@twenty five thousand"
At two twenty five and thirty milliseconds, a claim of five hundred words a minute, about twice an ordinary rate, needs twelve point seven five characters. A thousand needs twenty five point five. Twenty five thousand needs six hundred and thirty seven point five.

@list heading="The verdicts" items="500: beyond identification, within the span@twelve point seven five|1,000: beyond the perceptual span@beyond the perceptual span|25,000: about ten lines a glance@ten lines"
So twelve point seven five is more than a fixation identifies, though not more than it sees. Twenty five point five is beyond the perceptual span. And six thirty seven point five is about ten lines of sixty five characters, taken in at each glance.

@art claim-overflow
The bigger the claim, the further past both spans each glance would have to reach.

@art bookshelf
Follow each rate to the bookshelf. A ninety thousand word book takes three hours at five hundred words a minute, an hour and a half at a thousand, and three point six minutes at twenty five thousand.

@chapter At these settings
@math heading="350 words a minute" items="350 × 6 × 255 / 60,000 = 8.925@two hundred and twenty five;;350 × 6 × 220 / 60,000 = 7.7@one ninety"
These are verdicts at stated settings, and the settings move. Skilled readers who reach three fifty to four hundred do it with shorter fixations, and by skipping short words. At three fifty with the default two hundred and twenty five, a fixation must advance eight point nine two five characters, just past identification. With fixations of one ninety, it's seven point seven, inside.

@art skip-words
Skipping isn't one of the model's settings. But each skipped word adds its characters to a step without a stop, which has the same effect as a larger step.

@art stop-timer
Settings do little for a thousand, though. Bringing it inside the identification span needs fixation and saccade together under eighty milliseconds. That means fixations under fifty, less than a quarter of the average, on every word.

@chapter The test is comprehension
@art carver-ceiling
The tradition's answer is that trained readers stop reading word by word, and take in meaning by the line or the page. Trainees often report it feels that way. The test is comprehension. When Carver, in nineteen eighty five, tested some of the fastest readers he could find, speed reading graduates among them, none showed comprehension above about six hundred words a minute.

@words text="At 1,000: skimming"
What happens at a thousand words a minute is real, and it has a name: skimming. A later course teaches it as that, with its own questions and its own costs. The mistake is calling it reading with full comprehension.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="1,000 with full comprehension is technique@The first|A verdict without its settings@The second|Testing a claim by speed alone@And the third"
The first slip is believing a thousand words a minute with full comprehension is a matter of technique. The second is stating a verdict without its settings. And the third is testing a claim by speed alone. Any reader can move their eyes across a page at a thousand. The claim worth testing is comprehension at that rate.
