@chapter The question
@title text="Looking Back" sub="Reading Faster · How the Eyes Read" art=thumb
Your eyes slip back. The line gets read twice, and it feels like time lost. Many speed reading courses teach readers to suppress it. But what were those backward glances doing? Are they a habit to cut, or a repair you need?

@art regressions-normal
Start with how common they are. About ten to fifteen percent of saccades in skilled readers go backwards. They're part of normal reading, not a fault peculiar to slow readers.

@chapter The drill
@art mask-lines
So the drill asks what happens when looking back finds nothing. Two readings at the same pacer rate. Reading A, the passage stays on the page. Reading B, each line above the highlight is hidden as it goes. You answer six questions after each.

@words text="Same rate → compare comprehension"
Because both readings run at the pacer's rate, their rates come out the same by design. So the difference lies in the questions, read through the effective rate. Both readings are logged as conditions, not tests.

@chapter The evidence
@art x-mask
The evidence comes from a study that did this far more precisely. Schotter, Tran and Rayner, in twenty fourteen, tracked readers' eyes and replaced each word with x's the moment the eyes left it. A reader could still look back, but would find only x's.

@list heading="Schotter, Tran and Rayner (2014)" items="Comprehension fell with the mask@Comprehension fell|For ordinary sentences, not just ambiguous ones@ordinary sentences|Normal regressions came when needed@when they were needed"
Comprehension fell, for ordinary sentences as well as sentences built to be ambiguous. And in normal reading, sentences where readers had regressed were understood about as well as ones where they hadn't. The regressions came when they were needed.

@chapter A coarser mask
@art pacer-schedule
The kit's mask is a coarser version. It hides whole lines, not words, and only once the highlight moves on, so you can still look back within your line. It hides less, but on the pacer's schedule, not your eyes'. Fall behind the highlight, and you can lose the end of a line before finishing it.

@chapter What one question is worth
@math heading="Masked-costs preset, both at 300 wpm" items="A: 300 × 6/6 = 300@six right;;B: 300 × 4/6 = 200@four right;;B − A = −100@minus a hundred;;one question = 300 / 6 = 50@fifty"
Here's a reader at three hundred words a minute both times. With the text left on the page, six right, an effective rate of three hundred. With the mask, four right: two hundred. The difference is minus a hundred. And at this rate, one question is three hundred over six, fifty words a minute. So it's two questions' worth.

@words text="5 of 6 both times: 250 and 250" sub="no-cost preset"
The no cost preset is a reader who got five of six on both: two fifty and two fifty, a difference of nothing at all. And remember: a difference of one question is not a result.

@chapter Reading your own
@art two-passages-order
If yours looks like no cost, you haven't refuted the study, and the study hasn't refuted you. Your readings were of different passages, one came first, and each is one reading. The study had many readers, many sentences, and a tracker that knew where every word was.

@art repair
What the study doesn't support is the idea that regressions are wasted time to be trained away. When a regression repairs a misreading, cutting it costs you understanding. Some may be needless, though. The next lesson separates the ones that repair understanding from the ones that only follow a wandering mind.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Calling regressions a bad habit@The first|Reading a one-question difference as a result@The second|Comparing rates instead of scores@And the third"
The first slip is calling regressions a bad habit to eliminate. The second is reading a one question difference as a result. And the third is comparing rates instead of scores. Both readings ran at the pacer's rate, so their rates agree by design.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Both at 270 wpm · 5 of 6, then 4 of 6" sub="the two effective rates?"
Your turn. Both readings ran at two seventy words a minute. With the pacer, five of six. With the mask, four of six. What are the two effective rates, and is the gap a result? You should get two twenty five and one eighty. The forty five is one question, so it isn't.
