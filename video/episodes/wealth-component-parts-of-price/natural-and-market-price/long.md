@chapter The question
@title text="Natural and Market Price" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 7" art=thumb
A yard of cloth sells for one price today and another next month. Is there a price underneath that it keeps coming back to? Smith says there is, and he calls it the natural price.

@words text="Natural price" sub="rent + wages + profit, each at its natural rate"
When a price is just enough to pay the rent, the wages and the profits of bringing a thing to market, each at its ordinary rate, that's its natural price. Read natural as ordinary. Smith calls the ordinary rates of a time and place the natural rates. Nothing about justice is claimed.

@art gravitation-line
The natural price is the one a dealer can go on selling at. Below it, he's losing, because his stock could earn the ordinary profit somewhere else. So it's the lowest price he's likely to accept for any length of time.

@chapter Effectual demand
@art coach-and-six
The price actually paid is the market price. It depends on the quantity brought to market, against the effectual demand: the demand of people willing to pay the natural price. A very poor man may like a coach and six, but his demand isn't effectual. It can never bring the coach to market.

@chapter Short and glut
@art short-supply
If less is brought than the effectual demand, some buyers would rather pay more than go without. Competition begins among them, and the price rises. That's why necessaries are so dear in a blockade or a famine.

@art glut-oranges
If more is brought, some must be sold to people who'll pay less, and that low price drags down the whole. How far depends on the sellers' hurry. A glut of oranges is worse than the same weight of old iron, because oranges perish.

@chapter Putting numbers on it
@math heading="Natural price 10s · effectual demand 1,000 yards" items="buyers take:  1300 at 8s · 1000 at 10s · 900 at 12s · 800 at 14s@buyers would take;;short, 800 brought:  14s, 4s over@eight hundred yards;;4 · 800 = 3200s more@three thousand two hundred;;glut, 1300 brought:  8s, 2s under · 2600s less@thirteen hundred"
Smith gives no table, so the lesson writes a schedule. At eight shillings buyers would take thirteen hundred yards, at ten a thousand, at twelve nine hundred, and at fourteen eight hundred. Bring only eight hundred yards, and the price is fourteen shillings, four over natural. That's three thousand two hundred shillings more. Bring thirteen hundred, and it's eight shillings, two thousand six hundred less.

@words text="1,000 brought → 10s"
Bring exactly a thousand, and the price is ten. The competition of the dealers obliges them all to accept this price, but doesn't oblige them to accept less.

@chapter Gravitation
@quote text="The natural price, therefore, is, as it were, the central price, to which the prices of all commodities are continually gravitating." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 7"
Why does it come back? If too much is brought, some part of the price is paid below its natural rate. Landlords withdraw land, labourers labour, employers stock, and the quantity falls. A shortfall works the other way. The natural price, therefore, is, as it were, the central price, to which the prices of all commodities are continually gravitating.

@art black-cloth
His example is a public mourning. It raises the price of black cloth, and the profits of merchants holding it, but not weavers' wages, because the market is short of cloth, not labour. Yet it does raise the wages of journeymen tailors. There, the market is short of labour.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking natural to mean fair@The first|Taking every want for demand@The second|Expecting the market price to equal the natural@The third"
The first slip is taking natural to mean fair. A natural price can rest on low wages if those are the going wages. The second is taking every want for demand. The third is expecting the market price to be the natural price. It's the centre prices keep returning to, not the price on any given day.

@art scissors-blades
Later writers reworked the idea. Alfred Marshall, in 1890, compared demand and supply to the two blades of a pair of scissors. And critics note Smith says nothing about how long the return takes, and assumes land, labour and stock can move freely.
