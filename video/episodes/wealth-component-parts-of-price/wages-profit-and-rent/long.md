@chapter The question
@title text="Wages, Profit and Rent" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 6" art=thumb
What is a price made of? Smith's answer comes in three stages, each one adding a part. He starts with hunters, before anyone owns stock or land.

@quote text="If among a nation of hunters, for example, it usually costs twice the labour to kill a beaver which it does to kill a deer, one beaver should naturally exchange for or be worth two deer." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 6"
If among a nation of hunters, for example, it usually costs twice the labour to kill a beaver which it does to kill a deer, one beaver should naturally exchange for or be worth two deer.

@art beaver-and-deer
In this early and rude state, the whole produce belongs to the labourer, and labour alone sets the rate of exchange. Smith allows for harder work, and for rare skill, which may be no more than fair pay for the time spent learning it.

@chapter Stock, and profit
@art stock-advance
The second stage begins when stock gathers in particular hands. An owner supplies workmen with materials and their keep, and sells what they make. Now the value the workmen add splits in two: one part pays their wages, the other the employer's profit on the whole stock he advanced, materials and wages together.

@words text="Profit of stock" sub="in proportion to the stock advanced"
So profit is a revenue of the stock's owner, in proportion to how much he put up. But is it just the wages of a particular sort of labour, inspection and direction? Smith raises that objection himself, and answers it with two manufactories.

@chapter Two manufactories
@math heading="Profit at 10%" items="wages, each:  20 · £15 = £300@twenty workmen;;capitals:  £300 + £700 = £1000 · £300 + £7000 = £7300@seven hundred;;profits:  £100 · £730@a hundred pounds"
Ordinary profit is ten per cent. Each manufactory has twenty workmen at fifteen pounds a year, three hundred pounds in wages. One spends seven hundred a year on materials, the other seven thousand. So the capitals are a thousand pounds and seven thousand three hundred. The first owner expects about a hundred pounds, the second about seven hundred and thirty.

@art two-manufactories
Yet the labour of overseeing may be exactly the same. A clerk who does the overseeing is paid wages with no regular link to the capital. The owner, freed of almost all labour, still expects profit in proportion to his capital. So profit isn't the wages of management.

@chapter Land, and rent
@quote text="the landlords, like all other men, love to reap where they never sowed, and demand a rent even for its natural produce." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 6"
The third stage is when all the land becomes private property. Then the landlords, like all other men, love to reap where they never sowed, and demand a rent even for its natural produce. Rent becomes the third component of most prices.

@art three-parts
And every part, Smith says, is measured by the labour it can buy. Then the rule: in every society, the price of every commodity finally resolves into one or other, or all, of three parts. Wages, profit and rent.

@art plough-horse
Corn pays rent, wages and the farmer's profit. Isn't there a fourth part, to replace the worn out plough horse? Smith says no. The horse's own price is rent, labour and profit, so it all resolves, sooner or later, into the same three.

@chapter A batch of bread
@math heading="Profit 10% at each stage" items="farmer:  (40 + 30) · 1.1 = 77@the farmer;;miller:  (77 + 5) · 1.1 = 90.2@the miller;;baker:  (90.2 + 10) · 1.1 = 110.22@the baker;;wages 55 + rent 30 + profit 25.22 = 110.22@add up"
Follow a batch of bread, in the lesson's figures. The farmer pays forty in wages and thirty in rent, and adds ten per cent: seventy seven. The miller adds five of wages, and asks ninety point two. The baker adds ten, and asks a hundred and ten point two two. Add up: fifty five wages, thirty rent, twenty five point two two profit. The whole price, nothing over.

@art bread-chain
Notice the baker's profit, ten point oh two, beats the farmer's seven, though he spent less on labour. Each later profit is greater, Smith says, because the capital behind it is always greater.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking profit for the owner's wages of management@The first|Adding a fourth part to replace the stock@The second|Reading the rule as a theory of how large each part is@The third"
The first slip is taking profit for the owner's wages of management. The second is adding a fourth part to replace the stock, though that's exactly the step Marx later disputed. The third is reading the rule as a theory of how large each part is. The chapter says what a price is made of, not what fixes the rates.
