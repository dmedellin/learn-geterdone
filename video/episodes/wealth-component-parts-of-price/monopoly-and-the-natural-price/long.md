@chapter The question
@title text="Monopoly and the Natural Price" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 7" art=thumb
Prices gravitate to the natural price. So what can hold a price above it for years, or even centuries? The end of chapter seven gives three kinds of case: a secret, a natural scarcity, and a monopoly.

@chapter A secret
@art sealed-secret
First, the secret. When demand rises and the price climbs, the sellers in that market keep quiet about it. If rivals knew, they'd crowd in and the price would fall. If the market is far away, it may be kept for years. But Smith admits secrets of this kind can seldom be long kept.

@art dye-vat
Secrets in manufacturing last longer. A dyer who finds how to make a colour with materials costing half as much may keep the advantage all his life. Smith says his gains are really high wages for his private labour. But because they're in proportion to his stock, they're commonly counted as extraordinary profits.

@chapter A scarce soil
@art happy-vineyard
Second, the natural scarcity. Some things need a soil and situation so singular that all the land fit for them can't supply the effectual demand. Wine from French vineyards of a peculiarly happy soil is his example. Such goods may sell high for whole centuries, and the part paid above its natural rate is the rent.

@chapter Monopoly
@art locked-gate
Third, monopoly itself. Monopolists keep the market constantly understocked, never fully supplying the effectual demand, and sell much above the natural price. Corporations and the statutes of apprenticeship, Smith says, are a sort of enlarged monopolies, and may keep wages and profits above their natural rate for ages together.

@quote text="The price of monopoly is upon every occasion the highest which can be got." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter 7"
Then the sentence the chapter builds to. The price of monopoly is upon every occasion the highest which can be got. The natural price is the lowest which can be taken, not every time, but for any considerable time together.

@chapter Withholding, in numbers
@math heading="Natural price 10s a yard" items="1000 yards:  (10 − 10) · 1000 = 0@a thousand yards;;800 yards:  (14 − 10) · 800 = 3200s@eight hundred;;500 yards:  (20 − 10) · 500 = 5000s@five hundred;;400 yards:  (22 − 10) · 400 = 4800s@four hundred"
The lesson writes a schedule of buyers. At the natural ten shillings, they take a thousand yards. Bring a thousand yards, and nothing is over. Bring eight hundred, and the price is fourteen, three thousand two hundred shillings over. Five hundred, twenty shillings a yard, five thousand over. Four hundred, twenty two shillings, but only four thousand eight hundred over.

@art withhold-bars
So the highest price isn't the largest gain. On this schedule, the excess peaks at five hundred yards. That treats the natural price as the cost of every yard, which is the lab's simplification, not Smith's. Augustin Cournot worked this out as a choice of quantity in 1838. Smith states only the plainest form.

@chapter Above, but seldom below
@art above-and-below
The other side is different. A market price can stay above its natural price for a long time, but seldom stays long below it. Whoever is paid below the natural rate withdraws land, labour or stock at once. Only a policy as violent as caste, Smith says, could hold wages or profit below it for generations.

@words text="Monopoly, or reward?"
And there's a question the chapter leaves open, which critics keep asking. When is a price above the natural price a monopoly, and when is it a reward? Joseph Schumpeter later argued that the prize of a temporary advantage drives invention. Smith marks the question out, but doesn't draw the line.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the highest price as the best choice@The first|Calling every high price a monopoly@The second|Expecting the same force below@The third"
The first slip is reading the highest which can be got as the monopolist's best choice. It's the highest for the quantity brought. The second is calling every high price a monopoly. The dyer has no privilege, and the vineyard no design. The third is expecting the same force below the natural price.

@words text="Natural 10s · 800, 500 or 400 yards" sub="14s · 20s · 22s"
Your turn. For each quantity, subtract the natural price from the price, and multiply by the yards brought. Which quantity gives the largest excess, and is it the one with the highest price?
