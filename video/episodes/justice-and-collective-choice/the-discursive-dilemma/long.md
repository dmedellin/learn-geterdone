@chapter The question
@title text="The Discursive Dilemma" sub="Justice and Collective Choice" art=thumb
A group is often asked for more than a choice. A court gives a verdict, and reasons for it. When the reasons are decided by vote too, do the reasons and the verdict agree? Not always.

@art three-judges
A court of three judges decides whether a defendant is liable. Liability requires two things. p, that there was a valid contract, and q, that the defendant breached it. The conclusion is p and q, that both hold. Each judge is consistent. They accept the conclusion exactly when they accept both premises.

@chapter The court
@list heading="Three judges" items="Judge 1:  p yes,  q yes,  liable yes@Judge one|Judge 2:  p yes,  q no,  liable no@Judge two|Judge 3:  p no,  q yes,  liable no@And judge three"
Judge one accepts both premises, so finds the defendant liable. Judge two accepts p, rejects q, and so says not liable. And judge three rejects p, accepts q, and also says not liable.

@art column-count
Now take a majority in every column, separately.

@math heading="Majority in each column" items="p:  2 to 1,  accepted@p is accepted;;q:  2 to 1,  accepted@q is accepted;;p ∧ q:  1 to 2,  rejected@The conclusion"
p is accepted by judges one and two, two to one. q is accepted by judges one and three, two to one. The conclusion is accepted only by judge one, so it fails, one to two.

@art broken-triangle
So the court's majority says p is true, q is true, and p and q is false. Those three can't all be true together. That's the discursive dilemma. The group's majority judgments are inconsistent, though every member's are consistent.

@chapter Two procedures
@art gavel-split
The court has to rule. The premise based procedure takes the majority on p and on q, and derives that the defendant is liable. The conclusion based procedure counts the votes on liability itself, and finds not liable.

@art two-paths
Each has a cost. The premise based verdict holds the defendant liable, though two of the three judges wouldn't. The conclusion based verdict leaves the court saying a contract existed, it was breached, and no liability follows. Choosing is a choice about whether a group's verdict should follow its reasons, or its votes.

@chapter The Condorcet paradox again
@words text="Truth values in place of rankings" sub="no judge at fault, no tie-break repairs it"
Three voters, three propositions, each voter consistent, and a majority that doesn't cohere. It's the shape of the Condorcet cycle, with truth values in place of rankings. As there, no judge is at fault, and no tie break repairs it.

@words text="Kornhauser and Sager · Pettit · List and Pettit" sub="the doctrinal paradox, the discursive dilemma, an impossibility theorem"
The court is Kornhauser and Sager's, who called it the doctrinal paradox. Pettit gave it this name, when he showed any group voting on connected propositions can meet it. List and Pettit then proved that, for an agenda like this, no way of aggregating judgments meets all of a short list of reasonable conditions. The premise based procedure gives up treating every proposition by the same rule.

@chapter The other direction
@math heading="Conclusion: p ∨ q" items="judges:  p only,  q only,  neither@p only;;p:  1 of 3,  q:  1 of 3@one judge in three;;premise-based:  reject@rejects both;;conclusion-based:  2 of 3,  accept@two judges"
The disagreement can run the other way. Make the conclusion p or q, and take judges who accept p only, q only, and neither. Each premise is accepted by one judge in three, so the premise based procedure rejects both, and rejects the disjunction. But two judges accept the disjunction, so the conclusion based procedure accepts it.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="If a majority accepts each premise, it accepts the conclusion@The first|Blaming a judge@The second|Treating premise-based as the correct one@And the third"
The first slip is thinking that if a majority accepts each premise, it accepts the conclusion. Majorities are taken column by column. The second is blaming a judge. Each is consistent, and the fault is in the combining. And the third is treating the premise based procedure as the correct one. Which to prefer depends on whether the group is answerable for its reasons, or for its result.

@chapter Try it
@art change-one-row
Try the lesson's check. In the court, change any one judge's row, and recount every column. You'll find the contradiction needs exactly one judge who accepts only p, one who accepts only q, and one who accepts both. Change any of the three, and the procedures agree.
