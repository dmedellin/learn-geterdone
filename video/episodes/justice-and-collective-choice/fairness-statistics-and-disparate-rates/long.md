@chapter The puzzle
@title text="Fairness, Statistics and Disparate Rates" sub="Justice and Collective Choice" art=thumb
Can a university admit men and women at exactly the same rate in every department, and still admit men at nearly twice the rate overall? Yes. And when that happens, two ideas of fairness give different answers.

@art two-departments
Picture an open department and a selective one. The open department admits three applicants in five, and the selective one, one in five. Men and women are admitted at exactly those rates in both. The only difference is where they applied.

@art mixed-queues
Of every hundred men, eighty applied to the open department, and twenty to the selective one. Of every hundred women, it's the reverse. Twenty open, and eighty selective.

@chapter The counts
@math heading="Admitted of applied" items="open:  men 48/80,  women 12/20,  both 3/5@In the open;;selective:  men 4/20,  women 16/80,  both 1/5@In the selective;;pooled:  men 52/100,  women 28/100@Pooled"
In the open department, forty eight of eighty men and twelve of twenty women are admitted. Both three fifths. In the selective one, four of twenty men and sixteen of eighty women. Both one fifth. Pooled, the men's rate is fifty two in a hundred, and the women's is twenty eight in a hundred.

@art pooled-gap
A glance at the pooled rates suggests men are admitted at nearly twice the rate. A glance at the departments shows no group treated differently anywhere. This is the pattern Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell found in graduate admissions at Berkeley in nineteen seventy three. The counts here are just small enough to follow by hand.

@chapter Two questions
@list heading="Two comparisons" items="Within a department: equal treatment@within department|Pooled: equal outcomes@pooled comparison"
Both statements are true of the same counts. They answer different questions. The within department comparison asks whether, for the choice already made, the outcome depends on your group. The pooled comparison asks whether the two groups end up in the same overall position.

@chapter Standardising
@math heading="Standardised rate" items="each department:  100 of 200 applicants@Half of all;;(1/2)·(3/5) + (1/2)·(1/5) = 2/5@one half times;;both groups:  2/5@The two groups"
A standardised rate weights each department's rate by the same shares for both groups, so the mix can't move the comparison. Half of all applicants applied to each department. So the rate is one half times three fifths, plus one half times one fifth, which is two fifths. The two groups are equal at two fifths. The gap came from the mix.

@chapter Where they come apart
@art balance-two-fairness
Equal treatment holds here, and equal outcomes fails. Each is a respectable notion of fairness. A rule that equalised the pooled rates would have to admit men and women to the selective department at different rates. That breaks equal treatment.

@art upstream-barrier
What the lab can't decide is whether the mix is itself the unfairness. If women apply to the selective department because the open one is closed to them in practice, the equal rates hide a disadvantage upstream. If they apply from preference, they don't. The same counts fit both stories.

@chapter A reversal
@math heading="Women ahead in each department" items="open:  women 3/4,  men 7/10@three quarters;;selective:  women 1/8,  men 1/10@one eighth;;pooled:  men 58/100,  women 25/100@Pooled"
Now make women's rates higher in each department. Three quarters against seven tenths in the open one, and one eighth against one tenth in the selective one, with the same mix. Pooled, men get fifty eight in a hundred, and women twenty five. Women do better in every department, and worse overall. The standardised rates put women ahead.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading a pooled gap as a gap in treatment@The first|Thinking equal department rates settle it@The second|Expecting one policy to satisfy both notions@And the third"
The first slip is reading a gap in the pooled rate as a gap in treatment. It's a reason to ask why, not proof. The second is thinking equal rates within departments settle the matter. They don't certify their own inputs. And the third is expecting one policy to satisfy both notions. When the mixes differ, the choice is a choice of values.

@chapter Try it
@art shift-applications
Try the lesson's suggestion. Move ten women's applications from the selective department to the open one, keeping the rates, so their cells read eighteen of thirty and fourteen of seventy. Work out the women's pooled rate. You should get thirty two in a hundred, against the men's fifty two, while every department rate stays put.
