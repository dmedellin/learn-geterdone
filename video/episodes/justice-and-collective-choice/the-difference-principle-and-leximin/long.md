@chapter The principle
@title text="The Difference Principle and Leximin" sub="Justice and Collective Choice" art=thumb
Is an unequal society ever more just than an equal one? Rawls's answer is yes, on one condition. And that condition is precise enough to turn into a rule you can run.

@words text="To the greatest benefit of the least advantaged" sub="Rawls's second principle, second part"
The second part of Rawls's second principle, the difference principle, says inequalities are to be arranged so that they're to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. As a rule for comparing distributions, prefer the one where the worst off person is better off. If they tie, the next person up decides, and so on.

@chapter Leximin
@art sorted-bars
That rule is called leximin. Sort each distribution from lowest to highest, then compare them entry by entry from the bottom. The first position where they differ decides, and the distribution with the larger entry there ranks higher.

@math heading="Raising the floor" items="A = 5, 5, 5@five, five, five;;B = 6, 9, 20@six, nine, twenty;;first entries:  5 < 6@The first entries;;B ranks above A@ranked higher"
Take five, five, five against six, nine, twenty. They're already sorted. The first entries are five and six, and six is larger, so B is ranked higher. The nine and the twenty are never looked at. The verdict would stand if the top were twenty thousand, or seven.

@chapter Not equality
@art level-vs-raised
That's where the principle parts company with equality. A pure egalitarian prefers five, five, five, because it's level. The difference principle prefers the second, because everyone is no worse off in it, and the worst off are better. Preferring the first would mean denying the worst off a unit of welfare, so that others wouldn't have more.

@chapter When the floors tie
@art tie-then-next
Sometimes the worst off do equally well in both. Then you don't stop.

@math heading="A tie at the bottom" items="A = 5, 8, 8:  total 21@five, eight, eight;;B = 5, 6, 20:  total 31@five, six, twenty;;first: 5 = 5,  second: 8 > 6@The first entries;;A ranks above B@so A wins"
When the bottoms tie, leximin looks upward. Compare five, eight, eight with five, six, twenty. The first entries are both five. The second entries are eight and six, so A wins, even though B's total is far larger, thirty one against twenty one. Maximin alone would call it a tie. Leximin finishes the job.

@chapter The incentive argument
@art incentive-ladder
Why choose the unequal distribution at all? The standard answer is incentives. If talented people are paid more, they'll do work that produces more for everyone, and some of it reaches the worst off. In the numbers, the step from five to six at the bottom is bought by the nine and the twenty above.

@words text="A premise the lab can't check" sub="did the high rewards raise the floor?"
That's an argument, with a premise the lab can't check. If the high rewards don't in fact raise the floor, the unequal distribution has nothing to recommend it. The lab ranks the vectors you type. Whether one caused the other is what the philosophy is about.

@math heading="When equality wins" items="5, 8, 20  vs  5, 5, 5:  unequal wins@floors tie;;4, 8, 20  vs  5, 5, 5:  equal wins@the floor is lower"
Try two more. Five, eight, twenty against five, five, five. The floors tie at five, and the next entries are eight and five, so the unequal one wins. But make it four, eight, twenty, and the floor is lower. Now leximin ranks the level one higher, however large the top entry.

@chapter Three limits
@art primary-goods
Three limits. Rawls applied the principle to primary goods, like income, rights and opportunities, not to one scale of welfare. He took the worst off to be a representative group, not a named person. And he described looking upward at ties as a refinement his argument didn't need.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking the principle demands equality@The first|Stopping at a tie in the first entry@The second|Reading the ranking as a defence of inequality@And the third"
The first slip is thinking the difference principle demands equality. It permits any inequality that raises the floor. The second is stopping at a tie in the first entry. Equal floors don't make equal distributions. And the third is reading the ranking as a defence of inequality. That the unequal one has the higher floor is a premise somebody has to establish.

@chapter Try it
@art zero-floor
Try the lesson's extreme case. Give a distribution a lowest entry of zero, and make the others enormous. Compare it with any distribution whose lowest entry is positive. Leximin ranks it lower every time. Then ask whether a priority that strict is too strong.
