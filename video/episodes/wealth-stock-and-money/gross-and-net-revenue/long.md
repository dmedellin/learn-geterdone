@chapter The question
@title text="Gross and Net Revenue" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 2" art=thumb
Is a country's money part of its wealth? It seems obvious that it is. Smith says money is a valuable part of a country's capital, but no part of its revenue. To see why, start with a private estate.

@art estate-gross-net
The gross rent of an estate is whatever the farmer pays. The neat rent is what's left free to the landlord after management, repairs and other necessary charges. And his real wealth is in proportion, not to his gross, but to his neat rent.

@chapter A country's net revenue
@words text="Gross: the whole annual produce" sub="Net: what's left after keeping up the capital"
Smith does the same for a country. Its gross revenue is the whole annual produce of its land and labour. Its net revenue is what's left after maintaining its fixed and circulating capital. The test is what people can spend on living without encroaching on their capital.

@art machine-upkeep
The first deduction is the whole upkeep of fixed capital. Not because machines are a waste. Spending properly on them is always repaid with great profit. But their upkeep uses materials and workmen who could have made food, clothes and lodging. It's like repairs on an estate.

@math heading="One great manufactory" items="before:  10000 − 1000 = 9000@a thousand;;after:  10000 − 500 = 9500@five hundred"
Smith's undertaker spends a thousand a year keeping up his machinery. If he can cut that to five hundred, he'll spend the other five hundred on more materials and more workmen. Give the works ten thousand pounds of produce, the lesson's figure, and the net goes from nine thousand to nine thousand five hundred.

@chapter Only money comes off
@words text="Money is the only part of the circulating capital whose upkeep comes off"
The second deduction is subtler. A merchant's goods aren't his own consumption, but they go into other people's, who pay for them from other revenue. So provisions, materials and finished work stay in the society's net revenue. Money is the only part of the circulating capital whose maintenance can reduce the net.

@quote text="The great wheel of circulation is altogether different from the goods which are circulated by means of it." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 2"
Machines and money are alike. Both cost something to make and keep up, and neither is part of the revenue it helps produce or distribute. The great wheel of circulation is altogether different from the goods which are circulated by means of it.

@art great-wheel
The revenue of the society consists altogether in those goods, and not in the wheel which circulates them.

@math heading="A country, in the lesson's figures" items="gross:  £10,000,000@ten million;;fixed upkeep:  −£2,000,000@two million;;money upkeep:  −£500,000@five hundred thousand;;net:  £7,500,000, or 3/4@seven and a half"
Take a country with a gross of ten million pounds. Its fixed capital costs two million a year to keep up, and its money five hundred thousand. All the lesson's figures. The net revenue is seven and a half million, three quarters of the gross.

@art net-bar
Notice what has no entry: the provisions, materials and finished work. They stay in the net. Only the two upkeeps come off.

@chapter The guinea and its worth
@art one-guinea
But incomes are paid in money, aren't they? Smith says the word covers two things. The circulating money of England was computed at eighteen millions, meaning the metal pieces. But a man worth a hundred a year means the goods his money buys. His revenue is the guinea's worth, rather than the guinea.

@words text="One guinea: today's pension, tomorrow's, the next day's"
And the same guinea that pays one man's weekly pension today may pay another's tomorrow, and a third's the day after. So the coins in a country are worth much less than all the payments made with them. Smith says it's only the ambiguity of language that makes this look paradoxical.

@chapter A cheaper wheel
@art paper-for-gold
Then the step to the next lesson. Any saving in the upkeep of money that doesn't reduce productive power raises the net. Replace gold with something cheaper that circulates as well. Cut the money's upkeep from five hundred thousand to a hundred thousand, and the net rises to seven million nine hundred thousand. Smith's cheaper thing is paper money.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Counting money as part of revenue@The first|Subtracting all circulating capital@The second|Thinking machines should be cut@The third"
The first slip is counting money as part of revenue because incomes are paid in it. The second is subtracting all the circulating capital, as if it were all like money. The third is thinking machines should be cut because their upkeep is deducted. The saving Smith praises keeps the productive power.

@words text="Gross £10m · fixed £2m · money £0.5m → £0.1m" sub="net before and after?"
Your turn. Subtract both upkeeps from the gross, then do it again with the cheaper money. You should find a gain of four hundred thousand.
