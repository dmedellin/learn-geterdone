@chapter The question
@title text="Drawing and Redrawing" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 2" art=thumb
Smith says the practice is so well known to men of business that it may hardly seem worth explaining. But its effects on banking aren't well understood, even by men of business themselves. So he explains it, as distinctly as he can.

@art leaning-house
It began with traders who had over traded, after the banks wouldn't lend them as much as they wished. They turned to a well known shift of traders on the brink of bankruptcy. Smith's image is a weary traveller who thinks the house he's reached won't stand very long, but it's a chance if it falls tonight.

@chapter A and B
@art a-and-b-bills
A in Edinburgh draws a bill on B in London, payable in two months. In reality B owes A nothing. B accepts, on condition that before it falls due he can redraw on A for the same sum, plus interest and a commission, another two month bill. Then A does it again, and B again. Sometimes for years.

@words text="Each bill discounted at a bank"
And A discounts the bills he draws with a bank in Edinburgh, and B the ones he redraws in London. So in every round, the money A raises is advanced by a bank.

@chapter The cost
@math heading="Two-month bills on £10,000" items="draws a year:  12 / 2 = 6@six times;;interest:  5%@five per cent;;commission:  6 · ½ = 3%@a half per cent;;yearly cost:  5 + 3 = 8%, £800@eight per cent"
What did it cost? Two month bills are drawn six times a year. The interest was five per cent a year. The commission was never less than a half per cent on each draught, so six halves, three per cent. That's eight per cent, eight hundred pounds a year on ten thousand.

@art commission-stack
The commission is the part that makes it dear: three of the eight. And it cost something more than eight, Smith says, when the commission rose, or interest was charged on earlier interest and commission.

@words text="Ordinary profit: 6% to 10%"
Where ordinary profits run between six and ten per cent, it would take a very fortunate speculation to repay that and still leave a surplus. The projectors, Smith says, had in their golden dreams the most distinct vision of great profit, and on waking very seldom found it.

@chapter Fictitious payment
@art water-pond
Compare a real bill, drawn by a real creditor on a real debtor, and really paid when due. Then a bank's coffers are like a water pond. A stream runs out, but another runs in, fully equal to it.

@art drained-coffer
With drawing and redrawing, each bill is paid by the next. The payment was altogether fictitious. The stream that ran out of the banks was never replaced by one running in. The projectors drew a capital out of the banks, for a while perhaps without the banks having the most distant suspicion they'd advanced it.

@chapter The new bank
@math heading="A new bank, in Smith's round figures" items="notes out:  £200,000 at 5% → a clear gain@two hundred thousand;;bills on London:  £600,000 at 8% = £48,000@six hundred thousand;;loss:  8 − 5 = 3%, £18,000@three per cent"
Then a new bank was set up in Scotland to relieve the distress. Smith doesn't name it; historians identify it as the Ayr bank, open from seventeen sixty nine to seventeen seventy two. On its two hundred thousand in notes, five per cent may have been clear gain. But on upwards of six hundred thousand drawn on London, it paid upwards of eight. A loss of three per cent, eighteen thousand pounds.

@words text="It increased the real distress of the country"
So it was losing more than three per cent on more than three quarters of all its dealings. It let the projectors get only deeper into debt, and in the long run increased the real distress of the country it meant to relieve.

@chapter What a bank may lend
@art slow-returns
So what can a bank properly lend? Not a trader's whole capital, but only the part he'd otherwise keep idle in ready money. Fixed capital returns too slowly. That should be borrowed on bond or mortgage, from private people who mean to live on the interest.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Counting only the interest@The first|Thinking each bill was paid, so no harm@The second|Thinking banks should lend only to the rich@The third"
The first slip is counting only the interest and forgetting the commission, three eighths of the cost. The second is thinking the bills were harmless because each was paid on time. Each was paid by the next. The third is thinking Smith says banks should lend only to rich men. His rule is about what's lent, not to whom.
