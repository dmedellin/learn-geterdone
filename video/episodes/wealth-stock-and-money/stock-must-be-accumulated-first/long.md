@chapter The question
@title text="Stock Must Be Accumulated First" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Introduction" art=thumb
Book One showed what dividing labour can do. Book Two opens by asking what it needs. And Smith's answer is: a stock, stored up beforehand.

@art hunter-in-forest
Start with a rude society, with no division of labour, where every man provides everything for himself. There, nothing needs storing. When he's hungry, he goes to the forest to hunt. When his coat wears out, he clothes himself with the skin of the first large animal he kills. His labour and his supper are the same event.

@chapter The weaver waits
@art weaver-loom
Now divide the labour. A man makes one thing and lives by what others make. But he can only buy theirs with his own, and his own isn't worth anything until it's made and sold. So it's a problem of waiting.

@quote text="A weaver cannot apply himself entirely to his peculiar business, unless there is before-hand stored up somewhere, either in his own possession, or in that of some other person, a stock sufficient to maintain him" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Introduction"
A weaver cannot apply himself entirely to his peculiar business, unless there is before-hand stored up somewhere, either in his own possession, or in that of some other person, a stock sufficient to maintain him, and to supply his materials and tools, till he has not only finished but sold his web.

@words text="Stock: goods laid up beforehand" sub="not money"
So stock, in Smith's word, is goods laid up: food, clothes, materials, tools. Not money. And notice, it needn't be the weaver's own. A master or a lender can hold it. What matters is that someone holds it first.

@chapter Three parts
@art three-stocks
The lab sorts the weaver's stock into three parts. The yarn is circulating: it leaves him in the web and comes back in its price. The loom stays with him and earns by staying, so it's fixed. And the food that keeps him until the sale earns nothing. It's kept for his own use, with his clothes and furniture.

@math heading="The weaver" items="loom £20 · yarn £30 · food £40 · clothes £10@a loom;;stock:  20 + 30 + 40 + 10 = £100@a hundred;;capital:  20 + 30 = £50, a half@capital"
In the lesson's figures, the weaver holds a loom and tools worth twenty pounds, yarn worth thirty, food for the weeks until the web sells worth forty, and clothes and furniture worth ten. A hundred pounds of stock. The capital he works with, loom and yarn, is fifty, a half.

@chapter Division needs more
@words text="Labour can be more and more subdivided in proportion only as stock is previously more and more accumulated"
Then the proportion. As the accumulation of stock must come before the division of labour, labour can be more and more subdivided only as stock is more and more accumulated. A divided trade works up far more material with the same hands, and uses machines for each simple operation.

@math heading="After division" items="tools £60 · yarn £90 · food £40 · clothes £10@sixty;;stock:  £200@two hundred;;capital:  150 / 200 = 3/4@three quarters"
So the same workmen need an equal stock of provisions, but more materials and tools. Triple the tools to sixty and the yarn to ninety, and keep the food at forty and clothes at ten. The stock is two hundred, and capital has gone from a half to three quarters.

@art stock-bars
The stock doubled and the food didn't move. The whole increase is in tools and materials. That's Smith's equal stock of provisions, and greater stock of materials and tools.

@art long-wait
Now change something else. Let the first weaver wait twice as long for his sale. His food doubles to eighty, and his stock is a hundred and forty. But his capital is still fifty, now only about thirty six per cent. A longer wait asks for more stock without adding any capital.

@chapter Stock invites division
@words text="As great a quantity of work as possible"
And the argument turns round. Whoever employs stock to maintain labour wants to get as great a quantity of work from it as possible. So he divides the work and buys the best machines he can, in proportion to his stock. Stock makes division possible, and having stock makes division worth trying.

@art plan-steps
The Introduction ends on a plan. Chapter one, the parts a stock divides into. Chapter two, money as one branch of it. Three and four, capital employed by its owner and lent. Five, what different employments do to a country's industry.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking the weaver's stock to be money@The first|Thinking the weaver must own it all@The second|Counting everything he owns as capital@The third"
The first slip is taking the weaver's stock to be money. It's goods. The second is thinking the weaver must own it all. The third is counting everything he owns as capital. Only what he expects to give him a revenue is capital. His food, clothes and furniture are stock, but not capital.

@chapter Try it
@words text="Loom £20 · yarn £30 · food £80 · clothes £10" sub="stock, capital, and its share?"
Your turn. Take the long wait. Add up the stock, add up the capital, and divide. You should find a hundred and forty, fifty, and about thirty six per cent.
