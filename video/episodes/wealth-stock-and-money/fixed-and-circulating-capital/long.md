@chapter The question
@title text="Fixed and Circulating Capital" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 1" art=thumb
Is a farmer's seed fixed capital or circulating? It goes into the ground and comes back to the granary every year. Smith's answer is surprising, and it comes from one simple test.

@words text="Capital: the part that affords a revenue"
First, the cut that comes before it. A man with a week's food just eats it. A man with stock enough for months or years tries to get a revenue from most of it, keeping back only enough to live on until that revenue comes in. The part expected to afford a revenue is his capital.

@chapter Staying or going
@art merchant-goods
Now the test that splits capital in two. A merchant's goods earn nothing while they sit. His capital keeps going from him in one shape and coming back in another, and only by circulating does it yield a profit. That's circulating capital.

@art iron-work
The other way is to lay it out on things that yield a revenue without changing masters. That's fixed capital. A merchant's capital is almost all circulating. A tailor's fixed capital is a parcel of needles. An iron work's is a furnace, a forge and a slit mill, at very great expense.

@chapter The farmer
@art farm-sort
The farmer is where the test earns its keep. He profits by one part by keeping it, and by the other by parting with it. His labouring cattle are fixed, like his plough. But their keep is circulating, like his servants' wages. Cattle bought to fatten and sell are circulating throughout.

@art seed-granary
And then the surprise. The whole value of the seed is properly fixed capital. Though it goes backwards and forwards between the ground and the granary, it never changes masters, so it doesn't properly circulate. The test asks about owners and sales, not whether a thing moves.

@math heading="A farmer's stock, in the lesson's sums" items="fixed:  40 + 60 + 60 + 20 = £180@fixed;;circulating:  30 + 60 + 30 + 40 = £160@circulating;;kept for use:  £60@kept;;capital:  340 / 400 = 17/20@three forty"
The lab sorts a farm with Smith's kinds and the lesson's sums. Fixed, a hundred and eighty pounds. Circulating, a hundred and sixty. Kept for his own use, sixty. That's a stock of four hundred, of which capital is three forty, seventeen twentieths. His fixed capital is under half the whole.

@chapter A whole society
@art dwelling-house
A country's stock divides the same way. The first portion, for immediate consumption, includes every dwelling house. A house its owner lives in contributes nothing to his revenue. Let it, and it pays him rent, but the tenant pays from some other revenue, so it yields nothing to the public.

@list heading="Fixed capital, four parts" items="Machines and instruments of trade@machines|Profitable buildings@profitable buildings|Improvements of land@improvements|Acquired and useful abilities@abilities"
Fixed capital has four parts. Machines and instruments of trade. Profitable buildings, like shops, warehouses and farmhouses. Improvements of land, from clearing to draining. And the acquired and useful abilities of all the people. A person's keep while he learns is a capital fixed and realized, as it were, in his person.

@list heading="Circulating capital, four parts" items="Money@money|Provisions@provisions|Materials@materials|Finished work in dealers' hands@finished work"
Circulating capital has four too. The money that circulates everything else. Provisions in the hands of butchers, farmers and brewers. Materials not yet made up. And finished work still in the shops. A parish in the lesson holds six hundred pounds fixed and two fifty circulating, a capital of eight fifty in a stock of twelve fifty.

@chapter Each needs the other
@art machine-and-materials
The two capitals lean on each other. No fixed capital can yield any revenue but by means of a circulating one. The best machine makes nothing without materials and the workmen's keep. And both exist for the first portion, the stock which feeds, clothes and lodges the people.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Sorting by what a thing is made of@The first|Counting an owner's house as capital@The second|Thinking fixed capital means machinery@The third"
The first slip is sorting by what a thing is made of, instead of what it does for its owner. The second is counting an owner's dwelling house as capital. The third is thinking fixed capital means machinery. Improved land and learned skills are fixed capital too. And David Ricardo sorted differently, by how long a capital lasts.
