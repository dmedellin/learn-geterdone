@chapter The question
@title text="Paper Money and the Scottish Banks" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 2" art=thumb
If money is an expensive wheel, can you build a cheaper one? Smith says yes, with bank notes. But only up to a limit, and the limit is the interesting part.

@art banker-coffer
A banker lends his customers his own promissory notes, say a hundred thousand pounds. They pay him the interest they'd pay on coin. Some notes come back, but twenty thousand in gold and silver may often be enough to meet them.

@math heading="One banker" items="notes:  £100,000@a hundred thousand;;reserve:  1/5 · 100,000 = £20,000@twenty thousand;;spared:  100,000 − 20,000 = £80,000@eighty thousand"
So twenty thousand pounds in gold and silver do all the work a hundred thousand could otherwise have done. Eighty thousand of gold and silver can be spared from the country's circulation. And if many banks do the same, the whole circulation can run on a fifth of the gold it needed.

@chapter The channel
@words text="The channel of circulation remains precisely the same"
Now a whole country. Suppose its circulation needs one million in money. Then banks issue a million in notes, keeping two hundred thousand in coin. That's eight hundred thousand of coin and a million of notes poured into a channel that held a million. The channel of circulation will remain precisely the same as before.

@art channel-overflow
So eight hundred thousand must overflow. And the paper can't go abroad, because far from the banks, nobody will take it. So gold and silver, eight hundred thousand pounds' worth, will be sent abroad. Not for nothing: its owners exchange it for foreign goods.

@words text="Wines and silks · or materials, tools and provisions"
What goods matters. If they're foreign wines and silks for idle people, it's hurtful. If they're materials, tools and provisions to employ more workmen, it promotes industry. Smith thinks most will go the second way. But that's an inference from prudence, not a measurement, and he calls it only probable.

@chapter Two merchants
@art two-merchants
Smith compares two merchants of equal stock. The London merchant must keep a sum by him, say five hundred pounds, earning no interest, so his warehouse holds five hundred pounds less goods. The Edinburgh merchant keeps no money idle. He draws on a cash account at the bank, and repays it piecemeal from his sales.

@words text="I do not pretend to know"
He's careful with the evidence. He's heard Glasgow's trade doubled in fifteen years of banking. But he doesn't pretend to know, and says if true, it would be too great an effect for this cause alone.

@chapter The limit
@quote text="The whole paper money of every kind which can easily circulate in any country, never can exceed the value of the gold and silver, of which it supplies the place" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book II, Chapter 2"
Now the limit. The whole paper money of every kind which can easily circulate in any country, never can exceed the value of the gold and silver, of which it supplies the place. Any excess can't go abroad and can't be used at home, so it comes straight back to the banks for coin.

@math heading="A bank of forty thousand" items="notes the country absorbs:  £40,000@forty thousand;;reserve:  1/4 · 40,000 = £10,000@ten thousand;;issue £44,000:  excess 4,000@forty four thousand;;reserve needed:  11,000 + 4,000 = £14,000@fourteen thousand"
Smith's case: a bank whose notes the country can absorb come to exactly forty thousand pounds, and it keeps ten thousand in coin. Let it issue forty four thousand. The four thousand extra come back almost as fast as they're issued. So it must keep not eleven thousand, but fourteen thousand.

@art returning-notes
The excess earns nothing, comes straight back, and the bank bears the whole cost of collecting the coin again. A bank can't issue as much as it likes just because notes cost only paper.

@chapter Wings of paper
@art waggon-way
A highway grows no corn. Banking, Smith says, provides a sort of waggon way through the air, letting a country turn its highways into pastures and cornfields. But a country on paper is suspended upon the Daedalian wings of paper money, less secure than on the solid ground of gold and silver.

@art party-wall
So he wants rules: perhaps no notes under five pounds, and payment on demand without conditions. Is that a violation of natural liberty? Yes, exactly like the law obliging party walls to stop fire spreading. Within those rules, the trade can be perfectly free.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking notes add to the money@The first|Thinking the gold is a present to foreigners@The second|Thinking a bank can issue without limit@The third"
The first slip is thinking notes add to the country's money. Gold out equals paper in. The second is thinking the gold sent abroad is a present to foreigners. It buys goods. The third is thinking a bank can issue as much as it likes. And Smith's limit needs convertible notes. In 1797, the Bank of England stopped paying coin, until 1821.
