@chapter The question
@title text="Regulated and Joint-Stock Companies" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3" art=thumb
Some trades need more than a counting house. On the west coast of Africa, merchants needed forts. In much of Europe, the state handed that duty to companies of merchants. Smith asks how they did.

@art fort-on-the-coast
His first thought is that a trade needing forts should pay for them by a moderate tax on that trade, spent by the state. Instead, companies of merchants had the address to persuade parliament to entrust them with this part of the sovereign's duty.

@words text="Burdensome or useless"
His verdict governs the whole section. Such companies may have been useful for first introducing some branches of commerce. But in the long run they have proved, universally, either burdensome or useless, and have either mismanaged or confined the trade.

@chapter Two kinds of company
@art regulated-gate
A regulated company admits anyone qualified on paying a fine, and each member trades on his own stock, at his own risk. It's like a town corporation, a monopoly of the same kind. Even a fine of twenty pounds keeps out the merchant who'd risk one voyage. To be merely useless, Smith says, is perhaps the highest praise such a company deserves.

@art joint-stock
In a joint stock company, members share profit and loss in proportion to their shares. A share can be sold, and each partner is bound only to the extent of his share. So the proprietors, who seldom pretend to understand the business, draw far bigger stocks than any partnership could.

@chapter Other people's money
@quote text="The directors of such companies, however, being the managers rather of other people's money than of their own" cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3"
Here's the argument that lasted. The directors of such companies, however, being the managers rather of other people's money than of their own, can't be expected to watch it as anxiously as partners in a private firm.

@art steward
They're like a rich man's stewards, to whom small matters seem beneath notice. So negligence and profusion must always prevail, more or less. Without an exclusive privilege, these companies commonly mismanaged the trade. With one, they both mismanaged and confined it.

@chapter The histories
@words text="African · South Sea · Hudson's Bay"
The Royal African Company couldn't compete with private traders, even when they paid a ten per cent duty. The South Sea Company lost upwards of two hundred and thirty seven thousand pounds on eight whaling voyages. The exception is the Hudson's Bay Company, small, with few proprietors, very nearly a private partnership.

@chapter The East India dividend
@math heading="Capital £3,200,000" items="at 6%: 3,200,000 · 6/100 = £192,000@six per cent;;at 10%: 3,200,000 · 10/100 = £320,000@ten per cent;;increase: 320,000 − 192,000 = £128,000@increase;;at 12.5%: 3,200,000 · 1/8 = £400,000@twelve and a half"
The East India Company is Smith's long case. Its capital after seventeen oh eight was three million two hundred thousand. Its dividend went from about six per cent, a hundred and ninety two thousand, to ten per cent, three hundred and twenty thousand, an increase of a hundred and twenty eight thousand. It wanted twelve and a half per cent, four hundred thousand.

@art dividend-bars
But a dividend isn't the company's profit, and the lab can't show what it would have earned without its monopoly. In seventeen seventy three, heavily in debt, it cut the dividend back to six per cent and asked the government for a loan of one million four hundred thousand pounds.

@words text="Perfectly indifferent about the happiness or misery of their subjects"
As a government, Smith is sharper still. No other sovereigns ever were, or could be, so perfectly indifferent about the happiness or misery of their subjects, as the proprietors of such a mercantile company.

@chapter What a joint stock can do
@art four-routines
So when can a joint stock succeed without a monopoly? Only in trades that can be reduced to a routine. Smith names four: banking, insurance from fire and sea risk, a navigable canal, and bringing water to a city. For a new and dangerous trade, he allows a temporary monopoly, like a patent for an inventor.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking every joint stock fails@The first|Taking the dividend for the profit@The second|Thinking he praised regulated companies@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Smith says every joint stock fails. He names four that can work. The second is taking the dividend for the company's profit. And the third is thinking he praised regulated companies. Twentieth century economists turned his point into the theory of agency costs.
