@chapter The question
@title text="The Instruction of People of All Ages" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3" art=thumb
How should a state pay its clergy? Smith treats preachers as he treated teachers: as people who work as hard as their pay requires. Then he finishes the chapter by saying who should pay for everything the sovereign does.

@art two-pulpits
Religious teachers can depend altogether on the voluntary contributions of their hearers, or live on a fund the law gives them, like tithes or lands. Smith's first claim is that the first kind are more zealous. An established clergy gradually lose the qualities that gave them influence with the common people, while new sects have every art of winning converts.

@art no-plunder-no-pay
In the church of Rome, parish priests draw part of their living from offerings. The begging friars draw all of it. It is with them, he says, as with the hussars and light infantry of some armies. No plunder, no pay.

@chapter Bribe their indolence
@words text="To bribe their indolence, by assigning stated salaries"
Then Smith quotes a famous argument from the most illustrious philosopher of the age, usually taken to be Hume. Clergy who live by their hearers' favour will flatter the people's passions. So the magistrate's best bargain is to bribe their indolence, by assigning stated salaries, so they do no more than keep their flocks from straying.

@chapter Many small sects
@art one-great-sect
Smith's answer turns on numbers. Religious zeal is dangerous only where one sect is tolerated, or where society is split into two or three great sects.

@art many-small-sects
Divide it into hundreds or thousands, and each teacher must learn candour and moderation, because he's among more adversaries than friends. That needs a government decided to let them all alone, and to make them let one another alone. He offers two remedies for gloomy sects too: science and philosophy, and public amusements.

@words text="Incentives, or numbers?"
Hume's view is about incentives. Smith's is about numbers. A reader can take either side with reason. Later sociologists found no consistent evidence that more sects meant more participation, but Smith's question was about peace and moderation, a proposal about government, not a measurement.

@chapter What a church costs
@words text="The richer the church, the poorer the sovereign or the people"
An established church's revenue, Smith says, is a branch of the general revenue of the state. So all other things equal, the richer the church, the poorer must be either the sovereign or the people.

@math heading="The church of Scotland, 1755" items="68,514 / 944 = 34,257/472@divide;;≈ £72.58 ≈ £72 11s a year@about seventy two"
His example of how little a church needs is Scotland. In seventeen fifty five, its whole revenue was about sixty eight thousand five hundred and fourteen pounds, for nine hundred and forty four ministers. Divide, and each averages about seventy two pounds eleven shillings a year, a decent subsistence.

@art modest-livings
And yet, he says, the most opulent church in Christendom does not better maintain faith, devotion, order and austere morals than this very poorly endowed church of Scotland. Underpay a service and it suffers from meanness. Overpay it, and it suffers perhaps still more from negligence and idleness.

@math heading="Three livings" items="minister: £72.58 · from hearers 0@minister;;priest: 40 + 200 · 1/10 = £60 · a third from hearers@priest;;friar: 0 + 300 · 1/10 = £30 · all from hearers@friar"
Set the Scottish average beside two cases written for the lesson. A parish priest on forty pounds, with two hundred hearers giving two shillings each, has sixty, a third from offerings. A friar with three hundred hearers has thirty, all from them. The minister has the most, and none depends on his hearers.

@chapter Who pays for what
@art who-pays-what
The Conclusion sorts every expense. Defence and the sovereign's dignity benefit everyone, so everyone pays, in proportion to their abilities. Justice can be paid by those who use it. Local works by local revenue, since it's unjust for the whole society to pay for what benefits only a part. Roads by tolls. Schools and churches by everyone, or equally well by those who use them.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Taking the Hume passage as Smith's own@The first|Praising Scotland for poverty alone@The second|Reading the Conclusion as defence only@And the third"
The first slip is taking the Hume passage for Smith's own view. He quotes it to answer it. The second is reading his praise of Scotland as praise of poverty alone. And the third is thinking the Conclusion says the state should pay only for defence. It doesn't settle how large the state should be.
