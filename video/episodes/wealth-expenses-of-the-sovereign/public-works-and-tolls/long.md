@chapter The question
@title text="Public Works and Tolls" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3" art=thumb
Who should pay for a road? The sovereign's third duty is to build works so useful to a society that no individual could ever be repaid for them. Smith thinks most of the ones that help trade can still pay their own way.

@quote text="The expense of making and maintaining the public roads of any country must evidently increase with the annual produce of the land and labour of that country." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3"
The expense of making and maintaining the public roads of any country must evidently increase with the annual produce of the land and labour of that country. More goods, more carriages, more wear.

@chapter A toll by weight
@art toll-bridge
His first suggestion is that a highway, bridge or canal can usually be made and kept up by a small toll on the carriages that use it. Compare a toll bridge with a bridge paid from everyone's rates. The toll bridge has a built in test of whether anyone wants it.

@art weighbridge
Charge each carriage in proportion to its weight or tonnage, and each pays exactly in proportion to the wear and tear it causes. It seems scarce possible, he says, to invent a more equitable way of maintaining such works.

@words text="Paid by the consumer, who still gains"
The carrier advances the toll, but the consumer finally pays it in the price of the goods. And since the road makes carriage cheaper, the price isn't raised as much by the toll as it's lowered by the road. The person who pays gains more than he loses.

@chapter The turnpike
@math heading="Toll: 1s a ton a trip = £1/20" items="waggon: 3 · 100 · 1/20 = £15@waggon;;coach: 1 · 200 · 1/20 = £10@coach;;total: £25 · waggon's share 15/25 = 3/5@total"
Here's a turnpike, with figures written for the lesson. A shilling a ton a trip is a twentieth of a pound. A three ton waggon making a hundred trips pays fifteen pounds. A one ton coach making two hundred pays ten. The total is twenty five, and the waggon pays three fifths.

@art coach-and-waggon
That's what equitable means to Smith. The waggon carries three hundred ton trips and the coach two hundred, so they pay in proportion to tons, not vehicles. Double the toll, and every figure doubles, but nobody's share moves.

@art coal-cart
Add a two ton coal cart making four hundred trips. It pays forty pounds, two thirds of the total. A toll by weight bears on whatever moves the most tons, and that's coarse, bulky goods. Smith adds a tilt: charge coaches a bit more per ton, and the vanity of the rich relieves the poor. The lab can't show that tilt.

@chapter Who holds the tolls
@art canal-and-road
A canal's tolls can be private property. The canal of Languedoc cost upwards of thirteen million livres, and its tolls went to its engineer's family, who had every reason to keep it repaired. A neglected canal stops carrying anything. But a neglected road is still passable, so a road's tolls belong with trustees.

@chapter Not a general revenue
@list heading="Three objections" items="The tolls would be raised until trade was choked@First|A toll by weight falls on the poor@Second|A rich government repairs less@Third"
Some ministers hoped the turnpikes could bring the state half a million a year. Smith objects three times. First, a revenue gets raised with the state's needs, until inland trade is choked. Second, a toll on weight, not value, would fall at the expense of the poor, not the rich. Third, a government rich from tolls is harder to make repair the road.

@art splendid-and-neglected
France shows the risk. Under the intendants, the great post roads between cities were in good order, while most cross roads were neglected. A minister prefers a work of splendour that the nobility will see. The principle is local: let those who benefit pay, and manage the money near them.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Thinking Smith wants a toll everywhere@The first|Taking a toll by weight for one by value@The second|Assuming the turnpikes were well run@And the third"
The first slip is thinking Smith wants a toll at every public work. The second is taking a toll by weight for a toll by value. And the third is assuming the turnpikes he praises were well run. He reports abuses. Later engineers found road damage rises much faster than weight.
