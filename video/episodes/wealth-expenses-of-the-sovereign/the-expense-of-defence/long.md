@chapter The question
@title text="The Expense of Defence" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 1" art=thumb
Book Five turns from how wealth is made to how a government is paid for. The first and least avoidable item on the bill is the army. Smith asks a surprising question. Why does a soldier cost more, the richer the country gets?

@quote text="that of protecting the society from the violence and invasion of other independent societies, can be performed only by means of a military force." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 1"
The first duty of the sovereign, that of protecting the society from the violence and invasion of other independent societies, can be performed only by means of a military force. And its cost is very different in different states of society.

@chapter Hunters and shepherds
@words text="Every man a warrior, as well as a hunter"
Among hunters, every man is a warrior as well as a hunter, and he feeds himself in the field as at home. So the sovereign pays nothing. Smith's account of these peoples comes from travellers' reports. Read it as a stage in his argument, not a field study.

@art herds-to-war
Among shepherds, the herds and flocks go to war with the men and feed them. A Tartar chief spends nothing preparing a man for the field, and once he's in it, the chance of plunder is the only pay he expects.

@chapter Husbandmen
@art seedtime-harvest
Farmers come next, and here the cost begins. A farmer can take the field after seedtime and be home for harvest, because Nature does most of the farm's work meanwhile. So a short campaign costs little. The Greeks served this way until after the second Persian war, and the Romans until the siege of Veii.

@chapter Manufactures and long wars
@art empty-workshop
Then two causes make it impossible: the progress of manufactures, and the improvement of the art of war. For the smith or the weaver, Nature does nothing. He does all for himself. The moment he leaves his workshop, his income stops. So the public must maintain him. And once wars run for several campaigns a year, no private man can bear the cost.

@chapter One in a hundred
@art one-in-a-hundred
That sets a ceiling. In ancient Greece, a fourth or a fifth of the people thought of themselves as soldiers. In modern Europe, Smith says, it's commonly computed that not more than one hundredth of the people can be employed as soldiers, without ruin to the country that pays for them.

@math heading="1,000,000 people at 1/100" items="soldiers: 1,000,000 · 1/100 = 10,000@ten thousand;;cost: 10,000 · 20 = £200,000@twenty pounds;;per head: 200,000 / 1,000,000 = £1/5@a fifth"
Let's compute, with a population and a cost written for the lesson. A million people at one in a hundred is ten thousand soldiers. At twenty pounds a year each, that's two hundred thousand pounds. Spread over the million, it's a fifth of a pound, four shillings a head, with ninety nine others at work for every soldier.

@math heading="One in twenty, not Smith's" items="1,000,000 · 1/20 = 50,000@one in twenty;;50,000 · 20 = £1,000,000@a million pounds"
Now try one in twenty, which isn't Smith's figure. That's fifty thousand soldiers, and a million pounds a year, a pound a head, with only nineteen at work for each. The ceiling isn't a rule of arithmetic. The rest must feed the soldiers, and themselves, and the officers of government and law.

@chapter Militia or standing army
@art drilled-ranks
A rich nation is the most likely to be attacked, and its artificers have no leisure for drill. So the state can force everyone to practise arms, a militia, or make soldiering a separate trade, a standing army. Only the wisdom of the state can make soldiering a particular trade. And Smith judges a well drilled standing army always beats a militia.

@words text="Dangerous to liberty?"
Critics in his day called a standing army dangerous to liberty, and Smith grants the examples of Caesar and Cromwell. His friend Adam Ferguson argued for a citizen militia. Smith's answer, that an army led by the sovereign and the gentry can't threaten liberty, is a claim about Britain, not a theorem.

@art javelin-and-cannon
Last, the weapons. A musket costs more than a javelin, a cannon more than a catapult, and powder spent in a review is gone for good. So firearms favour the rich. In ancient times the opulent struggled to defend themselves against poor and barbarous nations. In modern times, the reverse.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading one in a hundred as a law@The first|Thinking militias fail as amateurs@The second|Taking hunters and shepherds for field reports@And the third"
The first slip is reading one in a hundred as a law. It's a remark about Smith's Europe. The second is thinking a militia fails just because it's amateur. After a few campaigns, it becomes a standing army. And the third is taking his hunters and shepherds for field reports.
