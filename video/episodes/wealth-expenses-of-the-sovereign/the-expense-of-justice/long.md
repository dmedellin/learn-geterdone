@chapter The question
@title text="The Expense of Justice" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 2" art=thumb
The sovereign's second duty is protecting each person from the injustice of every other. Smith finds that how much that costs, and who pays, depends on one thing: property.

@quote text="Where there is no property, or at least none that exceeds the value of two or three days labour, civil government is not so necessary." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 2"
Where there is no property, or at least none that exceeds the value of two or three days labour, civil government is not so necessary. A hunter can wrong another only in his person or his name, and gains nothing by it.

@chapter Property needs a magistrate
@art locked-door
With property, it's different. The benefit of the person who does the injury is often equal to the loss of him who suffers it. Avarice and ambition in the rich, and in the poor the hatred of labour, never rest. So the rich man, surrounded by unknown enemies, can sleep securely only under the civil magistrate.

@words text="Civil government ... is, in reality, instituted for the defence of the rich against the poor"
Hence the best known sentence of this part. Civil government, so far as it is instituted for the security of property, is, in reality, instituted for the defence of the rich against the poor. Smith states it as the consequence of a stage of society, not as a defence or an attack.

@chapter Four sources of rank
@art four-ranks
Before law settles rank, four things sort people. Personal qualities come first but give the least, since they're always disputable. Age is better, being plain and palpable. Fortune is greater still. And birth supposes an ancient superiority of fortune in the family.

@art tartar-chief
Fortune counts most among shepherds. A Tartar chief whose flocks can feed a thousand men can use them no other way, so a thousand men obey him in war and accept his judgment in peace. In a rich society, a man may own far more and still not command a dozen people, because everyone pays for what he gets.

@chapter Justice for sale
@art present-in-hand
For a long time, the courts were not a cost but a revenue. Suitors brought presents, and the guilty paid fines for breaking the king's peace. Smith sees the flaw. The person who applied for justice with a large present was likely to get something more than justice. With a small one, something less.

@words text="Justice, however, never was in reality administered gratis in any country."
When sovereigns paid judges salaries instead, justice was said to be free. Smith's reply is dry. Justice, however, never was in reality administered gratis in any country. The parties still pay lawyers and attorneys, far more than the judges' salaries.

@chapter Fees after judgment
@art cashier-and-court
His design has three safeguards. Fees are fixed by law, paid all at once into a cashier's hands, and shared among the judges only after the case is decided. No suitor can buy a judge, yet pay follows the work. Public services are never better performed than when their reward comes only once they're performed.

@math heading="Toulouse: salary £6 11s = £6.55" items="diligent: 120 · 1/2 = £60 → 6.55 + 60 = £66.55@diligent;;idle: 8 · 1/2 = £4 → 6.55 + 4 = £10.55@idle"
Smith's one figure is a counsellor's salary at Toulouse: a hundred and fifty livres, about six pounds eleven, or six fifty five. The caseload and the ten shilling fee are written for the lesson. A diligent counsellor deciding a hundred and twenty causes earns sixty in fees, sixty six fifty five in all. An idle one deciding eight earns ten fifty five.

@words text="A comfortable, though moderate revenue"
That's Smith's contrast. A diligent judge gains a comfortable, though moderate revenue by his office. An idle one gets little more than his salary. Critics like Bentham replied that charges on going to law fall hardest on the poor, and can shut them out of justice altogether.

@chapter Keep the courts apart
@art separate-powers
The most lasting argument comes last. When the judicial is united to the executive power, justice is often sacrificed to politics. So the judge should be as independent of that power as possible, and his salary shouldn't depend on its goodwill.

@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading the rich and poor sentence as his whole view@The first|Believing salaries made justice free@The second|Treating fees as the old presents@And the third"
The first slip is reading the sentence about rich and poor as Smith's whole view of government. The second is believing salaries made justice free. And the third is treating his fees as the old presents. Those came before the case. His come after.
