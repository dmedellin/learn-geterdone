@chapter The question
@title text="The Education of Youth" sub="The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3" art=thumb
Do public endowments make teachers better? It seems obvious they should. Smith thinks they made teachers worse, and then argues that one class of people really does need public help with schooling.

@quote text="In every profession, the exertion of the greater part of those who exercise it, is always in proportion to the necessity they are under of making that exertion." cite="Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book V, Chapter 1, Part 3"
He starts from a general rule. In every profession, the exertion of the greater part of those who exercise it, is always in proportion to the necessity they are under of making that exertion.

@chapter Salary against duty
@art empty-lecture-hall
Where a teacher is barred from fees and lives on a salary, his interest is set as directly against his duty as possible. And if his supervisors are other teachers, they'll be indulgent to each other. Hence the famous line. At Oxford, the greater part of the public professors have long given up altogether even the pretence of teaching.

@words text="A sort of statutes of apprenticeship"
Smith had studied at Balliol, and at Glasgow part of his income came from students' fees. He adds that privileges of graduation force students to universities whatever their teachers' merit, a sort of statutes of apprenticeship. College discipline is contrived not for the students, but for the ease of the masters.

@art fencing-school
The evidence on his side? The parts of education with no public institution are generally the best taught. A fencing or dancing school teaches fencing and dancing. Endowments, he says, have not only corrupted public teachers, but made it almost impossible to have good private ones.

@chapter Salary or fees
@math heading="Three teachers, £100 each" items="salaried: 100 + 0 = £100 · one more pupil adds 0@salaried;;mixed: 50 + 25 · 2 = £100 · adds 2@mixed;;fee master: 0 + 50 · 2 = £100 · adds 2@fee master"
Here's the contrast, with figures written for the lesson. Three teachers each earn a hundred pounds a year. The salaried one gets it all from an endowment. The mixed one gets fifty in salary and fifty in fees, from twenty five pupils at two pounds. The fee master gets it all from fifty pupils.

@art fee-and-salary
The incomes match. What differs is what depends on the work. One more pupil adds nothing for the salaried teacher, and two pounds for the others. Smith's argument is for the middle case, a fee large enough to matter.

@chapter The labourer's mind
@art one-operation
So far he's attacked the teachers of the rich. Now he turns to the labourer. As labour divides, most workers' jobs shrink to a few very simple operations, often one or two. A man whose whole life is spent that way has no occasion to exert his understanding, and generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become.

@words text="Unless government takes some pains to prevent it"
This is the same Smith who praised the division of labour in Book One. He's counting a cost those chapters left out. And his conclusion is conditional. This is the state the great body of the people must fall into, unless government takes some pains to prevent it. Marx later quoted the passage.

@chapter The little school
@art parish-school
His fix is cheap. A little school in every parish, with a fee so moderate that even a common labourer can afford it. The master is partly, but not wholly, paid by the public. Wholly paid, he would soon learn to neglect his business.

@math heading="A parish school" items="40 children · 5s = 40 · 1/4 = £10@forty children;;5 + 10 = £15 · two thirds from fees@fifteen"
In the lesson's example, the parish pays the master five pounds, and forty children pay five shillings each, which is ten pounds. So he earns fifteen, and two thirds of it depends on his pupils. A master paid fifteen entirely by the public has none of it at stake.

@art read-write-account
The public can facilitate this with schools, encourage it with prizes, and impose it with an examination before a man sets up a trade. In Scotland, parish schools taught almost the whole common people to read, and many to write and account. An instructed people, he says, are always more decent and orderly.

@chapter Where people slip
@list heading="Common slips" items="Reading Oxford as every university@The first|Thinking he left schooling to the market@The second|Taking his remark on women as a programme@And the third"
The first slip is reading the Oxford line as a verdict on every university. The second is thinking Smith left the labourer's schooling to the market. And the third is taking his remark on women's education as a programme. Mary Wollstonecraft later pressed that question hard.
