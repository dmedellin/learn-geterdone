## Slavery and the native peoples: checked against the text

The course quotes Smith on these subjects at length and does not soften him. I read every such passage against the text and found the quotations exact. These passages are:

- St. Domingo stripped in "six or eight years" (l. 18060–18080);
- the "cruel destruction of the natives" and the creoles remark (l. 18304–18307);
- "Folly and injustice… harmless natives" (Part 2's close);
- the sugar colonies and the slaves (l. 18934–19003);
- "dreadful misfortunes" to the natives of both Indies (Part 3).

Four things were inaccurate or one-sided, and I fixed them:

- **L2, sugar colonies.** The lesson said the French colonies "did as well as the English" and that the difference lay in slave management. Smith says "at least equal, perhaps superior, to that of the greater part of those of England", and gives two reasons: the French were free to refine their own sugar, and, "of still greater importance", their slaves were better managed (l. 18936–18942). The lesson now gives both.
- **L2, Smith's argument for protecting slaves.** The lesson presented it purely as "the vocabulary of profit and management, comparing slaves to cattle". That left out his appeal to humanity: the magistrate protects the slave "and common humanity naturally disposes him to do so" (l. 18965). It now says he argues "partly from humanity and partly from profit".
- **L2, Book III.** Added Smith's own explanation of why slavery persists although it is "the dearest of any": "The pride of man makes him love to domineer", and "The planting of sugar and tobacco can afford the expense of slave cultivation" (l. 12535–12552).
- **L3, editorial voice.** The lesson made its own judgment ("how little a hope for a balance of force does for those who died"). I replaced it with a neutral sentence that leaves Smith's account in his words.

## Other fixes

**Accuracy against the text.**
- L1: "reached India round the Cape in 1497" is now "sailed for India", since da Gama arrived eleven months later (l. 17956–17958).
- L1: "a lottery with one prize in twenty" is not in this Part. It is now Book I's "twenty fail for one that succeeds" (l. 3444).
- L2: Smith gives no per-head cost of the colonial establishments. The lesson said its per-head figure was "roughly his"; it now says it is the lesson's, with his "no exact account has been got" for Maryland and North Carolina (l. 18498).
- L3: Smith used "a nation of shopkeepers" twice in one passage (l. 19895–19896), so "uses the phrase once" is now "in one passage only".
- L3: the preset `smiths-thousand-pounds` named the once-a-year trade "near-trade" and the twice-a-year trade "foreign-trade". That reverses Smith, whose once-a-year case is "a foreign trade of consumption" (l. 19433). The trades are renamed `once-a-year` and `twice-a-year`, and the body and worked example are updated. I re-pinned from `--observe` after checking by hand; the figures are unchanged.
- L4: "the single exception of defence" is now Chapter 2's two cases, defence and an equal duty.

**Lab (L5, `artificers-who-save`).** The preset had the artificers *produce* 450, but the label and prose called it saving. It now keeps produce at 400 and advances at 200 and cuts consumption to 150, giving a net product of 50. That matches the system's "parsimony and privation" (quoted) and Smith's fourth objection. The tiles are unchanged (`artificers +£50`, total `+£250`); I verified them by hand and they are pinned.

**Accuracy about others.** Eric Williams now has his title and his actual thesis: *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) argued that slavery's profits did much to finance British industry. Checked and correct as written: Malthus 1798 on American doubling, Thomas 1965 on the Navigation Acts, Olson 1965, List 1841, and the Capital lesson titles (*The Modern Theory of Colonisation*, *Genesis of the Industrial Capitalist*, *Former Discussions*).
