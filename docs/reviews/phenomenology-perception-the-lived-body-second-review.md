# Second review: The Lived Body (`perception-the-lived-body`, `c3_lived_body`)

Brief: `docs/phenomenology/SECOND-REVIEW.md`, held to the "Lessons from"
sections of `docs/phenomenology/AUTHORING.md`. I read Smith pp. 77–170 whole
against all eight lessons, rendered the course, read every preset's tiles with
`labcheck.js --observe`, and checked the clinical citations (the Schneider
re-examinations and reviews, the Ramachandran aside) against the published
papers. I wrote these findings before opening the pedagogy assessment.

**Verdict.** The arithmetic is all right: every tile, worked line and quiz
answer recomputes, and every figure is credited to the model that computes it.
All but one of the clinical names and dates are right. The exception is the
modern reviews of Schneider: the course credited Marotta and Behrmann with a
diagnosis they do not give, and gave Goldenberg a diagnosis he does not make.
The other errors are in sequence and attribution: "the next sentence" that is
in fact a page later, a symbolic function credited to Cassirer that the
chapter's note gives to Head, a Bergson definition stated as a fact when the
book reports it only in a note, and one concept gloss that says the phantom is
the habit-body. All are fixed. The preview and the quotation checker are still
OK.

## Findings

### 1. Experience and Objective Thought

- **The reservation is not the "next sentence" (fixed).** The body said "Now
  read the next sentence… It begins with 'But'". The second mistake said "the
  very next paragraph adds", and quiz 3 asked what the introduction says
  "right after" calling the house the house seen from everywhere, with a `why`
  that said "the next paragraph begins with that reservation". In Smith the
  passage (p. 79) is followed by a paragraph extending the point to time (pp.
  79–80, the house an old man or a child might see). Only after that does the
  reservation come, beginning "But, once more" (p. 80). That second
  paragraph's "once more" is the hinge, and the lesson had cut it out.
  Changed: `read_intro`, the body paragraph (it now names the temporal
  paragraph and quotes "But, once more" in single marks), the mistake ("a page
  later"), the quiz stem ("Once it has called the house… and said the same of
  time, what reservation does the introduction add?") and the `why`. The §C
  entry in PLAN said "at once adds, p. 80". I corrected those words in §C.
- Leibniz, the lamp, the film close-up, the pipes and cracks, Kierkegaard's
  "objective thought" and the crystal are all accurately located and voiced.
  The lab: `0 3 4` has length 5, so front 4/5 and above 3/5; `1 2 2` has
  length 3, so 2/3, 1/3, 2/3; union 5 of 6, max 3, cover east and west. All
  correct, and all said to be the projection's figures, not what anyone sees.
- *Recommendation (taste):* "Neither side of that dilemma is the house you see
  from the street" is opaque. The chapter's own dilemma (p. 82) is "having to
  fail to understand either the subject or the object", and quoting that or
  paraphrasing it would serve better.

### 2. The Phantom Limb

- **The in-itself and the for-itself are not "Sartre's vocabulary" (fixed).**
  The lesson said "In the vocabulary Sartre had given the in-itself and the
  for-itself two years before". The terms are Hegel's. *Being and
  Nothingness* (1943) gave them their currency in French philosophy, and p. 89
  of the chapter uses them without naming Sartre. The sentence now says the
  terms go back to Hegel and that Sartre's book had put them at the centre of
  French philosophy two years before.
- **Ramachandran (fixed).** "The neurologist V. S. Ramachandran" is now "the
  neuroscientist". He is a physician by training but works as a
  neuroscientist, and "neurologist" suggests clinical specialty practice. The
  rest of the aside checks out: the mirror box was reported in the mid-1990s
  (Ramachandran, Rogers-Ramachandran and Cobb, *Nature* 1995; Ramachandran
  and Rogers-Ramachandran, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 1996), and some patients reported
  a clenched phantom unclenching. Both §C caveats are present: the evidence is
  mixed and mostly of low quality, and his explanation sits nearer the
  *image* reading. The aside is in a late `p` and stated on both sides,
  within §F.1.
- Checked: Stein (pp. 85–86), the physiologist's objection in his own voice
  (pp. 87–88), cocaine and brain injury (p. 88), the posture and the shell
  splinters, emotion, the shrinking "as the patient consents", anosognosia and
  the translator's gloss, the cut nerves (p. 89), the hybrid "valid as a
  statement of the known facts; but… fundamentally obscure", Lhermitte's own
  conclusion (n. 15), organic repression (Schilder, Menninger-Lerchenthal,
  Lhermitte), the insect from *The Structure of Behaviour*, and the patients
  near blindness (p. 92). *L'Image de notre corps* is 1939. All correct.
- *Recommendation:* the §C note says cocaine and brain injury stay in the prose
  because "they tell against the peripheral theory only". The posture of the
  injury is used the same way on p. 88, yet it is a table row coded `n` for
  the physiological explanation. The coding is defensible: a phantom keeping
  the arm's position at the moment of the wound is a memory-shaped fact. But
  the lesson could say in a clause why posture is in the table and cocaine is
  not.

### 6. Schneider: Concrete and Abstract Movement

- **The modern reviews were misattributed (fixed; also in `not_covered`).**
  The lesson and the course `not_covered` said that Goldenberg (2003) and
  Marotta and Behrmann (2004) "read it as a visual form agnosia and judge that
  the theoretical conclusions went beyond the data". Neither paper says that.
  Marotta and Behrmann, "Patient Schn: has Goldstein and Gelb's case withstood
  the test of time?", *Neuropsychologia* 42 (2004) 633–638, note that Schn was
  reported as "the first well-defined example of apperceptive visual agnosia",
  grant the doubts, and "propose that perhaps the case of Schn should be
  reclassified as an example of integrative agnosia". Goldenberg's chapter,
  "Goldstein and Gelb's case Schn.: a classic case in neuropsychology?" (in
  Code et al., eds, *Classic Cases in Neuropsychology* II, 2003), questions
  whether the case deserves the name and judges, as Marotta and Behrmann
  report him, that Gelb and Goldstein "were too eager for an all-embracing
  theory". He does not argue for a diagnosis of form agnosia.
- **"Neurologists who examined him again after the war" was vague and partly
  wrong (fixed).** A reader takes "the war" to be the one in which he was
  wounded. The re-examinations were in 1949: Bay, Lauenstein and Cibis, and
  separately Richard Jung. Not all of them were neurologists. Jung found that
  Schn could see and recognise most objects (as reported by Marotta and
  Behrmann).
- The paragraph now reads: wounded June 1915 (Marotta and Behrmann: 4 June
  1915, by mine splinters; the book, p. 145, says a shell splinter at the back
  of the head, and the lesson keeps that as the book's report); Gelb, a
  psychologist, and Goldstein, a neurologist, published from 1918 in
  Frankfurt (both correct); 1949, "four years after this book", Bay,
  Lauenstein and Cibis, and Jung; Jung's finding; Goldenberg's doubt; Marotta
  and Behrmann's proposal of an integrative visual agnosia. The sentence that
  the book's argument concerns the case as reported stays.
- **What Schneider lacks (fixed in body, quiz 4 and its `why`).** "Neither
  motility nor thought, but the power of projection between them" ran two
  passages together. On p. 126 the something between them is a *motor
  project* or *motor intentionality*. *Projection* (p. 128) is the normal
  function that makes abstract movement possible. Both are now named in their
  places.
- **Cerebellar patients "with no disorder of sight" overstated the text
  (fixed in body, quiz 3 answer and `why`, and mistake 1).** P. 131 says that
  in cerebellar cases visual stimuli do produce imperfect reactions, yet there
  is "no reason to presume any primary disturbance of the visual function".
  The wording is now "no primary disorder of sight".
- Checked: the passage and its context (pp. 117–118: psychic blindness,
  wallets, three quarters), the dissociation "in the same patient, and also
  in cerebellar cases", the nose with n. 12 ("This is a cerebellar case"), the
  ruler, the salute and the mirror, play-acting (Sartre and Diderot in the
  notes), the mosquito and the phenomenal body, the leather "to be cut up",
  beckoning the friend, centripetal and centrifugal, the occipital injury and
  Mill (pp. 129–130), Gelb and Goldstein's first works giving the visual
  reading (n. 36), the patient S whom Goldstein sets beside Schneider (n. 44),
  and the auxiliary hypothesis (p. 135). All correct. The lesson presents the
  visual reading at strength and as Gelb and Goldstein's own first reading.
- *Recommendation:* "There are, moreover, no explanations without
  comprehension" (p. 132) is quoted after the argument of pp. 134–136, which
  inverts the order of the text. It does no harm, but "earlier, the chapter
  had said…" would be exact.
- *Not changed, for the plan owner:* PLAN §G carries the same misattribution
  ("modern reviews … read the case as a visual form agnosia and judge that the
  theoretical conclusions went beyond the data"), and calls all the 1949
  examiners neurologists. §G is shared across courses, so I left it. It
  should be corrected to match the paragraph above before any other course
  prints the citation.

### 7. The Intentional Arc

  function" and Van Woerkom for "projection". Cassirer enters on p. 143 and in

- **Hochheimer as "Goldstein's student" was unsupported (fixed).** The book
  says only that Benary, Hochheimer and Steinfeld published under Gelb and
  Goldstein's editorship (n. 36), and that is now what the lesson says.

### 8. Habit and the Blind Man's Cane

- **Bergson's definition rests on a note and was stated as fact (fixed).** "Henri
  Bergson… called habit 'the fossilized residue of a spiritual activity'"
  became "A note gives Henri Bergson… as an example, defining habit as…".
  Smith n. 105 is the only source, and AUTHORING forbids quoting from a
  secondary source. The phrase is the book's attribution, and the lesson
  should not certify it as Bergson's words. The mistake already says "Bergson's
  phrase for it is in the chapter's note".
- **"It calls habit 'a rearrangement and renewal of the corporeal schema'"
  (fixed).** The phrase qualifies *the acquisition of habit* (p. 164). The
  lesson now says "It takes the acquisition of habit as…".
- **"The organist shows it best" (fixed).** That was the lesson's verdict. It
  is the text's own claim ("shows even better", p. 167), so it is now
  attributed: "The organist, the chapter says, shows it even better."
- Checked: the dance, "kapiert", the passage, the car's wings, potentialities
  of volume, the Underground gate, the stick "not… as a middle term", the
  dilating of being-in-the-world, the typist and "knowledge in the hands", the
  key-bank space, Chevalier's *L'Habitude* (n. 109), the hour's practice, the
