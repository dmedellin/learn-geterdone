## Fixes made

**Lesson 1, The Origin and Use of Money**

1. **Accuracy.** "That is why twelve pence make a shilling" claimed something the text does not say. Smith says the penny was 1/240 of a pound, so 240 pence make a pound. He also says the shilling's number of pence varied ("only five pennies" among the Saxons) until the time of William the Conqueror (l. 890-908). The sentence now says that.
2. **Lab.** The preset `market-town` had no traders, so three of its six tiles printed "no traders given". I added six traders written for the lesson (butcher, shoemaker and tanner in a ring; weaver and shepherd who coincide; a smith whose iron nobody wants). Now every tile means something. I checked each tile by hand first: 66 ratios / 11 prices / 55 fewer; direct pair "weaver–shepherd"; through a money "butcher, shoemaker, tanner"; no buyer "smith". The observe output agreed, and I pinned it. The label and body paragraph now describe the traders.
3. `one-match`: "the brewer is still left out" now says why: no one wants his beer. This matches the `wmoNoBuyer` tile.
4. **Others.** The Humphrey (1985) sentence now says what she wrote: that no economy run on barter alone had been described. The old wording, "found no example of a society…", read as if it were fieldwork. Graeber (2011) is unchanged.

**Lesson 3, Corn Rents and Money Rents**

5. **Prose.** "a change of a fall of 75 per cent" is now "a fall of 75 per cent".
6. **Label.** The `dear-year` label now says the 250s rent is written for the lesson. The 25s and 50s are Smith's (l. 1196).
7. **Teaching.** The second mistake said the `dear-year` preset shows a corn rent's worth in labour doubling. The preset actually prints a money rent halving in corn. The mistake now says the preset shows the other side, and works the corn rent's doubling from it, using Smith's steady wage (l. 1197-1202).
8. **Others.** The text said "later economists replaced it with … the index number, which Jevons used in 1863". It now says the index number had eighteenth-century forerunners and that Jevons used it in 1863. Jevons's 1863 use is right, but the old wording made him look like its originator.

**Lesson 4, Gold, Silver and Copper Coin**

9. **Others.** "Gresham's law, a name given in 1858 by H. D. Macleod" is softened to "a name Macleod gave it in the 1850s". Sources put it in 1857 or 1858.
