# Phenomenology spec: scholarly accuracy review

Reviewed: `docs/phenomenology/PLAN.md` (branch `feat/phenomenology-subject`).
The book was not available in the environment. Everything below comes from the
reviewer's knowledge of *Phenomenology of Perception* (Smith 1962, Landes 2012,
Gallimard 1945) and its sources. Each finding is marked:

- **CERTAIN**: no check needed (arithmetic, or a fact the reviewer is sure of).
- **LIKELY**: the reviewer is fairly sure, but the page should confirm it.
- **CHECK-AGAINST-TEXT**: the reviewer is unsure; settle it from the operator's copy before authoring.

Page references are to Smith (1962) unless marked otherwise. They are
approximate.

---

## 1. Misplacements

**1. Habit examples are in Chapter 3, not Chapter 4.** Location: §B course 4; §C
course 4 lesson 1 (`habit-and-the-blind-mans-cane`, module "Part One · Chapter 4").
Problem: the feather in the hat, the car through a gate, the typist, the organist
and the blind man's stick are in the closing pages of Part One, Chapter 3
("The Spatiality of One's Own Body and Motricity"), in its section on acquiring
a habit as a reworking of the body schema (Smith c. pp. 142–7). Chapter 4, "The
Synthesis of One's Own Body", is short (Smith c. pp. 148–53). It argues that the
body's unity is like a work of art's, not a physical object's or a law's, then
returns briefly to habit as *perceptual* habit, using the cane again. The spec
splits courses 3 and 4 at a boundary that falls in the wrong place for its own
first lesson. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: either (a) move the habit lesson into
course 3 as "Part One · Chapter 3" and write a new course 4 lesson on Chapter 4
(see finding 25), or (b) keep the lesson where it is, but give it module
"Chapter 3 (end) and Chapter 4" and make its Read pointer separate the two. (a)
follows the book.

**2. The glass belongs to "Sensing", not "The Thing".** Location: course 6
lesson 3 (`the-thing-and-the-real`). Problem: the glass whose hardness and
brittleness are *seen*, and whose breaking sound is "conveyed by the visible
glass", sits with the steel, the plane blade and the shavings in the
synaesthesia passage of Part Two, Chapter 1 (Smith c. pp. 229–30). That passage
is about how the senses communicate. The "single manner of being" sentence about
glass is probably there too. Chapter 3's own examples of a thing's intersensory
unity are different: honey (taken from Sartre), the style of a thing, the thing
"in-itself-for-us", Cézanne. Confidence: LIKELY on the first sentence; CHECK-AGAINST-TEXT
on where "single manner of being" falls. Fix: move the glass preset to course 5
lesson 3 (`the-unity-of-the-senses`), where it fits the "Read" exactly, and use
honey or a Cézanne example in course 6 lesson 3. If the author finds the glass
in Chapter 3 as well, cite both pages.

**3. The ship on the beach is in Introduction III and is missing.** Location: course 2
lesson 4 (`attention-and-judgment`), and the spec as a whole, which never uses
it. Problem: walking along a shore towards a stranded ship whose funnel and masts
merge with the forest behind it, until they suddenly "become part of the ship".
This is Merleau-Ponty's main counter-example to the intellectualist claim that
perceptual unity is a judgement. It sits in Introduction III (Smith c. p. 17).
It fits the `cues/sequence` view better than anything the spec now gives that
view. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: add it to the Read of course 2 lesson 4, or make
it a second preset (`cues/sequence`: masts and funnel read as trees, then as the
ship).

**4. The "corrected illusion" in course 6 lesson 4 is left unnamed.** Location: course 6
lesson 4 (`the-natural-world`). Problem: the spec leaves the example to the
author. The best-known corrected illusion in the book is the "broad flat stone"
on a sunken road that turns out to be a patch of sunlight. The reviewer cannot
place it with confidence: it may be in Part Two Ch. 3 or in Part Three Ch. 1,
where illusion and the certainty of perception come up again. Confidence:
CHECK-AGAINST-TEXT. Fix: the author finds the example in the natural-world
pages and names its page. If the stone is not in Part Two Ch. 3, do not import
it from the Cogito chapter without saying so.

**5. Course 2 places attention under intellectualism only.** Location: course 2
summary ("intellectualism's attention and judgement") and lesson 4. Problem:
Introduction III criticises *both* accounts of attention. For empiricism,
attention is the searchlight that lights what is already there. For
intellectualism, attention also finds everything already constituted. In both
it is sterile, because neither lets attention *make* the object it attends to.
Only judgement is distinctively intellectualist. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: write
"attention, as both accounts conceive it, and intellectualism's judgement", and
in the Read: "attention as the empiricist's searchlight and as the
intellectualist's ...".

**6. The moon's textual home is unconfirmed.** Location: course 1 lesson 1,

where the spec opens with it. Merleau-Ponty does discuss the horizon moon
(including looking at it through a cardboard tube) as a case against both
classical accounts. The reviewer is not sure whether this is in Introduction III
or in the "Depth" section of Part Two Ch. 2, and it may be in both. Confidence:
CERTAIN that it is not in the Preface; CHECK-AGAINST-TEXT for its location.
Fix: in course 1 lesson 1, say in prose that the moon is the lesson's
illustration and that the book's own treatment comes later (name where). In
course 2 lesson 5, keep the moon among "the Introduction's cases" only if it is
in the Introduction.

## 2. Misrepresentations and quotations

**12. Phi movement is misattributed.** Location: course 5 lesson 6 (`movement`),
Misconception ("the phi phenomenon shows motion with no thing moving") and Read
("movement without a moving thing"). Problem: that is Wertheimer's
interpretation ("pure phi"). Merleau-Ponty discusses it and qualifies it. He
does not accept a movement with *no* mobile at all. He argues that what moves is
not a determinate, identifiable object: the mobile is indeterminate, with no
identity outside the movement. As written, the spec gives the Gestalt
psychologist's claim to Merleau-Ponty. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: in the
Misconception, Zeno and the classical theory treat motion as a series of
positions of an identical object. The Read says Wertheimer concluded "movement
without a mobile", and that the chapter answers: there is always "something"
that moves, but it is not an object posited apart from the movement.

**13. Wertheimer's mirror is a sudden switch, not a halving series.** Location: course 5
lesson 4, preset `wertheimer` ("level shifting by halves: 45, 22½, 11¼ …").
Problem: as Merleau-Ponty reports it, after a few minutes "a sudden change"
occurs and the walls and the man walking become vertical. A decay by halves
makes a gradual process out of what the book stresses is abrupt. The abruptness
is part of his argument: the level is re-anchored as a whole by the body that
takes up the scene, and is not corrected bit by bit. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix:
give `wertheimer` a `share` of 1 at the moment of the change (45 then 0), or add
a `switch` option. Keep the halving series only as a labelled model for
Stratton's days.

**14. "Wholly" free leans Sartrean.** Location: course 7 lesson 7, Misconception ("it says we
are free in a situation, wholly, and never outside one"). Problem: "wholly" is
Sartre's word for freedom, not Merleau-Ponty's. The chapter's formula is that
there is "never determinism and never absolute choice" (Smith c. p. 453).
Freedom works *through* motives and the situation, and he says it "gears
itself" onto them. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: "The chapter rejects the arithmetic
of a share without returning to totality: there is never determinism and never
absolute choice; I am free not in spite of my motives but by means of them."
Quote only once checked.

**15. Sartre is mostly not named in the Freedom chapter.** Location: course 7 lesson 6, Read
("Sartre's absolute freedom, which the chapter states before criticising it").
Problem: the chapter sets out the view of absolute freedom largely without
naming Sartre in the body text. The identification with *L'Être et le néant*
comes from the reader and the secondary literature, and perhaps a note. §F.4
promises to state each opponent "in their own terms". Confidence:
CHECK-AGAINST-TEXT. Fix: "a theory of absolute freedom, recognisably Sartre's in
*Being and Nothingness* (the chapter mostly does not name him)". Adjust once the
page shows whether he is named.

**16. The Preface is sharper on science than the lesson says.** Location: course 1 lesson 1,
Misconception. Problem: the lesson is right that the Preface does not call
science false. But it is milder than the Preface, which calls scientific points
of view that make my existence a moment of the world "always both naïve and at
the same time dishonest", because they forget the perceived world they
presuppose. A reader of the lesson would underrate the claim. Confidence:
LIKELY. Fix: add one clause: science is second-order, and *naïve and dishonest
when it forgets that*, but not false.

**17. Constancy hypothesis: credit Köhler, and do not cast Weber's law as its refutation.**
Location: course 2 lesson 2; D.1 `stimulus/weber`, tile stConstancy ("`different`
whenever the stimuli differ"). Problem:
- "Constancy hypothesis" is Köhler's term (*Konstanzannahme*, 1913), which
  Merleau-Ponty adopts. The lesson should name its source, as §C requires.
- Merleau-Ponty does not discuss Weber's law in the Introduction.
- The hypothesis is not refuted by a threshold. A point-for-point theory can
  admit a just-noticeable difference.

Putting "constancy: different / Weber: not noticeable" side by side presents a
strawman as the hypothesis. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix:
- Name Köhler.
- Say that the Weber view is the lesson's addition.
- Either drop stConstancy, or describe it as "the stimuli differ", which is a
  fact about the stimulus and not a prediction.
- Better, compute what the hypothesis *does* forbid: the same local stimulus
  giving different percepts in two contexts. The Müller-Lyer preset already does
  this.

**18. Quotation wording does not match either translation.** Location: course 1 lessons 2–3,
course 2 lesson 2. Problem: the spec mixes the two translations.
- Smith: "The most important lesson **which** the reduction teaches us is the
  impossibility of a complete reduction."
- Landes (approximately): "The most important lesson of the reduction is the
  impossibility of a complete reduction."
- The spec's "the most important lesson the reduction teaches us" is neither.
- "The world is not what I think, but what I live through" is Smith. Landes
  ends "…but rather what I live".
- "Are neither equal nor unequal" is close to Landes. Smith reads "neither of
  equal nor of unequal length".

The spec marks these "wording checked", but they cannot have been checked
against a single edition. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: remove "(wording checked)"
until §E.2 names the edition, and keep these as pointers, as §C's own format
requires ("never a quotation").

**20. Two Descartes details are off.** Location: course 7 lesson 1. Problem: the Second Replies are cited
correctly (the cogito is not a syllogism but a "simple intuition of the mind").
But the major premise Descartes discusses there is the *general* "everything
which thinks is, or exists", which he says is learned from the particular case.
It is not "if I think, I exist". Separately, in the *Principles* (I.10) he
allows the general premise. Confidence: CERTAIN for the Replies; LIKELY for the
Principles. Fix: the `major` preset uses "whatever thinks exists; I think; so I
exist", and the prose says Descartes treated the premise as derived, not
presupposed.

**21. Kant used the same triangle proof.** Location: course 7 lesson 2, Misconception ("the old
dispute with Kant"). Problem: the spec is correct but leaves out the most
useful fact. Kant uses *exactly* this proof, drawing the parallel through a
vertex, as his example of construction in intuition (*Critique of Pure Reason*
A716–17/B744–45). Merleau-Ponty's reading competes with Kant's on Kant's own
example. Confidence: CERTAIN. Fix: name the Kant passage.

**22. The tacit cogito is disputed by Merleau-Ponty himself.** Location: course 7 lesson 3 and
§F.4. Problem: the lesson should say what the dispute is. In the working notes
of *The Visible and the Invisible*, Merleau-Ponty himself wrote that the tacit
cogito is "impossible". Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: one sentence in the `note`.

**23. The double-sensation reading is right; say so explicitly.** Location: course 3 lesson 4,
Misconception. Problem: none of substance. Its reading (the roles alternate and
never coincide) is right for this book. Readers who know the later "reversibility"
of *The Visible and the Invisible* will assume the opposite. Confidence:
LIKELY. Fix: the `note` says that the late work develops this differently.

**24. The Introduction's cases should not be coded so that one opponent wins.** Location: course 2
lesson 5, Worked ("empiricism absorbs two, intellectualism three, the split is
the moon"). Problem: the Introduction's thesis is that the two accounts fail
*symmetrically*, from one shared prejudice in favour of the objective world. A
coding in which intellectualism does better, with a "splitting case", tells the
reader the opposite of the chapter. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: code the cells so
that each classical account fails at least one case for the same reason, and
make the lesson's output "no split on the root": both fail where they assume
the objective world. The phenomenal field is the only survivor. If the author's
coding really does give a split, the lesson must say that it is the coding and
not the book.

## 3. Missing material and the 41-lesson division

**25. Part One, Chapter 4 has no lesson.** Problem: once finding 1 is applied,
"The Synthesis of One's Own Body" has no lesson of its own, although §0 claims
the whole book. Its arguments:
- The body's unity is not that of an object or a law.
- The body is comparable to the work of art: "a nexus of living meanings".
- Intersensory unity of the body.
- Perceptual habit, the cane as an instrument of perception.

Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: add `the-body-as-a-work-of-art` (module "Part One ·
Chapter 4"). Lab: `variation`, a gesture done with the right hand, the left
hand, the foot and the teeth, in the spirit of the book's own observation that
a learned movement transfers across limbs. The core is the movement's form, not
any one limb's muscles. To keep 41 lessons, merge course 3 lessons 6–7, or move
habit into course 3 and let course 3 hold 8 lessons and course 4 hold 4.

**26. Temporality's main thesis has no lesson.** Problem: the two lessons cover the river and
Husserl's diagram. They do not cover the chapter's actual claim: time must be
understood "as the subject and the subject as time". This includes passive
synthesis, self-affection (Kant and Heidegger), and the priority of the present
and of the future. The book's argument from Part One leads here, and Freedom
depends on it. Confidence: CERTAIN that the thesis is there; LIKELY that it is
the chapter's culmination. Fix: add `time-as-subject` (module "Part Three ·
Chapter 2"). Lab: `time/diagram` with an `ekstasis` view that shows the whole
diagram re-centred at each present, so that no single column is "the" time. To
keep 41, fold `the-tacit-cogito` into `the-cogito-and-the-certainty-of-seeing`,
or merge the two Freedom lessons.

**27. Sensing's account of the subject of sensation is missing.** Location: course 5 lessons 2–3.
Problem: the chapter's central and most-cited claims are absent:
- Sensation is pre-personal and anonymous: "one perceives in me", not "I
  perceive".
- Sensing is a coexistence or reciprocity of sentient and sensible: "I am the
  sky itself" before the blue sky.
- The long critique of reflective analysis and the Kantian a priori on whether
  each sense has its own space.

Confidence: CERTAIN that the material is there. Fix: revise lesson 2's Read to
include "one perceives in me", or add a lesson `the-anonymous-subject-of-sensing`.

**28. The Space chapter's opening argument is missing.** Problem: the chapter opens by
rejecting Kant's space as a container or a form, and distinguishes "spatialised"
from "spatialising" space. That is the frame for the four lessons. Confidence:
LIKELY. Fix: one sentence in course 5 lesson 4's Read, or a short lesson if a
slot is freed.

**29. Sartre's look is missing from the chapter on others.** Location: course 6 lessons 6–7.
Problem: Part Two Ch. 4 answers Sartre's "look". The other's gaze turns me into
an object only if both of us withdraw into thinking natures. That is the
chapter's main engagement with a named opponent, and it is absent. Confidence:
LIKELY. Fix: add "the look that objectifies (Sartre), and when it does not" to
lesson 7's Read and as an observation in its `cases`.

**30. The Cogito chapter's analysis of feelings is missing.** Location: course 7 lessons 1–3. Problem:
the chapter has a long analysis of true and false feelings: false love, and
hysteria. The certainty of an inner state is not given in advance either. This
is the bridge from the certainty of seeing to the tacit cogito. Its other
topics, eternal truths and expression (Hegel, Valéry), are also uncovered.
Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: add "true and false love" to lesson 1's Read and as a
`cases` observation in lesson 3.

**31. Schneider's thinking is missing.** Location: course 3 lesson 6–7. Problem:
Chapter 3 also reports that Schneider cannot grasp analogies or a story's point
unless he reconstructs it explicitly. Merleau-Ponty uses this against the
intellectualist "symbolic function" reading (Cassirer, and Gelb and Goldstein's
own "categorial attitude"). Without it, the claim in course 3 lesson 7 that
intellectualism fails "the concrete case" is underdetermined. Confidence:
LIKELY. Fix: add the analogy and story tasks as observations, and name Cassirer
in the Misconception or the prerequisites.

**32. The sources list is missing several names.** Location: §A prerequisites 1, §0 "Where an
instance comes from", §F.4. Problem: these are absent:
- Heidegger: being-in-the-world, temporality.
- Binswanger: the aphonia case.
- Minkowski: lived space, "dark space".
- von Senden: the operated blind.
- Cassirer.
- Scheler: others.
- Köhler, Koffka and Ehrenfels.

Heidegger's absence is the most serious, given "being-in-the-world" in the
prerequisites. Confidence: CERTAIN that each is used in the book. Fix: add them

**33. The 41-lesson division is otherwise sound, but "one lesson per section" overstates it.**
Problem: the division mostly follows the book. The Preface and Introduction
match closely, and Part One Chs 1–3 and Part Two Chs 2–4 are well covered. The

apply findings 25–27 with the merges proposed there. The result stays at 41:
merge course 3 lessons 6–7 into one Schneider lesson; merge

raise the count to 43 and update the tagline counts.

## 4. Empirical figures in presets, and internal arithmetic

**34. The hat preset does not clear the door.** Location: course 4 lesson 1, preset `hat`.
Problem: 165 cm head + 30 cm feather = 195 cm, which is more than the 190 cm
door. The preset says "clears", but it does not. Confidence: CERTAIN. Fix: make
the door 200 cm (clears by 5) or the feather 20 cm (clears by 5). Also say in
prose that the lab does the comparison the book says the woman does *not* do
("without any calculation").

**35. The far depth preset gives a tenth, not a hundredth.** Location: course 5 lesson 5, preset `far`
("5 m and 6 m: disparity a hundredth"). Problem: near gives 6.5 × (1/50 − 1/60) =
6.5/300. Far gives 6.5 × (1/500 − 1/600) = 6.5/3000. That is one *tenth* of
near, not a hundredth. Disparity falls with the square of distance only for a
*fixed* depth interval. Confidence: CERTAIN. Fix: either say "a tenth", or make
far 5 m and 5.1 m (a fixed 10 cm interval: 6.5 × 10/(500·510) ≈ 1/98 of near).

**36. Face counts in the perspective presets are wrong.** Location: course 1 lesson 4; course 3
lesson 1; D.1 `perspective/faces`. Problem:
- With axis views, `three` (front, right, above) sees 3 faces, not 5.
- `two` (front, back) sees 2 faces, not 4.
- The Worked line ("front-right-above sees 3; union of three views 5") describes
  a different set of views from the preset.
- In course 3 lesson 1, a house seen from "street, garden, above, everywhere"
  never sees its underside, so "every face seen" is false.
- "Street sees front and roof" needs an elevated oblique view.
- D.1's example "cup ahead 1, left 2" conflicts with the Worked line of course 3
  lesson 5. Facing east, the cup at (2, 1) is ahead 2, left 1.

Confidence: CERTAIN. Fix: define each preset's views as obliques (e.g.
front-right-above and back-left-above give 5 of 6). For the house, say "every
face but the one it stands on", which is itself a good point about the ground.
Correct the D.1 example.

**37. One fraction is unreduced.** Location: course 6 lesson 1, `far`: Brunswik ratio
"235/255". Problem: the lab reduces exact fractions, so it will print 47/51,
and the spec should match. Confidence: CERTAIN. Fix: write 47/51.

**38. Moon figures: accurate.** 3,474 km diameter, 384,400 km mean distance,
zenith nearer by about one Earth radius (6,371 km), giving about 0.52°. The coin
preset (7 mm at 77 cm) is consistent with this, but is introduced as "a 1
cm-ish coin". Confidence: CERTAIN. Fix: say "a 7 mm disc", or a real 1 cm
object at 111 cm.

**39. Wertheimer's 30 ms and 200 ms.** These are the figures usually reported
for Wertheimer 1912: simultaneity below about 30 ms, optimal motion around
60 ms, succession above about 200 ms. Merleau-Ponty does not give them. They are
roughly right as reported, and the spec labels them correctly. Confidence:
LIKELY. Fix: the label says "as later textbooks report Wertheimer (1912); not
in Merleau-Ponty", and adds that the bands depend on distance and intensity
(Korte's laws).

**40. Weber fraction for lifted weights.** The spec's 1/50 (0.02) is the usual
modern textbook figure. Weber's own (1834) figure for *lifted* weights was about
1/40, and about 1/30 for weights resting on the skin. It is labelled
illustrative, which is acceptable. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: either use 1/40 and
attribute it to Weber, or keep 1/50 and attribute it to modern estimates.

**41. Stratton's duration.** Stratton ran two experiments: 1896 (about 21½ hours
over three days) and 1897 (87 hours over eight days). The lenses inverted the
field vertically and horizontally, so "180°" is right. They were worn on one
eye, with the other covered. "Eight days" is right for the second experiment.
Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: the label says "the 1897 experiment", and the prose
mentions the shorter first one. The day-by-day adaptation in the lab is
illustrative and should be labelled so. Stratton did not report a clean
re-inversion, and Merleau-Ponty reads his account selectively.

**42. Wertheimer's 45° mirror.** The 45° angle, "a few minutes", and the
re-righting are all as the book reports. See finding 13 on the switch being
sudden. Confidence: LIKELY.

**43. Gelb's experiment.** This is Gelb (1929): a black disc lit by a hidden
projector in a dim room looks white, and turns black the moment a piece of
white paper is put in the beam in front of it. The spec describes it correctly.
The reflectances (1/20 black, 4/5 white) are plausible but illustrative, and
should be labelled so. Confidence: LIKELY.

**44. One five-hundredth of the book is wrong.** Location: §E.3. Problem: the book runs
to roughly 200,000 to 250,000 words. 150 words is about 1/1,500 of it, not
1/500. Confidence: LIKELY. Fix: "well under a thousandth of the book per
lesson".

## 5. The modern asides (§F.1)

**45. Predictive coding is disputed ground.** Problem: "Merleau-Ponty would call the first
'intellectualism'" is stated as fact. It is a live dispute.
- One side reads predictive processing as Helmholtz's unconscious inference,
  which is the intellectualism of Introduction III.
- The other side argues that active-inference and enactive readings are close
  to Merleau-Ponty: Clark, Gallagher, Kirchhoff, Bruineberg & Rietveld, and
  Dreyfus's "maximal grip" (*prise*).
- Predictive coding also *rejects* the constancy hypothesis, as Merleau-Ponty
