# Reading Faster — accuracy review of the specification

Reviewed: `docs/reading/PLAN.md` (branch `feat/reading-subject`), before any
authoring. Scope: every §S citation, every research claim in §C, §F and §G,
every figure and formula in §C and §D, and the eye-model assumptions.
Method: web lookups where reachable (Schotter et al. 2014 press release, the
Benedetto et al. 2015 highlights, Carver 1997's summary of the gears, Wade and
Tatler 2009, Acklin and Papesh 2017's listing), otherwise the reviewer's
knowledge of the primary literature. Anything not confirmed is marked
"unverifiable — check". No file other than this one was changed.

## Verdict

The bibliography is in good shape: almost every author, year, venue and
volume is right, and the headline figures (Brysbaert's 238/260/183, Carver's
five gears, Rayner 1998's fixation, saccade, regression and span figures,
Hu and Nation's 98%, the Flesch and Flesch–Kincaid coefficients) are
correctly attributed. The exact arithmetic is almost all right. The problems
are in what the lessons would *say the research found*, and they are the kind
a pedagogy review would praise. Four must be fixed before authoring:
**(1)** Dunlosky et al. rated summarisation **low** utility, not moderate;
**(2)** Carver's position is close to the opposite of "the capacity to shift
gears is the mark of a flexible reader" — his first law is that readers hold a
**relatively constant rauding rate** and rarely shift, and the "flexible
reader" was the view he argued against; **(3)** Schotter, Tran and Rayner did
not prevent regressions (they masked words after the eyes left them), and the
comprehension cost was **not confined to ambiguous sentences**; **(4)** the
`choosing-a-target-pace` preset's best effective rate is at 240, not 288,
so the preset contradicts its own tile definition. Also high: comparing a raw
238 wpm against Carver's gears (which are in standard-length words) puts the
average reader in the "learning" gear, which misreads both sources; and the
`flash/span` drill measures a symmetric letter-identification span, not the
perceptual span the lesson's misconception describes (and its worked example
uses a position, +5, that a 9-letter string does not have).

## Findings on §C, §D, §F and §G

Priority: **H** must change before authoring; **M** should change; **L** tidy.

1. **H — §C 7.4 Misconception (`notes-in-your-own-words`).** "Dunlosky and
   colleagues rate highlighting low and summarising moderate" is wrong.
   *Correct:* Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated both highlighting and
   summarisation **low utility** (summarisation helps learners who are
   trained to do it well; untrained summaries help little). The lesson's
   contrast still works if stated honestly: "both rated low; summarising
   from memory, with the passage hidden, turns it into retrieval practice,
   which they rated high."

2. **H — §C 5.7 Misconception (`shifting-gears`)** and §C 6.2.
   "Carver called the capacity to shift gears the mark of a flexible reader"
   misattributes. *Correct:* Carver's first law of rauding theory is that
   readers attempt to comprehend at a **relatively constant rate** (their
   rauding rate) across ordinary material, and he argued *against* the
   traditional claim that good readers' rates are "fluid and flexible". He
   held that readers shift gears in response to difficulty, instructions
   and consequences — e.g. into the learning gear on hard text — but that
   most readers seldom do, and he recommended deliberate shifting as
   advice. Source: Carver 1990; Carver 1997, *Sci. Stud. Reading* 1(1).
   Rewrite: "Carver found that most readers stay in one gear, their rauding
   rate, whatever they read, and recommended shifting deliberately." Also
   "Skilled readers slow down for difficulty" is contested by the same
   source (finding 13).

3. **H — §C 1.4 (`typical-rates-and-reading-gears`) presets.** Units
   mismatch. Brysbaert's 238 is words per minute; Carver's gears are
   standard-length (6-character) words per minute for college students. The
   `median-reader` preset puts the average silent reader (238) in the
   **learning** gear, telling the lesson's reader that ordinary reading is
   studying. That misreads both sources: 238 is Brysbaert's estimate of
   normal (rauding) reading, and Brysbaert explicitly argues that Carver's
   300 is too high an estimate of it. *Fix:* either convert the reader's
   rate to standard words before comparing (the drill has `chars`), and
   state that Carver's figures are for college students on his materials;
   or present the gears as purposes with approximate rates and drop the
   "nearest gear" computation. In any case the panel must say the two
   references use different units.

4. **H — §C 3.2 (`choosing-a-target-pace`) preset `holds-at-120`.** 240 ×
   5/5 = 240; 288 × 4/5 = 230.4; 360 × 2/5 = 144. The highest effective rate
   is **240 at target 240**, not 288. §D defines `drBestTarget` as the run
   with the highest effective rate, so the tile would show 240 while the text
   says 288. The worked line "288 × 4/5 = 230.4 > 360 × 2/5 = 144" omits the
   winning run. *Fix:* either make the tile "fastest target with comprehension
   ≥ 4/5" (then 288 is right, and the lesson should say effective rate
   dropped slightly, 240 → 230.4, which is the honest cost of practising
   above comfort), or change the preset (e.g. 5/5 at 288 → 288 effective).
   `holds-at-150` has no numbers (bare id).

5. **H — §C 2.2 (`the-perceptual-span`).** Three problems.
   (a) The drill flashes random letter strings and scores letter
   identification by position; that measures the **visual span** (Legge et
   al.), which is roughly symmetric, not the **perceptual span in reading**
   (moving-window paradigm), whose 3–4 left / 14–15 right asymmetry comes
   from attention following the direction of reading (it reverses for
   Hebrew readers: Pollatsek, Bolozky, Well & Rayner 1981). The lesson must
   say the drill measures a related but different span, or the reader's
   symmetric ±3 will appear to contradict the cited 14–15.
   (b) "acuity, not habit, sets it" is imprecise: the span is set by acuity
   **and attention**; it is smaller in beginning readers and on hard text and
   somewhat larger in faster readers (Rayner 1998; Rayner, Slattery &
   Bélanger 2010, *Psychon. Bull. Rev.* 17). The defensible claim: no
   evidence that training widens it to a line or a page.
   (c) Worked example "3 of 10 at +5" — the `typical` preset uses 9-letter
   strings, whose positions run −4…+4. There is no +5. Use +4 (the preset's
   3/10) or lengthen the strings.

6. **H — §C 2.3 and 4.4 (Schotter, Tran & Rayner).** "When Schotter, Tran and
   Rayner prevented them, comprehension fell" — they did not prevent
   regressions; they replaced already-read words with x's (trailing mask),
   so regressions found no text. Comprehension fell. *Write:* "when Schotter,
   Tran and Rayner made going back useless — each word turned to x's as the
   eyes left it — comprehension fell". In 4.4, "Schotter, Tran and Rayner's
   ambiguous sentences are where that costs" is wrong: the cost appeared for
   unambiguous sentences as well as ambiguous ones. The garden-path question
   is still a fine drill, but the lesson must not say the cost is confined
   to such sentences. Also the kit's `mask` display hides whole lines above
   the pacer, a coarser analogue; the lesson should say so.

7. **M — §C 3.5 (`fewer-needless-regressions`).** The split into "needless"
   (inattention) and "repair" regressions is a teaching device, not a
   distinction Schotter et al. or Rayner et al. 2016 measured as such, and the
   shares (1/10, 1/20, 1/50) are invented. Label them illustrative. The
   "self-count" of urges to look back is not a measure of regressions.

8. **M — §C 4.2 (`silencing-the-inner-voice`).** "When inner speech is
   suppressed, comprehension of anything but easy text suffers" overstates.
   Hardyck & Petrinovich (EMG feedback) found a cost on difficult text and
   none on easy text; Slowiaczek & Clifton (articulatory suppression) found
   costs on passage comprehension. Evidence is from a few small studies and
   suppression also adds a dual-task load. *Write:* "In studies that
   suppressed inner speech, comprehension of difficult text fell; easy text
   held up." Also label which method the drill copies (articulatory
   suppression, as Slowiaczek & Clifton).

9. **M — §C 4.3 (`one-word-at-a-time`).** The misconception's mechanism is
   right (saccades are roughly 10% of reading time; in the model 30/255 ≈
   11.8%). But Potter (1984) showed single sentences can be understood in
   RSVP at ~12 words/s (~720 wpm); the costs show up for passages, for
   memory, and with no way back (Benedetto et al. 2015: literal
   comprehension down, visual fatigue up; Acklin & Papesh 2017: comprehension
   down at speed). State it that way so the reader's own RSVP drill, which
   may feel fine, is not contradicted.

10. **M — §C 5.3 (`what-skimming-costs`).** "Gist holds, detail goes" is too
    clean. Masson (1982) found skimming reduced comprehension of important
    as well as unimportant information, but less for important; Duggan &
    Payne (2009) found skimmers do better on important information than
    readers forced through the whole text at the same speed, still below
    full reading. *Write:* "gist suffers less than detail."

11. **M — §C 3.6 (`repeated-reading-and-transfer`).** "for a skilled adult its
    gains are largely specific to the passage" is not supported by the cited
    Therrien (2004), whose meta-analysis of school-age students found
    transfer gains to new passages (smaller than on the practised one). The
    adult claim is an inference. *Write:* "with school-age readers it also
    improves new passages, less than the practised one; for a skilled adult
    the drill's own new-passage run is the test."

12. **M — §C 6.3 (`unknown-words-and-coverage`).** Hu & Nation's participants
    were second-language learners reading one fiction text; the 98% figure is
    their estimate for most such readers to reach adequate unassisted
    comprehension, and at 95% "some, but most did not". Applying it to fluent
    native readers is an extrapolation, and the "threshold" is contested:
    Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010, *Reading in a Foreign Language*
    22(1)) give 95% minimal / 98% optimal; Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011,
    *Modern Language Journal* 95(1)) found a roughly linear relation with no
    threshold. *Write:* "In Hu and Nation's study of learners of English, most
    did not understand a text adequately at 95% coverage; they estimated about
    98% for unassisted reading." Also `txRun` = tokens / unknown is "one unknown
    in every N tokens", not "the run of known words" (that is N − 1).

13. **M — §C 6.2 (`your-rate-on-hard-text`).** The 290 → 170 preset (59%) is a
    large drop; Carver's constancy law predicts little rate change within
    the reader's level, with comprehension falling instead, and a large drop
    when material is hard enough to shift into the learning gear. Rayner's
    eye-movement data show longer fixations and more regressions on hard text.
    Both are compatible with the lesson if it says the drop depends on how
    far above the reader's level the hard text is. Do not present "one number
    per kind of text" as settled against Carver.

14. **M — §C 6.4 (`what-you-already-know`).** "Background knowledge is one of
    the strongest predictors of comprehension (Recht and Leslie's baseball
    study)" is defensible; the study was 64 junior-high students and measured
    recall/reenactment, not speed. "fast readers of a field are mostly readers
    who know the field" is unsourced; drop or flag as inference.

15. **M — §C 7.3 (`spacing-your-reviews`).** Cepeda et al. support spacing over
    massing (the misconception), not the **expanding** schedule the lab
    computes; expanding vs equal intervals is mixed (e.g. Karpicke &
    Roediger 2007, *JEP: LMC* 33(4), found equal intervals as good or better
    at a delay). The lesson should present expanding gaps as one reasonable
    schedule, not as what the meta-analysis found.

16. **M — §C 6.5 (`how-much-you-read`).** "the volume is itself what grows
    vocabulary and knowledge" is causal; Anderson, Wilson & Fielding is
    correlational (fifth graders' diaries). Nagy & Herman argue on estimates
    that incidental learning from context accounts for most vocabulary
    growth. *Write:* "is strongly associated with". "Ten more minutes adds
    more than any plausible rate gain": +10 min = +50% volume, equal to a rate
    gain from 250 to 375; defensible given the evidence, but say the
    comparison.

17. **M — §C 7.2 (`retrieval-beats-rereading`).** "Rereading wins on the
    immediate test and loses on the delayed one" is right for Roediger &
    Karpicke (5 min vs 2 days / 1 week). Add that their test condition was
    free recall without feedback, and that the drill's delayed test is
    multiple choice (recognition), which tends to show smaller testing
    effects. The presets are rightly labelled illustrative.

18. **L — §C 2.2, 150 ms flash.** Correct that 150 ms is shorter than a
    saccade's latency: Rayner 1998 gives ~175–200 ms (around 150–175 ms to
    program a saccade). State "about 175–200 ms" in the panel. Note that the
    browser may not deliver exactly 150 ms (the spec already prints the
    measured time).

19. **L — §C 2.1 Misconception.** "nearly all the reading happens in the
    stops" — correct (vision is suppressed during saccades; Rayner 1998).
    Javal: see the §S table.

20. **L — §C 2.3.** "Regressions are about 10–15% of saccades in skilled
    readers" — correct (Rayner 1998). The model's `reg` is a share of
    fixations; close enough, but name it consistently.

21. **L — §C 4.5 / §G.** Just, Carpenter & Masson as "the model of a test" is
    fair; any numbers taken from it need checking (§S table). Carver 1985's
    finding is a better source for "no one tested comprehended above ~600".

22. **L — §F exemplary paragraph.** "often by half again" is unsourced; it is a
    plausible description of the drill, so phrase it as "often by a lot" or
    tie it to the reader's own numbers. Also "Rayner and his colleagues' 2016
    review": Rayner died in January 2015; the review was published
    posthumously — harmless, but avoid implying he wrote it in 2016.

23. **L — §C 2.4 verdict thresholds.** With fix 225 + sac 30 and reg 0, the
    ≤ 8 threshold corresponds to ≈ 313.7 wpm, so any claim above ~314 wpm
    is labelled "beyond identification". Fast skilled readers manage
    350–400 wpm with good comprehension by shorter fixations and skipping.
    The verdict text should be "beyond what an average fixation identifies
    at these settings", and the panel should let `fix` change.

24. **L — §C 5.2.** "a limit of one third of its full reading time": the
    preset's 40 s is 2/7 of 140 s. Make it 46⅔ s or say "under a third".
    §P says gist-only passages have 4 questions, but rule 4 requires ≥ 2
    detail questions per passage — a gist-only set cannot meet it.

## Model assumptions

- **Characters per word = 6 (space included).** Defensible: it is Carver's
  standard-length word (six character spaces, letters plus space and
  punctuation). Running English prose averages about 4.5–5 letters per word,
  so 5.5–6 with the space; 6 is the convention, not a measurement. Say so.
- **The eye budget** (time/word = (cpw/step)/(1 − reg) × (fix + sac)) is a
  reasonable simple model and reproduces the average silent rate (≈ 267 wpm
  vs Brysbaert's 238–260). The more usual back-of-envelope form is "about
  four fixations a second, each moving ~1.2 words" (Rayner et al. 2016), which
  is the same idea. Two simplifications the panel should state: (a) each
  regression usually costs more than one non-advancing fixation (the eyes
  must also return), so the model *understates* the cost of regressions;
  (b) word skipping (~30% of words, mostly short function words) and
  refixations are folded into the average step. Treating regressions as
  non-advancing fixations is acceptable with that note.
- **Thresholds 8 and 15.** Consistent with Rayner 1998: word identification
  ~7–8 characters right of fixation; perceptual span ~14–15 right. Comparing
  them with the *advance per fixation* is a fair simplification (new text
  identified per fixation cannot much exceed the identification span), but the
  verdict depends on the fixed 255 ms (finding 23).
- **150 ms flash vs saccade latency.** Saccade latency ~175–200 ms (Rayner
  1998), so a 150 ms flash ends before an eye movement can land; correct.
- **Regression share.** Rayner gives 10–15% of saccades; `reg` 3/20 = 15% is
  at the top of the range. Fine; 1/8 would be mid-range.
- **Line-length model** (finding 2.5 row): define whether the first fixation
  on a line is the landing of the return sweep; the share is 1/5 or 1/6
  depending, and the spec currently uses both.
