# Second review: Merchant's Capital and Interest (Course 11)

Course `capital-merchants-and-interest`, package `content/capital/c11_interest/`,
Volume III, Chapters 16–24. Reviewed 2026-10-24 against the Kerr text
(`volume-3-kerr.txt`, ll. 2583–3595, read whole, and footnotes 58–83 and the
Preface where the lessons lean on them), following `docs/capital/SECOND-REVIEW.md`.
The findings in sections 1–5 were written before the pedagogy assessment was
opened; section 6 is written after it.

**Result.** No arithmetic errors: every figure in prose, worked lines, quiz
answers and `why`s recomputes exactly, and every preset's tiles match the
prose (section 4). The quotation checker reports 0 misses. Fourteen sentences
were changed, nearly all because they said a little more than the chapter
does. The two that mattered most are in “Interest-Bearing Capital” (Marx's
"irrational expression" is about calling interest the price of *capital*, and
the lesson gave it as a verdict on "the price of money", a phrase Marx in fact
explains in Chapter 22) and “Historical Facts about Merchant's Capital” (it
called interest-bearing capital "even older" than merchant's capital, which
Marx calls "the oldest free existence of capital").

Final preview line: `OK` (10 pages rendered; 9 pages executed and swept, 9
with pinned figures, 0 failing).

## 2. Critics and historians

Where I rely on my own knowledge rather than a source I could check here, I
say so; I changed nothing on that basis except Pitt's Act (finding 14), where
the text itself does not support the sentence.

- **Adam Smith** (Commercial Capital): counting wholesale and retail capital
  among productive employments is *Wealth of Nations* Book II, chapter 5. Right,
  from my knowledge. "Modern national accounts count a trade margin as value
  added": right (SNA measures distributive output by margins).
- **Pirenne, Sweezy, Dobb, Brenner** (Historical Facts): Pirenne's trade
  thesis in the 1920s (*Medieval Cities*, 1925), the Sweezy–Dobb exchange in
  the early 1950s on Dobb's *Studies* (1946), Brenner's agrarian class-relations
  account (1976): all accurate from my knowledge and stated at strength. The
  lesson says the dispute continues and does not take a side. Good.
- **Böhm-Bawerk and Fisher** (Interest-Bearing Capital): 1880s and 1930 are
  right; the description (time preference plus the productivity of roundabout
  production; interest under any social system) is a fair statement of both, in
  their own terms rather than Senior's. The lesson does not say Marx answers
  them. Good.
- **Wicksell** (The Rate of Interest): *Interest and Prices*, 1898; the natural
  rate as the rate equating loan demand and saving, near the expected yield on
  new capital, with the cumulative process. Accurate, and the Wicksellian reply
  is given at strength.
- **Schumpeter 1911, Knight 1921** (Interest and Profit of Enterprise):
  accurate. See finding 13 for the one sentence about what the chapter says.
- **Piketty 2013** (Interest-Bearing Capital as a Thing): r > g and shares,
  with wars, inflation and taxes as the historical breaks; accurate, and the
  lesson keeps his claim apart from Price's.
- **Roscher, Massie, Proudhon/Bastiat 1850, Child 1669, Price 1772, Aristotle,
  O'Connor 1859**: all as in the text.

Lesson verdicts in the lesson's own voice: none that endorse Marx after the
fixes above. The remaining characterisations ("Marx's dry summary",
"Marx does not romanticise the old merchant", "here Marx is pointed", "He
enjoys the shopkeeper's motto", "Abbreviated is the right word") describe
Marx's tone or approve a word, not a conclusion; I left them (recommendation
R5 below).

## 6. Recommendations (taste, not changed)

- **R1, Money-Dealing Capital.** The lab enters the dealer's capital with the
  merchant's, so the money-dealer's share reaches him through the last
  seller's price. Marx does not say who pays the dealer's charges. The `note`
  half-says this ("whoever holds the capital"); one more sentence in the body,
  that this routing is the lab's simplification, would close it.
- **R2, Commercial Profit.** "Who pays for the clerks, on this account?"
  answers with how the merchant profits by their unpaid labour. Marx states the
  b problem as an open "difficulty" (ll. 2789–2830) and works at it without
  finishing; the lesson could say that the chapter leaves it unresolved, which
  `not_covered` already implies.
- **R3, Commercial Profit.** The transformation problem is named in a
  one-sphere aggregate example where price of production and value coincide by
  construction (the lesson's own `note` says so). The objection is real but
  sits more naturally in the next sentence about Part II than as a qualification
  of 1,062 and 1,080.
- **R4, Interest-Bearing Capital as a Thing.** "a rate an economy can sustain
  for a long time" (1%) is the lesson's own empirical claim. "an illustrative
  rate" would be safer.
- **R5, voice.** The voice is good throughout: it reads the passage, goes back
  through it phrase by phrase, opens with an everyday scene only where the
  object is in the reader's life (a shop, a savings account, a mortgage), and
  keeps to one light remark. Nothing is performed or preachy. The small
  characterisations listed in section 2 could go if the editor wants the lessons
  more neutral, but none tells the reader what to conclude.
- **R6, PLAN §C, lesson 1.** The entry still reads "`merchant` (Marx's £3,000
  of linen, once a year: £3,000)"; the instance is unchanged, but "taken as one
  turnover a year" would match the label.

## 7. Where I disagree with the pedagogy assessment

(Written after reading `docs/pedagogy/capital-merchants-and-interest.md`.)

The assessment's arithmetic, tile readings, quiz audit and speech work agree
with mine (its 1,579,494s for Child's sum is right; my first note had a slip),
and I agree with its four kit-level remaining issues. Where we differ is, as in
Course 1, in passages it read as faithful:

1. **"Part V begins with a capital even older than the merchant's"**
   (Historical Facts). The assessment cites this sentence as evidence that
   "the seam at lesson 6 is Marx's". Marx calls merchant's capital "the oldest
   free existence of capital" (l. 3037) and interest-bearing capital "likewise
   a very old form" (l. 3119). Changed (finding 7).
2. **Pitt's sinking fund.** The assessment's own repair introduced "set aside
   £250,000 a year". The text gives the £250,000 without a period, and, from my
   knowledge, the Act's £1 million a year was paid in quarters of £250,000.
   Its reason for removing the date (1786 against Marx's 1788) was sound; the
   replacement figure was not. Changed (finding 14).
3. **Roscher.** The assessment's added clause made Roscher imagine prices
   changed by "a merchant's" prudence and humanity; the text does not name
   whose. Changed (finding 5).
4. **"Every 'Marx says' was traced to a line."** Two were not right: the
   merchant's advance as money "of his own" (Marx: "his own or borrowed",
   finding 1), and "the price of money" as the phrase Marx calls irrational
   (Marx: the price of *capital*; the money market's "price of money" he
   explains, finding 9).
5. **Schumpeter and Knight.** The assessment praised "the honest admission
   that ... it does not discuss risk or innovation". Half of that is too
   strong: the chapter names better methods and market smartness as causes of
   an individual profit's departure from the average and sets them aside.
   Refined (finding 13).
6. **The crisis preset.** The assessment lists "a breach of the ceiling
   treated as impossible" among the well-caught misconceptions without noting
   that the lesson said Marx "expects exactly this" in a crisis. He allows
   exceptional cases and puts the highest rates in crises, but does not say
   interest exceeded profit then. Reworded so the pairing is the lesson's
   (finding 10).
7. **The £3,000 "once a year".** The assessment accepts the label as Marx's
   figures; the once-a-year is the lesson's assumption (finding 2).
8. **Minor:** "10/11 of a yard for the price of one" (finding 4), "a page
   earlier" (finding 8), the joint-stock companies' "altogether" (finding 12),
   and "paid for no work at all" in the lesson's own voice (finding 11) were
   not raised.

I agree with its voice judgement: the course reads the passage, goes back
through it phrase by phrase, and meets the critics at strength without
having Marx answer them. Its repairs that I checked and agree with: the
sugar paragraph's pounds, "lent" to "put into" in Money-Dealing Capital, the
scare-quoted "should", the obscure quiz distractor, and the "mark-up"
key-line rewording.
