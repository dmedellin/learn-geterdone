## The check I was asked to make: "7 1/5" in Chapter 12

The first lesson's worked example says the book's "7 1/5 hours" is a slip for
7 1/2. Having read the whole example (Kerr l. 2625, pp. 349–350), I keep the
claim. Marx's own figures force it:

- the day's new labour is carried by 9 3/5 of the 24 articles, of which
  "6 articles represent the necessary labour-time": 6 / 9 3/5 = 5/8, and
  5/8 of 12 hours is 7 1/2;
- "The ratio of the necessary labour to the surplus-labour ... is now only
  5:3": 12 hours in the ratio 5:3 is 7 1/2 and 4 1/2;
- the new value is 8s, of which the wage is 5s: 5/8 of the day again;
- surplus value "grows from one, into three shillings": 3/8 of 12 hours is
  4 1/2 hours of surplus labour.

"7 1/5" and "2 4/5" agree with each other (12 − 7 1/5 = 4 4/5 = 2 + 2 4/5) but
with nothing else in the paragraph; 7 1/5 is three-fifths of 12, which is what
the 5:3 ratio gives if it is misread as a fraction of the day. A reader who
recomputes any of Marx's four figures gets 7 1/2. The claim is therefore one the
text itself forces, not a correction imposed on it. **Changed:** the worked
example's `after` now lays out those figures (articles, ratio, 8s, 1s to 3s) so
the reader sees the slip from Marx's own numbers instead of taking the lesson's
word for it. I did not say whether the slip is Marx's or the translator's.

## Findings and changes

### 1. The Concept of Relative Surplus Value (Chapter 12)

- **Worked example, the 7 1/5 note** — see above. Changed.
- Arithmetic otherwise all correct: 2/10 = 20%, 3/9 = 1/3, pioneer 24 × 10d =
  20s, 20s − 12s = 8s, 8s − 5s = 3s, extra at 1s = 6s = 12 social hours,
  general preset 5s to 3s gives 6 necessary hours and 100%. Quiz answers and
  every distractor check out.
- "Here c is fixed by law or custom at twelve": Marx says only that the day is
  "given". Harmless gloss, left.
- Schumpeter's date (1911) and the Quesnay attribution are accurate; Quesnay is
  "one of the founders of political economy" in the text, and the lesson's
  "founded the Physiocrats" is ordinary history. No change.

### 2. Co-operation (Chapter 13)

- **Misrepresentation (fixed).** "Marx's reply is that the managers who
  coordinate are themselves wage labourers", placed after Coase's 1937 theory
  of the firm, gave Marx a reply to a writer who came 54 years after his death,
  the AUTHORING rule's named failure. The content (supervision handed "to a
  special kind of wage labourer", Kerr l. 2703) is in the chapter, so I kept it
  but reframed: the chapter was written long before Coase, and what it says
  bears on the first half of the view. Quoted the phrase so it is checkable.
- Lab and quiz arithmetic correct (10s/12 = 10d; 120d/144 = 5/6 d; 720d/144 =
  5d; 1200d/1000 = 1 1/5 d; weaver 6d and 3d). The quiz distractor "1/2 d alone,
  1/4 d together" is correctly described as reading shillings as pence.
- Burke: the lesson does not repeat Marx's "sophist and sycophant"; fine.

### 3. Division of Labour and Manufacture (Chapter 14)

- **Fact (fixed).** `concepts_intro` said the chapter "runs from the late
  sixteenth century"; the text (l. 2733) and the lesson's own `def` say the
  middle of the sixteenth century. Changed to "the middle".
- **Lesson's own verdict (fixed).** "Marx's sharpest line is about the people
  who…" is the lesson ranking Marx's sentences. Changed to "Marx then turns to
  the people who…".
- Checked: carriage trades, the 20 needlemakers (Kerr says needles for what
  Smith called pins), 500 varieties of hammer, Babbage (fn. 50), "the hole"
  and its five members, Ferguson as "the master of Adam Smith" (l. 2837; fn.
  77 says Smith was his pupil), Smith's state education "in homœopathic
  doses", minimum capital rising (l. 2863), the machine-making workshop as
  manufacture's last product (l. 2905). All supported.
- Arithmetic: 4, 2, 1 (7); 2, 3, 5 (10) for 3,000/2,000/1,200; 2, 2, 1 (5);
  quiz 600/300/200 gives 1, 2, 3. Correct.
- **Recommendation.** The note calls Smith's figure "48,000 pins"; the Kerr
  text Marx quotes in Chapter 15 §8 calls them needles ("10 men ... over
  48,000 needles a-day"), and lesson 6 then speaks of "the needle machine that
  does the work of Smith's ten men". A half-sentence saying the translation has
  needles where Smith has pins would stop a careful reader tripping on it.

### 4. Machinery and the Value It Transfers (Chapter 15 §1–2)

- **Misrepresentation (fixed).** "This is why, Marx says, machines were
  invented in England to be used only in North America, where wages were
  higher." The text (l. 3023) gives the mechanism, that the difference
  between a machine's value and the value of the labour-power it replaces
  varies by country, and does not say "wages were higher". Rewritten to state
  Marx's mechanism. The quiz question on it ("Wages in North America were
  higher, so the machines saved more in wages") is still the one defensible
  answer, since it is the direct consequence of that mechanism; left.
- Arithmetic: £3000/10000 = £3/10, £5000/10000 = £1/2, £1200/6000 = £1/5,
  labour saved at 100% = £6000, tests and verdicts all match the tiles
  (including the society switch, which prints "adopted (society's test)").
- The `note` says "Marx notes that wear is not exactly proportional to use";
  the text (§3(b), l. 3075) says wear from use is "more or less directly
  proportional" and wear from idleness inversely so. Close enough; a
  recommendation to say "and a machine also rusts standing idle" would match
  the text better.
- The Baynes paragraph compresses two passages (horse-power per spindle, and
  the 27,000 hours) into one; both figures are right.

### 5. Machinery and the Intensity of Labour (Chapter 15 §3)

- **Fact (fixed).** "a legal limit, twelve hours from 1832" — the Act was
  1833, and Marx himself writes "between 1833 and 1847, when the day was 12
  hours long" (§3c). Changed to 1833.
- Arithmetic: 10 × 6/5 = 12, 8/(6/5) = 6 2/3, 4/(6/5) = 3 1/3, 9 × 4/3 = 12,
  12 × 4/3 = 16 against 8 necessary = 100%. Correct, and Marx's 6 2/3 and 3 1/3
  are reproduced.
- Gardner, Ashworth, "2 as out of 24", "compressed into less than 10 hours",
  Ure's two quotations, the Pindar, fines replacing the lash, the ribbon-loom,
  the Luddites, and the predicted further reduction of hours: all checked.
- The `note`'s "Marx says a more intense day wears labour-power out faster, so
  its value may rise" is Chapter 17, case II, not Chapter 15; true of the book,
  so left.

### 6. Machinery and the Displaced Worker (Chapter 15 §6–10)

- No misrepresentation found. The carpet factory, the £1,000 variant ("about
  16 ... nay, less than 16"), "at the best", the three components of the
  £1,500, the means of subsistence "liberated", "not capital, but
  commodities", the new-capital point, Bill Sykes, and the £500 / £1,500 /
  £2,000 example of §7 all match the text.
- Arithmetic: 50 displaced, set free 0 and 1500/5/30 = 10; £1,000 variant
  500/30 = 50/3, 1000/5/30 = 20/3, total 70/3; quiz £200, 400 against 1,200.
  Correct.
- The Ricardo 1821 chapter and the strength-first statement of the
  compensation theory are well done. "Economists have mostly answered that it
  has not" is a fair one-line summary of a data question the course says it
  does not carry.

### 7. Absolute and Relative Surplus Value (Chapter 16)

- **Misrepresentation (fixed).** "The other 10 needed, in Marx's words, 'a
  whole series of historical events' and compulsion." In the text the
  historical events are what the sago-cutter needs before he can use his
  leisure productively *for himself*; compulsion is what makes him work it for
  others. The lesson had merged them. Rewritten with both clauses quoted; the
  quiz `why` that repeated the merge now says the five days "need compulsion,
  which nature does not supply".
- **Embellishment (fixed).** "who fells a tree and has his bread for the week
  from it" is not in the text, which gives 12 working hours a week and "cut
  bread for themselves, just as with us they cut firewood". Replaced with the
  text's image.
- **Anachronism (fixed).** "Those who explain profit ... by the reward for
  waiting, would reply ... goods available sooner are worth more ... Marx's
  answer is that this explains nothing". That is Böhm-Bawerk's time preference
  (and later Fisher), and it is not Mill's durability point; and "Marx's
  answer" again answers a later critic. Rewritten: Böhm-Bawerk named, his
  theory stated in its own terms (roundabout production, present goods valued
  above future), the note that Marx died in 1883, and then what the chapter
  actually says to Mill: he "confounds the duration of labour-time with the
  duration of its products".
- **Wording (fixed).** Concept "Productive labour": "the same teaching done for
  oneself is not" read as teaching oneself. Now "done on one's own account".
- Arithmetic: 8/6, 7/5, 9/5, sago 10/2 = 500%, quiz 6/4 = 150%, Mill 20/500 =
  4%, distractors 20/400 = 5% and 100/400 = 25%. Correct.
- Quiz 4 (productive labourer) uses a household cook as a distractor; the
  chapter does not mention cooks, but on its definition the answer is
  unambiguous. Left.

### 8. Changes in the Price of Labour-Power and in Surplus Value (Chapter 17)

- **Misrepresentation (fixed).** "Ricardo ... made a larger mistake: he knew no
  change in length or intensity". Marx lists two errors and says the second,
  confusing the rate of surplus value with the rate of profit, "vitiates his
  analysis much more". The lesson had made the lesser one the larger. Body now
  gives both, in Marx's order of weight; mistake 2 now says "the first of the
  errors Marx finds in Ricardo".
- **Lesson's own verdict (fixed).** "His last sentence is the chapter's
  sharpest" → "The chapter's last sentence puts it in terms of class".
- Arithmetic: 8 to 6 hours, 50% to 100%, the quarter and the half, 6s to 8s
  and 3s to 4s each, intensity 16 normal hours on 6 = 5/3, length 8/6 = 4/3,
  case IV 4/8, 6/8, 8/8 and the third on 6 to 8 hours: all correct and all
  Marx's. (Kerr itself prints "the surplus-value at 6 shillings" for the
  14-hour day where it means 3s; the lesson rightly works in hours and does
  not repeat it.)
- Quiz: each distractor is wrong for the reason given; question 3's `why`
  correctly notes that a longer day also raises both, so "no change can raise
  both" is wrong.

### 9. Formulae for the Rate of Surplus Value (Chapter 18)

- **Overstatement (fixed).** "Every step that doubles or triples the first
  barely moves the second": the presets take the share from 50% to 66.67% to
  75%, which is not "barely". Now "As the first climbs without limit, the
  second creeps towards 100% and never reaches it."
- The account of the lost displays is careful and correct; Lavergne, the
  "aliquot part" argument, the reconversion 6/12 to 6/6, unpaid over paid
  labour, and the closing line on Smith all match the text.
- Arithmetic: 1/2, 2/3, 3/4, 11/12; d/(1 − d); quiz 400% and 4/5, 400% from a
  fifth. Correct.

## Voice (§F)

The course reads like someone who knows the chapters. The scenes it opens with
(a pay rise in an inflationary year; a quarter and three-quarters) are in the
reader's world and reach the category within the paragraph. Second person
stays out of steps, standards and quiz items. The lesson's own verdicts were the
only voice faults: "Marx's sharpest line", "the chapter's sharpest", both
fixed. "This is a serious argument, and it deserves its best form first" (lesson
6) and "his case deserves its full weight" (Ure, lesson 5) are the lesson
announcing its fairness rather than just being fair; I would cut both clauses
and let the strength-first paragraphs speak, but that is taste, so left.

## Where I disagree with the pedagogy assessment

(Added after reading `docs/pedagogy/capital-relative-surplus-value.md`.)

I read it after writing everything above. It is a careful document and its
arithmetic is right; it was also the pass that introduced the `7 1/2`
correction, the Babbage and Ure paragraphs and the value-of-money proviso in
lesson 8's quiz, all of which I checked and agree with. Where I differ:

1. **"Each get their case at strength before Marx answers."** The assessment
   lists Coase on the firm and Mill on durability among the critics handled
   well. Both paragraphs ended with "Marx's reply" or "Marx's answer" to a
   position that is later than Marx: Coase wrote in 1937, and the
   "goods available sooner are worth more" view the lesson attached to Mill is
   Böhm-Bawerk's time preference, not Mill's durability argument. That is the
   exact failure AUTHORING.md's Course 1 lessons name. Both are now reframed as
   what the chapter says that bears on the later view.

2. **"Ricardo on the three laws."** Praised as presented at strength. The
   lesson said Ricardo's "larger mistake" was knowing no change in length or
   intensity; Marx says the other error, confounding the rate of surplus value
   with the rate of profit, "vitiates his analysis much more". The lesson had
   Marx's ranking upside down. Fixed.

3. **"Fidelity to Chapters 12–18 is high."** I agree with the verdict but not
   with its completeness. It missed: the sago-cutter's "whole series of
   historical events" attached to the surplus labour when Marx attaches it to
   the cutter's own productive use of his leisure (compulsion is what the
   surplus needs); the embellished "fells a tree and has his bread for the
   week"; "where wages were higher", which the text does not say; the 1832
   date for the Twelve Hours' Act (1833, as Marx's own §3(c) has it); and
   "the late sixteenth century" against the text's "middle". Each was a
   sentence-level slip, not a misreading of an argument, which is consistent
   with its overall verdict.

4. **"The `7 1/5` ... is in the Kerr text, in the German, and in every English
   edition."** I confirmed it in the Kerr text only. The claim about the German
   and every English edition may well be true, but nothing in this repository
   checks it, and the lesson rightly makes no such claim. The correction stands
   on Marx's own figures in the paragraph, which is the stronger ground; I
   rewrote the lesson's `after` to show those figures (6 of `9 3/5` articles,
   5:3, 8s, 1s to 3s) rather than the arithmetic alone.

5. **Voice.** The assessment counts Ure's paragraph ("his case deserves its
   full weight") and the compensation paragraph ("This is a serious argument,
   and it deserves its best form first") as strength-first presentation. They
   are, but each opens by announcing its own fairness, which §F's "do not force
   it" would cut. Recommendation only. It also passes "Marx's sharpest line"
   and "the chapter's sharpest", which are the lesson's verdicts; I changed
   both.

6. **`formulae`: "`extreme` shows one racing while the other crawls."** The
   lesson's body said each step "barely moves" the share, but the presets take
   it from 50% to 66.67% to 75%. Softened to "creeps towards 100% and never
   reaches it"; the assessment's image is fair for `extreme`, not for the
   steps before it.

I agree with its remaining issues (the pioneer at full social value, the
uncomputed co-operative surplus, the `sago` label, "Sikes") and with its
judgment that no lab is decorative.
