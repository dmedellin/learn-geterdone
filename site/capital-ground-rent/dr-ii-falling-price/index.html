The second case in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Chapter 42 is the longest run of tables in the Part. Its argument is short: a falling price does not settle what happens to rent, and it shows why with Marx's own soils.

1

A new regulator

When the better soils' added doses supply the market without A, A's capital is withdrawn and B, the next soil up, sets the price. B now pays no rent, as A did before.

2

Two opposing movements

The regulating price falls, which lowers each surplus quarter's money value; the surplus quarters multiply, which raises it. Total rent moves with whichever is larger.

3

Grain rent and money rent

Rent counted in quarters of wheat and rent counted in money. With the price halved, a grain rent must double for the money rent to hold, and Marx's tables track both.

The material

Marx's comparison first, then the tables behind it

The passage is Marx's summary of the two tables he says matter most. The rest of the lesson builds them, and one that goes further.

Volume III, Chapter 42. “We find that the total money rental has remained the same, namely 18 pounds sterling, while the price of production has fallen by one-half, from 60 shillings to 30 shillings per quarter, and that the grain rent has been correspondingly duplicated, from 6 quarters to 12.”

Begin from the last lesson's Table II: every soil with two equal doses, A yielding 1 and 1, B 2 and 2, C 3 and 3, D 4 and 4, twenty quarters at 60s and a rent of 720s. Now suppose the market wants only 18 quarters. The two quarters A supplies are not needed; its capital is withdrawn, and the least productive dose still in use is one of B's, at 60s for 2 quarters, or 30s a quarter. B pays no rent; C and D pay their extra quarters at 30s. The a-drops-out preset, Marx's Table IV, gives the lab exactly that: all four soils and a market of 18 quarters. It prints 30s a quarter, rents of 0s, 0s, 60s and 120s, and a total of 180s, Marx's fall “from 36 pounds sterling to 9 pounds sterling”. A slip on the way: the Kerr text says that “D and its price of production of 30 shillings” would regulate. It is B: D's price of production is 15s, and B's is the 30s the rest of the chapter uses.

So the price halved and the rent fell by three-quarters. But that is because nothing was added to make up for A. Marx now lays more capital on the best soils, at the same productivity per dose. A third dose on D, his Table IV b, lifts the total to 240s. Two more on D, Table IV d, give D five doses and 20 quarters: D's rent is 300s, C's 60s, and the total is 360s, the “18 pounds sterling” of Table I, now at half the price and twice the grain. That is the iv-d preset, and the passage above is about it.

Marx does not stop there. If D took eleven doses, producing 44 quarters, “the total rental would once more reach the level of Table II”: 54 quarters in all, a money rent of 720s, at 30s a quarter. The rent-rises preset runs it; the lab takes six doses a soil at most, so D's eleven doses of 50s go in as one capital of 550s yielding 44 quarters, the same 15s a quarter. The table on page 817 of the Kerr edition prints the money rents the lab does, £3 on C and £33 on D; it gives C a grain rent of 3 quarters and a grain total of 25, where £3 at 30s is 2 quarters and the total 24, the doubling of Table II's 12 quarters that Marx's sentence after it describes. Compared with Table I, the price has halved and the money rent has doubled; compared with Table II, the price has halved and the money rent is the same.

Why does this work? Because each dose on D yields 4 quarters where B's yields 2, so each D dose makes 2 surplus quarters, worth 60s at the new price. Halve the price and you need twice the surplus quarters to keep the money rent; Marx puts it in two clauses: “The price of grain falls by 50%, the surplus product increases by 100%.” And the capital this takes is less, the more of it goes to the best soil: Table IV c reaches the same 360s by putting more of the extra capital on C, and needs 550s of capital where IV d needs 450s.

Definition · The falling-price case

Added capital on the better soils supplies the market without the worst soil, which drops out; the next soil sets a lower price. Total rent then depends on how many surplus quarters the added capital creates against how far the price falls.

Marx is ironic about the habit of counting rent in grain, “merely due to tradition”: one might as well show that a manufacturer buys more of his own yarn with £5 of profit than he used to with £10. The tables keep both counts, and the lab prints money, because money is what the lease is written in. The chapter goes on to the cases where the added doses diminish and where they improve. With diminishing doses, his Table V on page 820 drops A and gives B 2 and 1½ quarters, C 3 and 2, D 4 and 3½. It prices every quarter at B's average, £6 for 3½ quarters or 1 5/7 pounds, and reaches a rent of 9 3/7 pounds. The lab values each dose by itself, so it would let B's weaker second dose, at £2 a quarter, set the price instead; it does not reproduce Table V.

Engels, editing the volume after Marx's death, closed the section he wrote for the next chapter with a passage that reads like this one come true. American prairie, Argentine pampas and the grain of Russian and Indian peasants forced to sell for their taxes reached Europe by steamship and railway; a portion of Europe's soil “fell definitely out of the competition for the raising of grain”, and rents fell everywhere. That is the a-drops-out preset on a continent: a falling price, with nothing added on the better land to make up for it.

The standard objection to these tables is that they are arithmetic, and Marx says as much: his yields of 1, 2, 3 and 4 are chosen to make the sums easy, and Engels complains they are so far apart that they “lead to utterly forced results”. What the tables establish is a possibility, that the same falling price is compatible with rent falling, holding or rising. Which happened in a given country is a question of where the capital went, and the tables cannot answer it.

Here the price fell because the added doses were productive enough. The next lesson, “Differential Rent II at a Rising Price of Production”, takes doses so unproductive that the price must rise to call them forth.

Method

Computing rent when the price falls

Four steps, when added capital makes the worst soil unnecessary.

1

Rank the doses

Price every dose: capital and profit divided by its output. Order them from cheapest to dearest.

2

Supply the market

Take doses in that order until the market's quantity is reached. The last dose taken sets the new, lower price; doses not needed drop out.

3

Revalue every surplus

Value each dose in use at the new price, less its capital and profit. Soils that paid rent before may now pay none.

4

Compare the totals

Set the new total rent beside the old in money and in quarters: the price fell by a known proportion, and the surplus quarters changed by another.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking a falling price must lower total rentIt is the natural inference: cheaper corn, smaller surpluses in money. Marx's tables show the price falling from 60s to 30s with the total rent falling to 180s, holding at 360s and doubling to 720s, according to how much capital the better soils take. A falling price lowers what each surplus quarter is worth; the number of surplus quarters decides the rest.
2
Reading the Kerr text's &ldquo;D&rdquo; as the new regulatorThe text says D's price of production of 30s would regulate. D's is 15s; it is B, the worst soil left, whose 2 quarters for 60s give 30s. Everything that follows in the chapter uses B.
3
Thinking the soil dropped is lost to rent for goodA pays no rent here because it is not needed, not because it cannot be farmed. Let demand rise past what the better soils supply and A comes back as the regulator at 60s, with every rent revalued at that price.
