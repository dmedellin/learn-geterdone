Measuring Difficulty

A readability formula turns three counts into a score: words, sentences and syllables. Compute Flesch Reading Ease and the Flesch–Kincaid grade for the passages you have read, by the kit's counting rule, and see what the formulas measure and what they cannot see.

Two formulas from three counts

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

You have felt the difference between an easy and a hard passage. A readability formula puts a number on part of that difference, and only part.

1

Two counts stand in for difficulty

Both formulas use the average sentence length in words and the average word length in syllables. Long sentences and long words make a text score harder; nothing else enters.

2

Two scales, one pair of inputs

Flesch Reading Ease runs downward with difficulty: the higher the score, the easier the text. The Flesch–Kincaid grade runs upward and is read as a school grade. The same two averages drive both, so they almost always agree on which of two texts is harder.

3

A count, not a judgement

The formulas cannot see whether the ideas are familiar, whether one sentence depends on the last, or whether the words are common. Short words about unfamiliar ideas score easy.

Definition · Flesch Reading Ease and the Flesch–Kincaid grade

With W words, S sentences and Y syllables, Rudolf Flesch's 1948 Reading Ease is 206.835, less 1.015 times the words per sentence, less 84.6 times the syllables per word; the Flesch–Kincaid grade, derived by Kincaid and colleagues in 1975 for Navy enlisted personnel, is 0.39 times the words per sentence, plus 11.8 times the syllables per word, less 15.59. The key lines above give both in symbols.

The counts need rules, and the lab states its own. A word is a whitespace-separated token with a letter or a digit in it. A sentence ends at a full stop, a question mark or an exclamation mark followed by a space. Syllables are counted by a rule: the groups of vowels in a word, with y as a vowel, less one for a silent e at the end, and at least one a word. The rule miscounts some words, so every syllable figure is an approximation, and another program's counts will differ a little.

Work the example by hand first. A text of 100 words in 5 sentences with 140 syllables has 20 words a sentence and 1.4 syllables a word. The preset by-hand carries those counts.

W/S = 100 / 5 = 20 Y/W = 140 / 100 = 1.4 ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × 20 − 84.6 × 1.4 = 206.835 − 20.3 − 118.44 = 68.095 grade = 0.39 × 20 + 11.8 × 1.4 − 15.59 = 7.8 + 16.52 − 15.59 = 8.73

Now the passages. The preset octopus counts The Octopus: 395 words, 21 sentences and 504 syllables, so about 18.8 words a sentence and 1.28 syllables a word, for a reading ease of about 79.8 and a grade of about 6.8. The preset continents counts From Drifting Continents to Moving Plates: 391 words, 16 sentences, 775 syllables, about 24.4 words a sentence and 1.98 syllables a word, for an ease of about 14.3 and a grade of about 17.3. Between them is the preset church-clock, The Church Clock at Wexley, the first passage you timed in Measure Your Reading: 491 words, 21 sentences, an ease of about 70.2 and a grade of about 9.3.

Look at where the difference comes from. The sentences of the hard passage are only about a third longer than the easy one's, but its words carry more than half as many syllables again, and syllables a word are multiplied by 84.6. Most of the gap between 79.8 and 14.3 is word length.

The passages you read for time in this Subject were sorted into bands by exactly these counts: easy at grades 5 to 7, standard at 8 to 11, hard at 13 and above. That is the honest use of a formula: to sort many texts quickly by how long their sentences and words are.

The formulas cannot see anything else. They do not know whether a word is common or rare, so a short technical word counts as easy. They do not know whether you know the subject, and they cannot tell a passage of separate facts from one in which every sentence depends on the one before. A sentence of short words about an idea you have never met can score as easy as a nursery rhyme, and What You Already Know puts two passages of nearly the same grade side by side to measure exactly that.

The lab also counts your own text: open Count a text of your own, paste a few paragraphs of something you are reading this week, and compare its grade with the three passages.

Method

Scoring a text yourself

Four steps for any text you want to place, then the day's practice.

1

Count the three things

Words, sentences and syllables, by one rule kept the same for every text. Paste the text into the lab's counter, or count a 100-word sample by hand.

2

Compute the two averages

Words per sentence and syllables per word. Write them down: they are the whole of what the formulas know about the text.

3

Compute both scores

Reading Ease is 206.835, less 1.015 times the words per sentence, less 84.6 times the syllables per word; the grade is 0.39 times the words per sentence, plus 11.8 times the syllables per word, less 15.59. Keep one decimal.

4

Say what the score leaves out

Before you trust the number, ask whether the words are common, whether you know the topic, and whether each sentence leans on the last. The formula has seen none of that.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Open The Daily Drill and do today's session as its routine says. When the pool reaches its hard passages, find your hold target again: it will probably be lower, and that is this course's subject measured on your own practice.

Common mistakes

1
Taking a readability score for how hard a text is to understandThe score measures sentence length and syllables a word, nothing more. Short words about unfamiliar ideas score easy, and a passage in which every sentence depends on the last scores the same as a list of separate facts with the same counts. The score is a count; whether you understand the text is a measurement only a reading can make.
2
Reading the two scales the same way roundReading Ease falls as difficulty rises; the grade rises. The octopus passage has an ease of about 79.8 and a grade of about 6.8, the continents passage about 14.3 and 17.3. A high ease is an easy text and a high grade a hard one.
3
Trusting the second decimalSyllables are counted by a rule that gets some words wrong, and different programs count sentences differently. A grade of 9.3 against 9.4 is no difference at all; use the formulas to sort texts into broad bands.
