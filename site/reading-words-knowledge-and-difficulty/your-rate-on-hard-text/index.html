Your Rate on Hard Text

Does your rate stay the same when the text gets harder, or does your comprehension give way instead? Read an easy passage and a hard one at your natural pace, answer six questions on each, and compute the ratio of the two rates and both effective rates.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Almost every rate in your log so far was measured on standard text. This lesson asks what one of those rates tells you about a page that is much harder.

1

Two ways to meet a hard text

A reader can keep their pace and understand less, or slow down and keep their understanding. The rate alone cannot tell the two apart; the rate beside the comprehension score can.

2

The ratio of two rates

Divide the rate on the hard passage by the rate on the easy one. A ratio near 1 says the pace held; a ratio well below 1 says it fell. Then look at the two effective rates, which count only what was understood.

3

A rate belongs to a kind of text

A figure measured on easy text is a measurement on easy text. Before you accept a speed claim, ask what it was read on and whether the questions were hard enough to catch a reader who kept the pace and lost the sense.

The material

What a hard text does to a rate

Two passages of almost the same length, one written for a young reader and one in the dense style of a geology textbook.

Do the drill below before you read on. Read The Octopus at the pace you would use if no one were timing you, answer its six questions, then do the same with From Drifting Continents to Moving Plates. The lab alternates which passage comes first, so follow the order it gives you. Both readings go into your log as tests, each with its passage's band, easy and hard; the medians that later lessons set against your baselines count only tests of standard passages, so these two stand beside your curve without bending it.

Now put your two readings side by side and look at two things, not one: the rates, and the scores. The passages are close in length, 395 words and 391, and far apart in difficulty. By the formula the next lesson teaches, The Octopus is written at about grade 6.8 and the continents passage at about grade 17.3: long sentences, long words, and one idea leaning on the last.

Some readers slow down a long way on the hard passage and keep their score. The preset big-drop is such a reader: 395 words in 79 seconds on the easy passage, 300 words a minute with 6 of 6 right, then 391 words in 138 seconds on the hard one, 170 words a minute with 5 of 6. The ratio of the rates is 170/300 = 17/30, about 56.7%, and the effective rate fell from 300 to about 141.7.

easy: 395 × 60 / 79 = 300 words a minute, 6/6 hard: 391 × 60 / 138 = 170 words a minute, 5/6 rate ratio = 170 / 300 = 17/30 ≈ 56.7% hard effective rate = 170 × 5/6 ≈ 141.7

Other readers barely change pace and pay for it in the questions. The preset held-rate read the easy passage at 250 words a minute with 6 of 6 and the hard one at 240 with 3 of 6. The rate ratio is 24/25 = 96%, which looks like a reader untroubled by difficulty, until you see the effective rates: 250 on the easy passage and 120 on the hard one. Half of the hard passage went by without being understood, at nearly full speed.

The second pattern is the one Ronald Carver described. In his 1990 review of reading rate he found that readers tend to keep a nearly constant rate, which he called the rauding rate, across texts within their level, and that when the text gets harder it is their comprehension that falls. On text well above their level, readers drop into a slower gear, which he called learning, at about 200 standard-length words a minute for the college students he studied. So how far your rate drops depends on how far above your level the hard text is.

The eye-movement studies show the same difficulty from the inside. Rayner's 1998 review reports that on difficult text fixations get longer and regressions more frequent: each glance costs more time, and more of them go backwards. You met both costs in How the Eyes Read; a hard passage raises both at once.

This is a comparison of two readings, and two readings differ in more than difficulty. The passages differ in topic as well as grade, and whichever you read second you read a few minutes more practised or more tired. One question is about 16.7 points, so a one-question difference in the scores is not a result. If your readings go the other way from Carver's account, you have not refuted his review, and his review has not refuted your readings: it describes readers on average, and yours is one pair on one day.

What this measurement settles is narrower and more useful: a rate is a rate on a kind of text. A figure measured on easy text says little about how fast you can read a contract, a paper or a manual with the sense intact, and a speed claim should be measured on the kind of text you need to read, with questions that would catch a held rate and a lost meaning.

The next lesson, Measuring Difficulty, counts the two passages you just read the way a readability formula does, and shows what the count can and cannot see.

Method

Measuring a hard text again

On another day, with a hard text of your own choosing and an easy one, the protocol is the same. The third step is the one that is easy to skip.

1

Choose two texts of the same length

One easy and one hard, both new to you, both 350 to 500 words. Count the words, or paste each into the counter in Measuring Difficulty.

2

Read each once at your natural pace

Not as fast as you can: at the pace you would use if no one were timing you. Time each reading, and note which came first.

3

Test your understanding before you compute

Write three sentences on each text with the page out of sight, or have someone ask you questions. Without a score, a held rate and a lost meaning look the same.

4

Compute the ratio and both effective rates

Hard rate over easy rate, then each rate times its score. Say in a sentence which pattern you showed: slower and understood, or same pace and less understood.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Open The Daily Drill and do today's session as its routine says. When the pool reaches its hard passages, find your hold target again: it will probably be lower, and that is this course's subject measured on your own practice.

The big-drop reader

A reader reads The Octopus, 395 words, in 79 seconds with 6 of 6 right, and From Drifting Continents to Moving Plates, 391 words, in 138 seconds with 5 of 6.

easy rate = 395 × 60 / 79 = 300 words a minute hard rate = 391 × 60 / 138 = 170 words a minute ratio = 170 / 300 = 17/30 ≈ 56.7% effective = 300 × 1 = 300; 170 × 5/6 ≈ 141.7

This reader slowed to a little over half their easy rate and kept most of the meaning. The held-rate preset shows the other pattern: a ratio of 96% and an effective rate that halved.

Common mistakes

1
Treating your reading speed as one numberA rate measured on one kind of text is a measurement on that kind of text. Carver found that readers keep a nearly constant rate across texts within their level and lose comprehension instead, and that on text well above it they drop into a slower gear; the eye-movement studies show longer fixations and more regressions on hard text. Either way, the big-drop and held-rate readers would each misreport themselves with a single figure.
2
Reading the rate ratio without the scoresA ratio of 96% looks like a reader whom difficulty does not touch. The held-rate preset has that ratio and an effective rate that fell from 250 to 120. Compare the two effective rates before you say what the hard text cost.
3
Taking one pair of readings as the verdictThe two passages differ in topic as well as difficulty, and one was read second. A one-question difference is about 16.7 points and is not a result; repeat the pair on other texts before you trust the pattern.
