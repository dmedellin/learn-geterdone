Triaging a Reading List

Reading a whole pile of eight documents at 250 words a minute takes an hour, and the budget is 45 minutes. Choose read, skim or skip for each document, and the lab computes the time the plan takes and the share of the pile's value it covers, under a stated model of what a skim keeps.

The triage model

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

A reading list is a scheduling problem before it is a reading problem. The lab states a simple model of it and computes what each plan costs.

1

Value for a purpose

Each document carries a value, a judgement of how much it bears on what you are reading for. The values are stated, not computed, and choosing them is half of triage.

2

Three choices and their price

A document read costs its words over your reading rate and yields its whole value. One skimmed costs its words over a skimming rate and yields a stated share of its value. One skipped costs and yields nothing.

3

Fit, then coverage

A plan first has to fit the budget. Among the plans that fit, the better one covers more of the value, and reading in the order the pile arrived is a plan too, usually a poor one.

The material

Eight documents and 45 minutes

A warm-up reading first, then a plan for the pile.

Start with the warm-up at the top of the lab: a timed reading with six questions, logged as practice, so that this lesson, which has no drill of its own, still has a reading in it. Then turn to the pile.

The pile is for a stated purpose: tomorrow you help decide whether your team moves to a new building. Eight documents arrived, in the order the lab lists them, 15,000 words in all. Each has a value for that decision, from 1 for the neighbourhood newsletter to 10 for the landlord's lease proposal, 36 in all. The read, skim and skip buttons beside each document rewrite the plan, and the tiles recompute.

The model is plain. A document read costs its words over a reading rate of 250 words a minute. One skimmed costs its words over a skimming rate of 750 and is credited with half its value. One skipped costs nothing. The half is an assumption in the spirit of What Skimming Costs, where a skim kept the gist better than the detail, and you can change it in its box.

read everything 15,000 / 250 = 60 min budget 45 in arrival order 10,800 / 250 = 43.2 min 23/36 of the value triaged 7,500 / 250 + 7,500 / 750 = 40 min 32/36

The read-everything preset is the plan most piles get: 60 minutes, 15 over the budget, so in practice the last documents are never reached. The skip-wrong preset is what that looks like: read in the order they came until the time runs out, which gets through the staff survey and stops, 43.2 minutes with 1.8 to spare, and covers 23 of the 36, about 63.9%, without ever opening the facilities manager's summary, the second most valuable document in the pile. The skim-the-rest preset reads the four documents worth 4 or more, skims the other four, and takes 40 minutes with 5 to spare, covering 32/36 = 8/9, about 88.9%.

Now compare that with reading faster. To read the whole pile in 45 minutes you would need 15,000 / 45 ≈ 333.3 words a minute, a third faster than 250, with the same understanding, and no lesson in this Subject promises that. The triaged plan came in under the budget with nearly nine-tenths of the value at the rate you already have. Skip the four low documents instead of skimming them and it takes 30 minutes and covers 28/36 = 7/9, about 77.8%.

Duggan and Payne (2009) studied readers with more text than time, enough for half of each text: those who skimmed all of it remembered more of its important ideas than those who read half of it, because they chose where to look, and the advantage depended on how easily they could move around the text. A reading list asks for the same choice one level up, between documents instead of within one.

The plan is only as good as its values. If the parking study matters more to your team than it does here, change its value and the plan; the lab recomputes. In this pile the time was saved by the documents not read in full, not by the speed at which the others were read.

Triage decides how much of each text to read. Shifting Gears, the last lesson of the course, asks whether you change speed within a text when its difficulty changes.

Method

Triaging your own pile

Five steps, and the reading starts only at the fourth.

1

Write the purpose

One sentence: what the reading is for, and when it is needed.

2

List and value the pile

Each document with its rough length and a value from 1 to 10 for that purpose. Do this before you read any of them.

3

Read, skim or skip

Read the highest values, skim the middle and skip the lowest, and add up the time at your own rates until the plan fits the budget with a little to spare.

4

Read in order of value

Work through the plan from the most valuable down, so that if the time runs out, it is the least valuable that goes.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Finish with today's reading from The Daily Drill.

The skim-the-rest preset

Read the lease proposal, the staff survey, the facilities summary and the removal quote; skim the other four.

read 3,000 + 2,400 + 1,200 + 900 = 7,500 words 7,500 / 250 = 30 min skim 1,800 + 1,500 + 2,100 + 2,100 = 7,500 words 7,500 / 750 = 10 min value 10 + 6 + 8 + 4 + (1 + 3 + 3 + 1) / 2 = 32 32 / 36 = 8/9 ≈ 88.9%, in 40 of 45 min

Half the words were read and half skimmed, and the skimmed half carried only 8 of the 36 points of value. That asymmetry, not a faster rate, is what got the pile under the budget.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the fastest way through a pile is to read fasterReading the whole pile in 45 minutes would need about 333.3 words a minute, a third faster than 250. Reading four documents and skimming four fits at 250 with 5 minutes to spare and 8/9 of the value. Choosing what not to read saved more time here than a rate gain you could count on.
2
Working through a pile in the order it arrivedThe order of arrival is nobody's judgement of value. In the skip-wrong preset it put the newsletter first and left the facilities manager's summary, worth 8, unread.
3
Forgetting that the values are yoursThe lab computes the plan from the values you state, and a wrong value produces a confident wrong plan. Value the pile for the purpose before you open any of it.

✓
Finish when you can plan a pile to fit a budget and beat reading it in order.

Given a stack with word counts and values, a budget, and reading and skimming rates, produce a plan that fits the budget, compute its time and the share of the value it covers, and show that it covers more than reading the stack in the order given.

Note: The model is stated, not measured: a skim's share of the value is set at one half, the values are judgements, and a real document's value is not spread evenly through it. The warm-up is a practice reading and fills none of the plan's boxes.
