Scanning for a Target

Scanning is a search for something whose form you already know: a time, a price, a name. Time yourself finding five targets in a 613-word timetable, then compute the seconds per target found and the words passed over per second, and see why neither figure is a reading rate.

Scanning in four lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

You already scan every day, in a timetable, a menu, a contract. The drill times it, so that you can see what kind of speed it is.

1

A known form

You scan when you know what the answer will look like before you see it: a time with a colon in it, a price after a pound sign, a name with a capital. The eye matches shapes, and it can pass over everything that does not have that shape.

2

Two figures, two questions

Seconds per target found says what each answer cost you. Words passed over per second says how much text went by while you looked. Neither says how much of the text you understood, because you were not trying to understand it.

3

Layout does half the work

A timetable with a heading for each route and the times in a row is built to be scanned. The same facts buried in prose take longer to find, and the headings are the first thing a good scan uses.

The material

What a scan measures

The drill comes first in the lab below; this is what its two figures mean, and what they do not.

The drill is the summer timetable of a ferry company: 613 words of sailing times, fares, check-in rules and exceptions, with prose between the tables. Press Start and a prompt asks for one thing, such as the first Sunday sailing. Click the word that answers it, or Tab to it and press Enter; a wrong click is counted and you keep looking. If you cannot find a target, press Skip this one. Five prompts, one clock, and the result is logged as a scan test: it never enters your rate curve, because a scan is not a reading, but The Full Battery at the end of the Subject reads your first scan and your latest. On a phone, scrolling the page is part of the search, and the clock includes it.

When the fifth prompt is answered or skipped, the panel fills with two figures. Seconds per target found is the time divided by the targets you found, not by the targets you were asked for: the time you spent hunting for a target you never found was still spent, and it belongs to the answers you did get. Words passed over per second is the length of the text divided by the time.

seconds per target = 40 / 5 = 8 s words passed over = 613 / 40 ≈ 15.3 a second in a minute = 613 × 60 / 40 = 919.5 words

Those are the figures of the quick preset, a reader who found all five in 40 seconds: 8 seconds a target, and about 15.3 words a second passed over, which is 919.5 words a minute. The slow preset found four in 95 seconds, with one wrong click and one target skipped: 95 / 4 = 23.75 seconds per target found, and about 6.5 words a second.

Nine hundred and twenty words a minute is nearly four times Brysbaert's (2019) estimate of 238 words a minute for silent reading of non-fiction, and it is not a reading rate at all. Nobody who scanned that timetable for five targets could tell you what the other six hundred words said. Carver (1990) counted scanning as the fastest of five gears, at about 600 standard-length words a minute for the college students he tested, against about 300 for ordinary reading; what sets it apart is its goal, finding a target rather than understanding the passage. A scan can go that fast because it is not reading for meaning.

What makes one search faster than another is mostly where you look. Read the headings first and decide which section a target lives in. Hold the form of the answer in mind, a colon for a time and a pound sign for a fare, and let the eyes jump from block to block instead of travelling along the lines. When a target sits inside a paragraph, as the vessel out of service for inspection does, look for its shape first and read only the sentence around it.

The figures belong to this text and these five targets. A search of a page you have just searched is quicker because you remember where things are, so only your first run is a measurement of scanning; a second run on the same timetable measures your memory of it, so a second visit to this page serves a different document, a museum's visitor guide with five targets of its own. Compare a scan with a scan of a similar document, never with a reading.

Scanning finds a thing you can name. When you cannot name it, when you want to know what a text is about, you skim, and Skimming for Gist times that next.

Method

Timing a scan of your own

Five steps, on a document you need something from.

1

Name five targets first

On a document of your own, such as a timetable, a manual or a contract, write down five things to find, and next to each the form its answer will take: a time, a sum, a name, a date.

2

Survey the layout

Before the clock starts, look at the headings only and guess which section holds each target.

3

Time the search

Start a clock, find all five, and stop it. A target you gave up on counts as not found, and its time stays in.

4

Compute both figures

Seconds over targets found; words in the document over seconds. Compare them with your last scan of a similar document, not with a reading.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Scanning is not on the practice pool. Do today's reading from The Daily Drill, at your hold target, as usual.

The quick preset, by hand

A reader found all five targets in the 613-word timetable in 40 seconds, with no wrong clicks.

targets found 5 of 5 per target found 40 / 5 = 8 s passed over 613 / 40 ≈ 15.3 words a second in a minute 613 × 60 / 40 = 919.5 words

Over nine hundred words a minute went past the reader's eyes, and they took in five answers from them, two times, a fare, a ship's name and a check-in rule, because nothing else was asked. That is what the figure measures: the speed of a search.

Common mistakes

1
Taking scanning for fast readingIn the quick preset 919.5 words a minute went past, and nothing in them was taken in except the five answers. Scanning is a search for a known form, a number or a name, and not reading for meaning; Carver (1990) counted it a gear of its own, whose goal is finding rather than understanding. Its speed says nothing about how fast you read.
2
Dividing by the targets askedSeconds per target is the time over the targets found. In the slow preset, 95 / 5 = 19 seconds would understate what each answer cost; 95 / 4 = 23.75 seconds is the true figure, because the hunt for the missing target was part of the time.
3
Reading line by line to find a target in proseWhen a target sits inside a paragraph, it is tempting to start reading every line. The form of the answer is still the guide: look for its shape, then read only the sentence that holds it.
