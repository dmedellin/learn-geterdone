The Fetishism of Commodities

Marx's word for how value looks is fetishism: a relation between people's labours takes the form of a relation between products. Compute the hours behind each product in Robinson Crusoe's ledger, in a village that trades the same goods, and in an association that shares its product by hours, and compare: the ratios agree, and only some of the settings show whose hours they are.

Fetishism in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Section 4 is the most quoted part of the chapter and the one least like the others. It does no new arithmetic. It explains why the arithmetic of the first three sections is hidden from the people who live by it.

1

The fetish

Where producers work privately and meet only by exchanging products, the social character of their labour shows only in the products' values. A relation between people appears as a relation between things.

2

Not an error of perception

The appearance is not a mistake that knowledge removes. Even after the scientific discovery that values are congealed labour, Marx says, the appearance stays as real as the air did after its gases were named.

3

Other forms of production

Robinson on his island, the medieval manor, the peasant family and an association of free producers all reckon labour-time, and in none of them is it disguised as a property of things.

The material

A trivial thing, and Robinson's ledger

The section opens with the commodity's oddity and closes the argument with a man alone on an island keeping accounts.

Volume I, Chapter 1, §4. “A commodity appears, at first sight, a very trivial thing, and easily understood. Its analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very queer thing, abounding in metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties. … There it is a definite social relation between men, that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things.”

You know the feeling this section describes even if you have never named it. A price seems to belong to the thing. A coffee costs what it costs, the way it weighs what it weighs, and the people who grew, shipped and roasted it are nowhere in the number. Marx's claim is that the number is in fact a relation between all of those people's labour, and that in a society of commodity producers it can only appear as a fact about coffee.

Go through the passage. A table made of wood is “that common, every-day thing, wood”, until it is sold, and then it “stands on its head” and evolves notions “far more wonderful than ‘table-turning’ ever was”. That is a joke at the expense of the séances fashionable when Marx wrote, and it is also the argument. The mystery does not come from use-value, or from the plain facts that labour is the use of brain and muscle and takes time. It comes from the form: the equality of labours shows as the equality of products' values, and “the mutual relations of the producers” show as “a social relation between the products”.

Why should that be? Because the producers work “independently of each other” and “do not come into social contact with each other until they exchange their products”. Nobody decides how much weaving society needs. Weavers find out from prices. So the proportions of social labour are set behind everyone's back, and to each producer they arrive as the movement of things, “which rule the producers instead of being ruled by them”. Marx calls this the fetishism of commodities, after the religious objects to which people ascribe powers that are really their own.

To show that the hours are there all along, Marx goes somewhere they are not hidden, to Robinson Crusoe, a favourite of the economists of his day:

Volume I, Chapter 1, §4. “His stock-book contains a list of the objects of utility that belong to him, of the operations necessary for their production; and lastly; of the labour time that definite quantities of those objects have, on an average, cost him. All the relations between Robinson and the objects that form this wealth of his own creation, are here so simple and clear as to be intelligible without exertion, even to Mr. Sedley Taylor.”

Sedley Taylor was the Cambridge fellow who, months after Marx's death, accused him in print of falsifying a quotation from Gladstone; Engels tells the story in his preface. So the name cannot be Marx's own: the English edition of 1887, which Engels edited, put Taylor where the German had named a German economist, Max Wirth. The jibe is the closest the section comes to a smile. The point is serious. Robinson tames goats, fishes, makes furniture and tools, and knows that all of it is his own labour in different modes. His ledger states the hours outright. Those relations, Marx says, “contain all that is essential to the determination of value”, and none of it is mysterious.

The lab gives Robinson a week of 40 hours, with illustrative figures since Marx gives none. Ten hours tame 2 goats, 15 hours catch 30 fish, 12 hours make 2 pieces of furniture and 3 hours make a tool: 5, ½, 6 and 3 hours a unit. The village preset gives each product to a different household, 30 hours each, trading with one another. The hours per unit are identical, so the exchange ratios are too: a goat for 10 fish. But in the village nobody reads the hours. They see that a goat fetches 10 fish, and the ratio looks like a fact about goats.

Marx's other settings all reckon labour-time openly. On the medieval manor “every serf knows” what he spends in his lord's service. A peasant family divides its work by age, sex and season. And in an association of free individuals working with common means of production, Marx supposes “merely for the sake of a parallel” that each member's share of the means of consumption is fixed by labour-time. The association preset is that community's whole output, 160 hours. The lab can show its hours per unit; it cannot show the shares, which the prose has to carry.

A marginal-utility economist would reject the frame, and at full strength. For Jevons or Menger the goat-for-ten-fish ratio is not a disguise for hours; it reflects what one more goat and one more fish are worth to the people trading, and nothing lies behind it to be uncovered. Others take Marx's idea and widen it: Georg Lukács made it the centre of his reading in 1923. This Subject computes the hours and leaves you to judge whether they are what the ratio is hiding.

So far commodities have done everything: expressed values, chosen an equivalent, ruled their producers. The next lesson, “Exchange”, starts from the fact that they cannot walk to market by themselves.

Method

Reading a ledger of hours

Four steps, whether the ledger is one person's or a community's.

1

List the products

Write each product with the hours spent on it and the units it yielded in the period.

2

Divide

Hours per unit is hours spent divided by units made. This is the figure Robinson's stock-book records.

3

Compare two products

The ratio of their hours per unit is how many of one equal one of the other in labour-time. A goat at 5 hours equals 10 fish at ½ hour.

4

Ask who can read it

State whether, in the setting described, the producers see the hours or only the ratio. That is the difference the section is about, and no figure in the table shows it.

Robinson's stock-book

Robinson spends 10 hours taming 2 goats, 15 hours catching 30 fish, 12 hours making 2 pieces of furniture and 3 hours making a tool. You want his hours per unit, and what a goat is worth in fish.

total: 10 + 15 + 12 + 3 = 40 h goats 10/2 = 5 h, fish 15/30 = 1/2 h furniture 12/2 = 6 h, tools 3/1 = 3 h one goat: 5 / (1/2) = 10 fish

A village of four households, each spending 30 hours on one product, arrives at the same ratio, a goat for 10 fish. Robinson can say why. The villagers can only say what a goat fetches.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking value is a physical property of the thing, like weightMarx's own test: “So far no chemist has ever discovered exchange value either in a pearl or a diamond.” In the lab nothing about a goat differs between Robinson's island and the village, and the goat is worth 10 fish in both, because that figure is a ratio of hours of labour. Weight would stay with the goat if every person vanished; its value would not.
2
Reading fetishism as simple deceptionNobody is fooling the villagers. The appearance arises from how they produce, independently and for exchange, and it stays after it is explained, as the air stayed the same after its gases were discovered.
3
Taking the association as a description of a real societyMarx assumes labour-time shares “merely for the sake of a parallel” with commodity production, and says the mode of distribution would vary with the community's development. It is a contrast case, not a programme computed here.
