The second half of the chapter in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The second half of Chapter 6 takes the gesture as the model of the word, meets the classical objection that the gesture is natural and the word conventional, and answers it.

1

Natural sign and conventional sign

The classical division the chapter takes up: a natural sign, like a blush or a smile, would mean what it means because of how the body is built; a conventional sign, like the word table, means what it means by agreement, and could have been otherwise.

2

The speaking word

Smith's phrase for speech in which a meaning is coming into being for the first time: the child's first words, the writer's or philosopher's new thought.

3

The spoken word

Language as an acquired store of available meanings, the vocabulary and syntax a speaker uses as one might use a fortune already made, and on which new speaking draws.

The material

The passage first, then the second half of the chapter line by line

The passage is two sentences five pages apart: the chapter's claim about speech and the case that tests it. The lesson reads the gesture first, then the objection, then the reply, and ends where Part One ends.

Part One, Chapter 6, pp. 214 and 219. “The spoken word is a gesture, and its meaning, a world.” Five pages later: “The angry Japanese smiles, the westerner goes red and stamps his foot or else goes pale and hisses his words.” Read from page 214, where the paragraph on gestures begins, to the end of the chapter on page 232, in your own copy before going on; pages 221 to 225, on aphasia, were read in the last lesson.

Across a café you see two people at a table you cannot hear. One leans forward, the jaw set, a hand flat on the table, and you know before any word reaches you that this is anger. How do you know? The obvious answer is that you recall your own feelings when you made that gesture, and reason by analogy. The chapter reports modern psychology, Max Scheler's among it, against that answer; Scheler was a German philosopher who in the 1910s and 1920s wrote on sympathy and on how we know other minds. You know very little from inside of what your own face does in anger, so the analogy has nothing to work with, and you do not see anger as a fact hidden behind the gesture. “The gesture does not make me think of anger, it is anger itself.”

But the meaning of a gesture is not seen as the colour of the carpet is. If it were, the chapter says, it would be hard to see why we understand mostly human gestures, and not the sexual pantomime of a dog or a praying mantis, or even the emotions of people in circles very unlike our own. The sense of a gesture is not given but understood: taken up by an act of the spectator's. It is as if the other person's intention inhabited my body and mine his; the gesture outlines an object and invites me to join in, and communication happens when my conduct finds the same path. The chapter compares this to perceiving a fireplace, which I take as a whole at once and not as the sum of views I have checked against each other, and it calls the recognition blind: it comes before any intellectual working out, as people performed the caress for generations before a philosopher, Sartre in the footnote, gave its intellectual meaning.

The linguistic gesture, the chapter says, likewise outlines its own meaning, and then it states the objection at length. A gesture points to a relationship between a person and the perceptible world, and that world is given to the spectator along with the gesture; a word aims at a mental setting that is not given to everyone. The first reply is that what nature does not supply, culture does. Earlier acts of expression leave available meanings that make a common world between speakers, and new speech plays on this keyboard of acquired meanings as a gesture plays on the perceived world. But then where did the acquired meanings come from? The objection presses. In emotional expression one can see what the gesture and its meaning have in common: the smile and the relaxed face have in them the rhythm of joy. The word's link with its meaning looks accidental, as the existence of many languages shows. This, the chapter says, is what is commonly expressed by calling gesture a natural sign and the word a convention.

Definition · The classical division of signs

Emotional gesture is a natural sign, the same in every body because the body is built the same; the word is a conventional sign, which means what it does by agreement. So, on this division, speech cannot be gesture.

The chapter replies twice. First, conventions are a late form of relationship between people; they presuppose an earlier way of communicating, and language has to be put back into it. The chapter grants that, if you consider only a word's conceptual meaning, the verbal form, endings apart, looks arbitrary. Taken with its gestural sense, which is everything in poetry, it does not. Words and phonemes, the sounds a language is built from, are so many ways of singing the world, not because they imitate things, as the naive theory of onomatopoeia held, but because they draw out their emotional essence. If the accidents of phonetics and borrowing could be set aside, the chapter suggests, with a probably, a language's original form would show a system not wholly arbitrary, in which calling night nuit makes it fitting to call light lumière. That is why the full meaning of one language never passes into another, and why one of the languages we speak is always the one we live in.

Second, and here the passage's case comes in, the chapter will not grant the other half of the division either. It does not reduce language to emotional expression, because “in man there is no natural sign”. A natural sign would need the body's anatomy to tie a given gesture to a given state of mind, and it does not. The angry Japanese smiles; the Westerner goes red and stamps, or goes pale and hisses. Having the same organs and nerves is not enough for the same emotion to produce the same signs, and the chapter goes further: the difference of behaviour corresponds to a difference in the emotions themselves. What matters is how a person uses the body.

The lab looks for the natural sign. The anger preset codes the passage's three expressions with the signs the sentence names: the Japanese smile, the Westerner's flush and stamp, the Westerner's pallor and hiss, each marked as anger. The core, the sign every expression of anger has, is none, and every sign is on the list of what varies. The west preset keeps only the two Western expressions, and the core is still none: the sentence's or else already gives one culture two ways. The third tile, in both, says no variant is marked as not anger.

The smile preset asks the question the other way round. It codes the angry Japanese's smile as anger and adds a smile of pleasure, the lesson's addition, marked as not anger. With one expression of anger the core is its one sign, smile, nothing varies, and the third tile prints smile in pleasure lacks none of the core. Everything the coding found in the angry smile is in the pleased one, so the sign alone does not decide which emotion it is. The objection's smile that has joy in it is not denied; what the chapter denies is that anatomy makes it joy's sign, since a natural sign would need the body's organisation to tie a gesture to a state of mind.

Then the sentence the section turns on: “It is no more natural, and no less conventional, to shout in anger or to kiss in love than to call a table ‘a table’.” A footnote observes that the kiss is not one of the traditional customs of Japan. “Feelings and passional conduct are invented like words.” Even paternity, the chapter says, is an institution. This is not the claim that emotions are arbitrary. Everything in man is both manufactured and natural: no word and no form of behaviour owes nothing to biological being, and none fails to slip away from the simplicity of animal life. Speech is one case of a power to create meanings that cannot be inventoried.

What sets speech apart, the chapter says, is that alone of all expressive processes it settles into a sediment and becomes an acquisition. Writing is not the reason, since music can be written down too. The difference is that each writer knows he is aiming at the same world others have written about, that one can speak about speech and cannot paint about painting, and that every philosopher has dreamed of a discourse to supersede all others, as no painter dreams of exhausting painting. This is what gives Reason its privileged position and the ideal of a thought without words. To understand it, the chapter says, thought has to be put back among the phenomena of expression.

At the end of the pages on aphasia and just after them, the chapter shows the same power in its beginnings and in its failure. Charles Darwin, the naturalist who wrote on the expression of the emotions, thought the knitting of the brows began as a way of shielding the eye from the sun; it becomes part of the human act of thinking hard and shows it to anyone watching. Speech is in this sense wholly motility and wholly intelligence. And Schneider, studied this time by Benary, has his vocabulary and syntax intact but never feels the need to speak, never speaks of what is merely possible, and finds a sentence like the sky is black meaningless: his meanings, the chapter says, are ossified. As with the earlier lessons, the argument concerns the case as reported.

Then the distinction the lesson is named for. The chapter first restates a celebrated distinction it does not name, between languages, the constituted systems of vocabulary and syntax, and the acts of speech they are the residue of. Or again, it says, one might draw “a distinction between a speaking word and a spoken word”. In the speaking word a significant intention is coming into being, aiming at something no natural object defines: “Speech is the surplus of our existence over natural being.” But the act of expression builds a linguistic and cultural world and lets what was reaching beyond fall back into being; hence the spoken word, which enjoys available meanings as one enjoys a fortune already made, and from which the writer's and the philosopher's new acts of expression become possible.

Part One ends here. The body, the chapter says, is not where it is nor what it is: it secretes a meaning that comes from nowhere and projects it on its surroundings. “It is the body which points out, and which speaks”, and Cézanne's remark that the little blue and brown touches make a sitter gaze as he gazes is its example. The Cartesian tradition allows two senses of existing, as a thing or as a consciousness; the experience of one's own body is a third, ambiguous one. Descartes himself, in a letter of 1643 to Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, distinguished the body known through living from the body known by the understanding, and kept the first subordinate to the second, the chapter says, because God stood behind our situation as its rational author.

The chapter's last sentence says the obscurity spreads to the whole perceived world. That is where the book goes next. The next course, Sensing and Space, begins Part Two, The World as Perceived, with its introduction, The Theory of the Body is already a Theory of Perception.

Method

Looking for a natural sign

Four steps; the last says what an empty core means.

1

Code each expression

List the bodily signs each expression of the emotion shows, as the text names them, and mark whether it is an expression of that emotion.

2

Intersect

Keep the signs every expression marked yes shows. If the classical division were right, the natural sign of the emotion would be here.

3

Check the expressions marked no

For each, name the core signs it lacks. If it lacks none, the signs do not by themselves decide the emotion.

4

Read the result, and no further

An empty core says no bodily sign is shared across the coded expressions. It does not say the gestures are meaningless: the chapter holds that anger is read in each of them.

Common mistakes

1
Dividing signs into natural gestures and conventional wordsThe classical division: emotional gestures are natural signs, the same in every body, and words are conventional; so speech cannot be gesture. The chapter grants that a word's conceptual meaning looks arbitrary, and places that arbitrariness: it belongs to the meaning taken apart from the word's gestural sense. And it denies the other half, since in man there is no natural sign. The anger preset's empty core is the passage's case in the lab's terms: no bodily sign is shared by the three expressions of anger it names.
2
Hearing invented like words as arbitrary at willFeelings and passional conduct are invented like words, the chapter says, but it does not say anyone could change them by deciding to. Everything in man is both manufactured and natural: every word and every form of behaviour owes something to biological being, and none reduces to it. The language one lives in cannot simply be exchanged for another either.
3
Taking the empty core to show that gestures have no meaningThe empty core shows only that no single bodily sign is common to the coded expressions. The chapter's claim is the reverse of meaninglessness: anger is read in the gesture, which is anger itself, and the difference between the Japanese smile and the Western flush is a difference in the angers, not in the signs of one anger.
