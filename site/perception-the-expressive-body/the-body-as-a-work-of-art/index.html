The Body as a Work of Art

Chapter 4 asks what kind of unity one's own body has, and answers with a comparison: the body is like a work of art, in which the meaning cannot be had apart from what carries it. The chapter in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Chapter 4 is the shortest in the book, seven pages. It generalises what the last chapter found about bodily space, rejects two accounts of the body's unity, and offers a comparison in their place.

1

Partes extra partes

Latin for parts outside parts: the way a physical thing exists, each piece beside the next and related to it only from outside. The Cartesian tradition makes this the only way of existing in itself, and the chapter denies that one's own body exists like that.

2

Unity under a law

The intellectualist's account of an object's unity: a cube is one thing because an idea of it prescribes in advance how its faces will appear from every side. The chapter asks whether the body is one in that way, and answers no.

3

A nexus of living meanings

The chapter's phrase for the body's unity. A touch on the upper arm means something for the forearm, the shoulder and the arm as seen, because they all take part in one action, as the colours of a painting take part in its meaning.

The material

The passage first, then the chapter line by line

The passage is the comparison the chapter is named for. The lesson then goes back to the chapter's first page and follows it through the telephone, the cube, the work of art and the blind man's stick.

Part One, Chapter 4, pp. 174–175. “The body is to be compared, not to a physical object, but rather to a work of art. … It is in this sense that our body is comparable to a work of art. It is a nexus of living meanings, not the law for a certain number of covariant terms.” The sentences are from pages 174 and 175 of Colin Smith's translation. Read the whole chapter, pages 171 to 177, in your own copy before going on; it is short, and the lesson goes back through it a phrase at a time.

The chapter opens by generalising. The analysis of bodily space in Chapter 3 has shown, for one's own body, something true of every perceived thing: the spatiality of a thing and its being a thing are not two problems. The Cartesian and Kantian tradition already knew this, Merleau-Ponty says, but read it one way round. It made the object's spatial limits its essence, so that to exist in itself is to exist partes extra partes, part outside part, and it explained perceiving a thing by perceiving space. The experience of one's own body teaches the reverse, to embed space in existence: “our body is not primarily in space: it is of it”. The evidence is two kinds of patient. Patients with anosognosia, an unawareness of their own illness, describe their arm as like a snake, long and cold, and still know where it is, since that is where they look for it. The phantom arm is felt where no arm is. So the felt presence of a limb is not settled by its objective place: anosognosia shows that the place is not enough, the phantom that it is not even needed.

Then a scene from the book. You are sitting at a table and want to reach the telephone. The movement of the hand, the straightening of the upper body and the tautening of the leg muscles are, the chapter says, enveloped in each other: you want a result, and the work is shared out among the parts of the body without your arranging it. The possible ways are given in advance as equivalent. You can stay leaning back and stretch the arm further, lean forward, or half stand up. “All these movements are available to us in virtue of their common meaning.” That is why, the chapter adds, children making their first attempts to grasp look at the object and not at their hand: the parts of the body are known by what they are for, and their co-ordination, the chapter says flatly, is not learnt.

The lab codes the scene. The telephone preset lists the three ways the chapter names, each with the parts it engages, and marks every one as a way of reaching the telephone. The lab prints the core, what every variant of the kind has, as hand-at-the-telephone; the list of what varies holds every posture of arm, trunk and legs; and since no variant is marked as not of the kind, the third tile says so. The coding is the lesson's: it decided which parts to name and that each way counts. The count over it is exact, and it says that what the three ways share is their result, not any part of the body.

The chapter turns next to how the parts and the senses are joined. You cannot see your foot inside your shoe, and yet, the chapter says, as you contract it you can see it. Experiments the book reports found that people do not recognise their own hand in a photograph, and are unsure of their own handwriting among others, and yet recognise their own silhouette or their own walk when it is filmed: what they have often seen they miss, and what they have never seen they know at once. So the links between the parts of the body, and between its look and its feel, are not forged gradually by association. I do not translate the data of touch into the language of sight one item at a time; that translation, the chapter says, is my body itself.

Here the chapter states the second account at its strongest. If the parts are not added up, perhaps the body is perceived through its law of construction, as you know in advance every possible face of a cube from its geometry. This is the answer an intellectualist, one of Kant's heirs, gives for any object: a thing is one because an idea of it prescribes how its appearances vary. The chapter grants that an external object may be defined this way, at least as a first approximation, because it stands in front of me and shows me its variations. The body does not. “I am not in front of my body, I am in it, or rather I am it.” Neither its variations nor the constant that would govern them can be set before me as objects; I am the one who sees and touches the arms and legs, the one who unifies them. In Leibniz's term, which the chapter borrows, the body is the effective law of its own changes, and if it interprets anything, it interprets itself.

Definition · The body's unity (the chapter's account)

Neither the joining of parts that lie outside one another nor the subsumption of appearances under a law, but the unity of an action: the arm seen and the arm touched, like the segments of the arm, perform one and the same action, and so each signifies the others.

Now the comparison. In a picture or a piece of music the idea cannot be conveyed except by showing the colours and the sounds. Paul Cézanne, the painter who in the 1880s and 1890s rebuilt the still life and the landscape out of patches of colour and is a touchstone for Merleau-Ponty, is the example: an analysis of his work, if you have not seen the pictures, leaves you choosing among several possible Cézannes, and only the pictures give you the one there is. A poem has a surface meaning that prose could carry, and a further life that makes it a poem; unlike a cry, it lasts, and it would be lost if its text were not kept to the last detail. Change the order of a novel's narrative and you change its meaning. These works are individuals, beings in which “the expression is indistinguishable from the thing expressed”.

Go back to the passage. The body is comparable to a work of art in exactly that sense, and the next sentence says what it rules out: a nexus of living meanings, not a law for covariant terms, a set of quantities that change together by rule. The chapter's own example follows. A touch felt in the upper arm signifies a touch in the forearm and the shoulder and a certain look of the arm, not because all of these are facets of one intelligible arm, as the faces of a cube belong to the idea of a cube, but because they take part in one action. A page earlier the chapter calls what joins the touch and the sight of the hand a certain style of its gestures.

The lab's second instance is the lesson's own, not the book's. Write your signature small on paper with the fingers, large on a blackboard with the whole arm, with the left hand, or with a foot in sand. The signature preset codes the four, each with the limb it uses and with the letter shapes, the order of the strokes and their rhythm. The core is letter-shapes, stroke-order, rhythm, and what varies is fingers, arm, left-hand, leg: no muscle is in every variant. The muscles preset codes the same four by their limbs alone, and the core is none. Coded by its parts, the movement has nothing in common with itself. The tracing preset adds a slow tracing of the signature by someone else, marked as not a signature, and the third tile prints tracing lacks rhythm: it has every shape and every stroke in order.

Notice what the lab has done, though. It found an invariant across a set of variants, and an invariant of variations is very nearly what the passage says the body is not: the law for a certain number of covariant terms. The core is a list drawn up by someone looking at four signatures from outside, the position the chapter says I am never in towards my own body. What the count does show is negative and exact: whatever holds the four together is not a part. What it is, on the chapter's account, is not something the hand consults before it writes.

The chapter ends by extending this from movement to perception. Every habit, it says, is both motor and perceptual, and the blind man's stick from Chapter 3 is the example. One is tempted, the chapter says, giving the view it is about to reject, to think that the blind man builds up the stick and its positions from pressures on his hand, reads the pressures as signs of the stick and the stick as a sign of the object, ever faster and from ever fainter signals. Its reply is that habit relieves him of any such reading. The pressures are no longer given; the stick is no longer something he perceives but “an instrument with which he perceives”, and the perspectives it gives on the kerb are aspects of the kerb, not signs of it. A footnote notes that Husserl long described consciousness this way, as a content taken up as the appearance of an object, and credits him with a decisive step beyond it in his lectures on time.

Last, the child. Kurt Koffka, one of the founders of Gestalt psychology, reported that a child who learns to tell blue from red finds the other colours easier too. Has the child grasped the category colour through the pair? The chapter answers that a category could recognise itself in the data only if it were already rooted in them. The gaze is a natural instrument like the blind man's stick, and to learn to see colours is to acquire a style of seeing, a new use of one's own body, enriching and recasting the body schema (Smith's word is body image). The body, the chapter concludes, is not an object for an I think but a grouping of lived-through meanings that moves towards its own equilibrium.

If the body's unity is the unity of a meaning, the next question is where else in bodily life meaning is found. The next lesson, “The Body as a Sexed Being”, reads Chapter 5, which looks for it in the area of experience that has significance only for us, affective life, and asks how something comes to exist for us through desire or love.

Method

Finding what one movement keeps

Five steps; the last is the chapter's caution.

1

Code each variant

List the parts of the body each way of making the movement engages, and what it produces, as separate words, and mark whether it is still the same movement. These are decisions about the example.

2

Intersect the variants of the kind

Keep the properties every variant marked yes has. That set is the core.

3

List what varies

Every property some variant of the kind has and another lacks varies. If every limb is on this list, no part of the body is what the movements share.

4

Check the variants marked no

For each, name the core properties it lacks. A tracing that keeps every shape and loses the rhythm shows that the rhythm was part of what made the movement the same.

5

Say what the core is not

The core is a list drawn up from outside. On the chapter's account the body's unity is not a law its movements are checked against, so do not read the core as that unity.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the body is one as a machine is, or as any object isThe Cartesian picture holds the body together as a machine's parts are held, each outside the next; the intellectualist holds it together as the mind unifies any object, under a law that prescribes its appearances. The chapter rejects both. The parts are not added up, since what we have never seen of ourselves we recognise at once, and the body is not set before me to be unified, since I am it. Its unity is like a painting's or a poem's, in which the meaning cannot be had apart from what carries it. The telephone preset makes the first point in the lab's terms: the core is hand-at-the-telephone, and every posture varies.
2
Taking the lab's core for the body's unityAn invariant of a set of variants is close to what the passage calls the law for a certain number of covariant terms, the thing the body is not. The core is a spectator's summary of four signatures. It shows exactly that no part is shared, and the chapter's account of what is shared, a style lived in the gestures, is not a further entry on the list.
3
Hearing the comparison with art as a claim that the body is beautiful or designedThe comparison is about one feature of a work of art: its meaning is accessible only through the work itself and cannot be detached from it, as a novel's meaning changes if its order changes. It says nothing about beauty, and nothing about an artist who made the body to a plan.
