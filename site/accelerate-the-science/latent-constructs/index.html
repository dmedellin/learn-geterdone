Latent Constructs

Chapter 13 answers the doubt every reader of survey research has: how far can you trust what people say? Then score a four-item construct for six invented people, reverse-coding the item worded the other way.

Latent constructs in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Latent construct

Something that cannot be measured directly, such as the culture of a team. It is measured through several manifest variables, the survey items that can be answered, and the answers are combined into a single number. The authors' items are statements rather than questions.

2

Operationalizing a construct

Deciding exactly what you mean, then writing one item for each aspect of it. Chapter 13 starts from a clear definition: for culture, Westrum's typology of pathological, bureaucratic and generative organisations, which the authors apply to the team rather than the whole organisation.

3

Reverse-coding

An item worded the opposite way round to the others is turned round before averaging. On a seven-point scale the scored value is 8 minus the answer, so that a high score always means more of what the construct names. The lab prints the score with and without it.

The material

Why a survey can be trusted, and how a construct is built

Read the opening of Chapter 13 through Latent Constructs Help Us Think Carefully About What We're Measuring in your own copy first. The lesson follows it, then scores a construct of its own.

“A latent construct is a way of measuring something that can’t be measured directly.” That is the sentence Chapter 13, Introduction to Psychometrics, turns on. The chapter opens with the two questions the authors are asked most: why use surveys, which Chapter 14 answers, and whether data collected with them can be trusted at all, which this chapter does.

It starts by agreeing that the scepticism is fair. Much of it comes from the surveys most of us meet: push polls, built to make the respondent agree with the pollster, of which they give a political example; quick surveys; and surveys written by people without training. The usual faults are four. Leading questions steer the respondent. Loaded questions force an answer that may not be true for them. Multiple questions in one leave you not knowing which part was answered. Unclear language uses words the respondent may not share. A related weakness is to rest everything on one question. The authors do not dismiss single-question surveys; the Net Promoter Score, they say, is a careful and well-documented one and is easy to compare, though only narrow conclusions should be drawn from it.

Their own answer to the doubt is the latent construct. The room's temperature and a website's response time can be read from a dial. Organisational culture cannot; it has no temperature. It is measured through its component parts, the manifest variables, each of which is an item answered by a person. The items together stand for the construct. The authors write that this is appropriate because culture is the lived experience of the people on the team. It also means they must say what they mean.

That is the first benefit the chapter names. Before writing an item, define the construct. A question like “is your culture good?” leaves the team or organisation unclear, leaves culture undefined and leaves good undefined, so that no answer tells you anything. The culture the authors cared about optimised trust and the flow of information, and they took Ron Westrum's typology of 2004 as the definition: pathological, bureaucratic and generative organisations, told apart by six aspects, cooperation, how messengers are treated, who is responsible, bridging between teams, how failure is handled and how novelty is received. From those aspects they wrote several items, seven in the end, and asked them about a team, not the organisation, since people answer more accurately about their team and large organisations hold different cultures in different corners.

The score then is the average. Respondents rate each item from 1 for strongly disagree to 7 for strongly agree, and the average over the items is, in the authors' phrase, an organisational culture temperature of sorts. Chapter 13 says that statistical tests can then confirm that the items do measure the same underlying idea; the next lesson reads those tests, and Measuring and Changing Culture's lesson Measuring Culture scores the authors' own culture construct.

Now score a construct. The lab's sample is invented for the lesson: confidence in releasing, measured by four items. Three are worded so that agreeing means more confidence, routine, unaided and clarity. The third, dread, says how much releasing worries the person, so agreeing means less, and it is marked as reverse-coded. Six invented people, R1 to R6, answer on the seven-point scale. On the preset four-items the lab scores each person by turning round the dread answer, 8 minus the answer, and averaging the four. R1's answers 5, 4, 1, 5 become 5, 4, 7, 5 and a score of 5.25.

The six scores are 5.25, 5, 4.25, 2.5, 6.5 and 3.25, and the construct mean over the six, 107/24, is 4.458 to three places. With a line drawn at the scale's neutral point, 4, four people score above it and two below. The item means are printed too, as scored: the dread item averages 14/3, 4.667, after reversing, where its raw answers average 3.333. A last tile shows what happens when the reversal is forgotten: the construct mean falls to 4.125, and counted by hand three people would be above the line instead of four, because R1 drops below it.

The second preset drops one item. On without-clarity the lab scores the same six people from three items. The construct mean becomes 13/3, 4.333, and R3, who scored 4.25 on four items, scores 3.667 on three, so now three are above the line and three below. One item moved one person across it. This is the sense in which the chapter says to use several: a single item can carry a lot of a person's score, and more than one lets you see when it does.

Exercise

  • Three items on the seven-point scale, the second reverse-coded: steady, calm, no; anxious, calm, yes; routine, calm, no.
  • Four people's answers: A, 6, 2, 5; B, 3, 3, 3; C, 5, 1, 5; D, 2, 6, 3.
  • Score each person, then the construct mean, with and without reversing the second item. Count the people above a line at 4 both ways.

Do it by hand and then in the lab. Say what the two construct means are, and which person is above the line only when the item is reversed.

A score is only worth reporting if the items behave. The next lesson, Several Views, and a Safeguard Against Rogue Data, reads the tests that check it.

How the lab counts. Each answer is a whole number from 1 to the scale's points, one per item in the items' order. A reverse-coded item is scored points + 1 − the answer (on 7 points a 2 scores 6). A respondent's score on a construct is the mean of their scored answers on its items; a construct's mean is the mean of those scores over the respondents; an item's mean is over the respondents too, as scored. A respondent is above the line when their score is greater than it, at it when equal. The last tile scores every item as answered, the mistake of forgetting to reverse. Constructs and items go in the order given; means are exact, shown to 3 places when they do not end.

Method

Scoring a latent construct

Five steps, the last of them on a short survey of your own.

1

Define the construct in a sentence

Write what you mean and for whom, such as a team's confidence in releasing. If the sentence leaves out an aspect you care about, add it before you write items.

2

Write one item per aspect

Each item is a statement about one thing. Note any item worded the opposite way round to the rest.

3

Reverse the opposite items

For an item marked reverse, replace each answer by 8 minus the answer on a seven-point scale.

4

Average each person's items

Add a person's scored answers and divide by the number of items, then average the people for the construct mean.

5

Do it on a short survey of your own

Write four statements about one thing in your team's work, one of them reversed, get six people to rate them from 1 to 7, and type the answers into the lab. Note the construct mean with and without the reversal.

Common mistakes

1
Asking one broad question and calling it a measureThe chapter's example is asking whether a culture is good, which leaves unclear whose culture, what culture means and what good means. A construct is built from a definition and one item for each aspect, so that each answer means something.
2
Averaging items without turning the reversed ones roundA reverse-worded item answered consistently with the others will pull the average toward the middle. In the lab, forgetting the reversal changes the construct mean from 4.458 to 4.125 and moves R1 across the line. A score is only the mean of items that all point the same way.
3
Taking a construct's score for a fact about the teamThe score is a mean of perceptions the people reported on a scale. Chapter 13 itself says all measures are proxies and that the constructs still have to pass tests, which the next lesson reads. The lab's mean describes six invented people and nothing else.
