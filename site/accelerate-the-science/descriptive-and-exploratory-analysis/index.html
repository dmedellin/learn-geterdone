Descriptive and Exploratory Analysis

The second rung of Chapter 12's ladder looks for relationships in the data, and its main tool is correlation. Read what exploratory analysis is for, what Pearson's r measures, and why the authors insist that it is only the exploratory stage; then compute r for two invented series that both climb with time, and again for their year-on-year changes.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Exploratory analysis

The second type of analysis in Leek's ladder. It looks for relationships among the variables, often with charts, and may find outliers, though the researcher must check that an outlier is not a legitimate member of the group. The authors call it where new hypotheses come from, and say that anyone who wants to speak of prediction or causation has to go further.

2

Pearson's r

The correlation the authors report throughout. It rates how strongly two variables follow a straight line together, on a scale from −1 to 1: 1 when they move together exactly, −1 when they move in exactly opposite directions, 0 when they are not correlated at all.

3

Spurious correlation

A strong correlation with nothing connecting the two measures but chance or a third variable. In the lab the third variable is time: two series that both grow every year correlate strongly whether or not either has anything to do with the other.

The material

Looking for relationships, and why that is not the end

Read Exploratory Analysis in Chapter 12, with its footnote on Pearson correlations, in your own copy first. The lesson follows it, then computes r on two invented series.

“Two variables moving together can always be due to a third variable or, sometimes, just chance.” That is the second rung of Chapter 12's ladder in a sentence. Exploratory Analysis, the section it comes from, follows Descriptive Analysis and precedes the inferential rung, and it is where a team first asks whether anything in its data goes with anything else.

The authors call it a broad category: looking for relationships among the data, perhaps with visualisations to show patterns, and spotting outliers. They add a caution about the last of those; a researcher has to be careful that an outlier really is one, and not a legitimate member of the group. They also say this is the enjoyable stage, where divergent thinkers generate new ideas, new hypotheses and new projects, and in the next breath that a team which wants to say anything about prediction or causation has to go further.

The tool of the stage is correlation. The chapter says correlation shows how closely two variables rise and fall together, or against each other, and not why. The measure the authors report everywhere in the book is Pearson's r, which their footnote describes as the strength of a linear relationship, running from −1 through 0 to 1. The value is not a percentage of anything. A scatter of points close to a rising line gives r near 1, close to a falling line gives r near −1, and a shapeless cloud gives r near 0.

r = Σ(x − mean of x)·(y − mean of y) ÷ √( Σ(x − mean of x)² · Σ(y − mean of y)² )

The chapter prints no formula, so the one above is standard statistics. Read it as three sums over the pairs: how the two measures vary together on top, and how each varies on its own underneath, so that the result is the same whatever units each is measured in. If both measures are above their means together and below together, the top sum is large and positive.

The chapter then makes its point with a joke that is also serious. Tyler Vigen's website of spurious correlations, the authors say, lists pairs of measures that correlate highly and that common sense tells us are neither predictive nor causal. Their Figure 12.1 shows per capita cheese consumption against deaths by tangling in bedsheets, with r = 0.9471. Nobody believes the one drives the other. Yet, the authors warn, when we fish in the data our minds fill in a story, because datasets that are related so often make sense. Hence the chapter's conclusion for this rung: report the correlations, then go on to analyses that ask more.

You can see one way such a correlation arises on a sample written for this lesson. Eight years of an invented company, a growing one: the number of deploys it makes per week, and the cups of coffee its office café sells per day. Both rise with the head count, and neither is connected to the other in any way you would defend.

year 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 deploys/week 13 19 25 28 33 37 43 48 coffees/day 60 65 68 71 73 79 83 86 change in deploys 6 6 3 5 4 6 5 change in coffees 5 3 3 2 6 4 3

On the preset shared-growth the lab reports r = 0.994 for the levels, the figures as they stand. Nearly a perfect line, and no reason to think the coffee has anything to do with the deploys. The lab then differences the series: each year's figure minus the year before, so that the steady climb drops out and only the year-to-year movement is left. The changes in the table are 6, 6, 3, 5, 4, 6, 5 against 5, 3, 3, 2, 6, 4, 3, and their r is exactly 0. Once the shared climb is taken away, nothing is left that moves together. Differencing is a standard device for series that trend with time; the book does not use it, and it is here only to show where the 0.994 came from. The lab's last tile does the comparing for you: the changes' r is smaller in size than the levels', and one of the two is 0.

The device does not always empty a correlation, and it should not be read as a test of cause. On the preset moving-together the deploys are the same but the second series is merged pull requests per week, 30, 33, 36, 37, 39, 41, 44, 46. The levels give r = 0.999, and the changes, 6, 6, 3, 5, 4, 6, 5 against 3, 3, 1, 2, 2, 3, 2, still give r = 0.955. The two really do rise and fall together year to year. That is a stronger sort of correlation, and it is still only one: a larger team may well produce both, which is the third variable of the chapter's sentence, and the lab cannot show whether it does.

Exercise

  • Five years of an invented company's test suite, in hundreds of tests, and its meeting-room bookings per week: 1, 10, 4; 2, 14, 7; 3, 17, 11; 4, 21, 14; 5, 24, 18.
  • Find r for the levels. Then write the four year-on-year changes of each and find r for those.

Use the lab to check your arithmetic after you have done it by hand. Say what the two answers tell you about the climb and about the year-to-year movement, and what neither can tell you about why the bookings follow the tests.

Exploration finds the pattern; it cannot say what lies behind it. The next lesson, Inferential Predictive Analysis, reads how the authors go further without leaving the survey, by deciding what they expect before they look.

Describing and relating measurestwo trending series: r of the levels and of the changes

How the lab counts. r = Sxy ÷ √(Sxx × Syy), where Sxx and Syy are the sums of squared deviations from the means and Sxy the sum of their products; r² = Sxy² ÷ (Sxx × Syy) is exact. The first differences are each value less the one before (n − 1 of them, x and y alike). The comparison is of size (|r|, on the squares, exactly) and of sign. An r is not defined when every x (or y) is the same, as the differences of a straight-line trend are. A square root is printed to 3 places from the exact rational under it by an integer square root, rounded half up, so the digits depend on the inputs alone; it is shown without ≈ only when it is exact.

Method

Checking a correlation for a shared trend

Five steps, the last of them on two series of your own.

1

Line the pairs up

Write the two measures side by side, one pair per row, in time order. Check that every row is the same period for both.

2

Find r for the levels

Find each mean, each distance from its mean, and the three sums. Divide the cross sum by the square root of the product of the other two.

3

Find r for the changes

Subtract each figure from the next one, in both series, and find r again for the two lists of changes.

4

Compare the two, and name what is missing

A large r of the levels and a small r of the changes means the series shared a trend. A large r of both means they also move together from year to year. Either way, write one candidate third variable.

5

Do it on series of your own

Take two monthly counts from systems you know, such as deploys and tickets, type eight or more months into the lab, and read the two figures.

Σx = 246, Σx² = 8550 Σy = 585, Σy² = 43345 Σxy = 18732 top = 8·18732 − 246·585 = 149856 − 143910 = 5946 x part = 8·8550 − 246² = 68400 − 60516 = 7884 y part = 8·43345 − 585² = 346760 − 342225 = 4535 r = 5946 / √(7884·4535) = 5946 / √35753940 √35753940 ≈ 5979.5, so r ≈ 0.994

The same three sums on the changes, with n = 7, give a top of 7·130 − 35·26 = 0, so r is exactly 0 and the square root never matters. A top of zero is what the table of changes looks like when they have nothing in common.

Common mistakes

1
Reading a high correlation as a connectionChapter 12 says a third variable, or sometimes plain chance, can always lie behind a pair that moves together, and illustrates it with a pair of measures nobody connects. The lab shows one route: two series that both climb with time correlate at 0.994 with nothing between them.
2
Treating r as a percentagePearson's r is a coefficient between −1 and 1, not a share of anything. An r of 0.5 is not half of a perfect relationship in any sense you can act on. Squaring it gives the share of variance a straight line accounts for, which the last lesson of Surveys, Data and Statistical Methods takes up.
3
Taking a zero correlation to mean nothing is going onPearson's r measures a linear relationship only. A pair that moves in a curve can have r near 0, and a zero r of the changes, as on the first preset, shows only that no common movement is left once the climb is removed from this sample.
