A or An: by Sound, Not by Letter

The usual rule says an goes before a vowel letter, one of a, e, i, o and u. On the 493 printed lines where the dictionary, a book that lists the words of a language, can say how the next word begins, that rule is right 486 times. A rule about the first sound of the next word is right on all 493. The seven lines that separate them show what the first rule was really about.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas.

1

The rule is about sounds, because the reason for it is about sounds

A and an are one word in two shapes. The shape that ends in n is used so that two vowel sounds do not meet: an egg, not a egg. When the next word begins with a consonant sound, any sound that is not a vowel sound, there is nothing to separate, and a is enough. The reason is spoken, so the rule is spoken.

2

The letter rule is nearly right because spelling usually follows sound

Most words that begin with a vowel letter begin with a vowel sound. The letter rule is right 486 times in 493 for that reason. It fails when the spelling and the sound come apart, and there are three ways for that to happen.

3

One question replaces the rule and its exceptions

Say the next word. If it begins with a vowel sound, use an. If not, use a. Nothing about the lines changes. The question changes, and the number of exceptions falls from seven to none.

The material

The seven lines the letter rule misses

Choose the letter rule and read the table of misses. All seven are in the play.

The rule you were taught is about letters, and letters are the wrong thing to look at. The lab holds 493 lines from three texts, each with an a or an and the word that follows.

Counted on this page, the rule that puts an before a vowel letter is right on 486 of the 493 lines, which is 98.6%. Seven lines break it. Look at what they are: a University, a utilitarian and an hour, which the play uses five times.

Why the rule was ever about letters

Most words begin with the sound their first letter suggests, and a rule about letters is easier to teach than a rule about sounds. That is the whole reason. The rule is a short way of writing the real one, and it is a good short way: 98.6% is a high score.

The rule about sounds

Change the question to the first sound of the next word, and the rule is right on all 493 lines. Nothing about the lines changed. The question changed. The same words are in the same table, and the column that said missed is empty.

The dictionary gives the sound. That is the sound of hour, which is the vowel in our, and of University, which starts as you does. Seven of these lines, and no more, are what separated the two rules here.

Three sources, one result

Switch the lab to the play, to the modern documents or to the passage from the novel. The play has 455 lines. The letter rule is right on 448 of them and the sound rule on all 455. On the modern documents both rules are right on 29 of 29, and on the passage both are right on 9 of 9. The table also sets aside ten marks that look like a but are not the word, such as (a) and a page number 21a.

The modern documents show that the two rules agree for most words. The play is where they part, and all seven lines that separate them are in it.

The whole novel, quoted

The page cannot carry the whole of Pride and Prejudice. A count made once, away from this page, over its 2,266 lines gives the letter rule 2,239 right (98.8%) and the sound rule 2,263 right (99.9%). That count is quoted here and not computed on this page. The three lines the sound rule misses are an union, twice, and an uniform. They are Austen's habit, and the habit has gone.

What the page cannot tell you

It reads one way of saying each word, the American one. In a different kind of English some words begin with a different sound, and the choice of a or an follows. The lab says so in its own words, under the numbers, and lists the 17 lines the dictionary cannot score.

Method

Choosing a or an for a word in front of you

Four steps.

1

Say the next word, and listen only to how it begins

Ignore the spelling. In hour the first sound is the vowel sound in our. In university it is the sound at the start of you.

2

Is that sound a vowel?

A vowel sound is made with the mouth open and the air not stopped. If yes, use an: an hour, an egg, an answer.

3

If it is a consonant sound, use a

A University, a utilitarian, a house. You and one begin with a consonant sound, whatever the letter says.

4

Check the three kinds that fool the letter

A u said like you, a silent h, and one. These are the words to remember. For everything else the letter and the sound agree.

Three words, and why each one fools the letter

The seven lines that the letter rule misses come from three words. In each case the first letter and the first sound disagree.

The first two begin with a vowel letter said as a consonant. The third begins with a consonant letter that is not said at all. Five of the seven misses are an hour, which the play uses five times. A third kind is one, which begins with the sound of w and so takes a. It is not among the seven. Common mistakes

1
Believing that an goes before a vowel letterThat is right 486 times in 493 on these lines, and it breaks on a University, a utilitarian and an hour. The rule you want is about the sound the next word starts with. The letter is a good guide, and a bad reason.
2
Treating an union as correct because an old book has itJane Austen wrote an union and an uniform in 1813. In a count over the whole novel, made once and quoted here, the sound rule is right on 2,263 of 2,266 lines, which is 99.9%, and those three lines are all it misses. The habit was real and is gone. The sound rule marks the lines wrong because it describes the English of today.
3
Reading the unknown words as errors17 of the 510 lines are not scored, because the dictionary does not have the next word. They are not wrong. The page says so in its own column, and the 493 are the lines it can judge.
