What Is Continuous Delivery?

Continuous delivery in seven lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first is the chapter's, the other two are what the lab measures.

1

Continuous delivery

A set of capabilities that let a team get changes of every kind into production, or into users' hands, safely, quickly and sustainably. It is a way of working, kept going by tools, and not one tool.

2

Batch

The changes released together. A batch of twenty-four waits until all twenty-four are ready; a batch of six waits for six. Chapter 4 argues for small ones, because feedback arrives sooner and less work is built on a wrong guess.

3

Release cost

The fixed time to put out one batch, whatever its size: the build, the checks, the approvals, the deploy. Small batches multiply it, which is why the chapter pairs them with automation.

The material

The chapter's argument, then the count

Read the opening of Chapter 4 and its first section, What Is Continuous Delivery?, in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the definition and the principles in order, then counts one of them on a small sample.

“Continuous delivery is a set of capabilities that enable us to get changes of all kinds—features, configuration changes, bug fixes, experiments—into production or into the hands of users safely, quickly, and sustainably.” That is the definition Forsgren, Humble and Kim give in Chapter 4, Technical Practices, and the chapter asks a plain question of it: do the technical practices behind such a definition measurably change how a team performs? Their reason for asking is that many Agile adoptions treated technical practice as secondary to the management and team practices some Agile frameworks stress, though Extreme Programming, among the most popular frameworks when the Agile Manifesto appeared in 2001, had put practices like test-driven development and continuous integration at its centre.

Five principles sit at the heart of it. The first is to build quality in. The authors quote Deming's third point, that you should stop relying on inspection at scale and design quality in from the start, and read it as a call to find problems quickly, while they are cheap to fix. The second is to work in small batches: plan in chunks that deliver a measurable outcome to a small part of the market, get feedback, and correct course. They admit that small chunks add overhead and argue that the overhead buys the chance to avoid work of no value or negative value; the goal, they say, is to change the economics of delivery so that pushing out a single change costs very little.

The third principle divides the labour. Repetitive, slow work such as regression testing and deployment should be automated, so that people are free for the work that needs judgement: improving the design of systems and processes in response to feedback. The fourth is relentless improvement, which the authors name as the defining trait of high-performing teams: they are never satisfied, and improvement is part of everybody's daily work. The fifth is that everyone is responsible. Drawing on Westrum, whom Measuring and Changing Culture read, they note that in a bureaucratic organization development watches throughput, testing quality and operations stability, though all three are outcomes of the whole system. Management's job is to make those outcomes visible and to set goals for them that are measurable and time-bound.

Three foundations make the principles practical. Comprehensive configuration management means environments and software can be built, tested and deployed in a fully automated way from what is stored in version control; manual approvals may remain, but once given, everything after them is automatic. Continuous integration (CI) means short-lived branches, under a day's work, merged into trunk often, with each change triggering a build and unit tests and any failure fixed at once. Continuous testing means tests run against every commit, developers able to run them all on their own machines, testers doing exploratory work on the latest builds, and no one calling work done until its automated tests exist and pass. The section closes on the picture: several feedback loops, and a release that is a routine activity available on demand. The pipeline that joins the loops is named in a footnote; the chapter does not describe one.

Now the count. Think of a team with twenty-four changes ready to ship, each taking an hour to prepare and check, and a release that costs two hours however many changes it carries. These numbers are invented for this lesson. The lab takes the number of changes, the batch size, the hours per change and the hours per release, and reports how many batches there are, when the first batch is out (the earliest feedback anyone can have), when the last is out, the average wait of a change from the start until its batch is released, and the total hours spent on releases. The preset whole sends all twenty-four as one batch: 1 release, out at hour 26, an average wait of 26 hours, 2 hours of release overhead.

The preset halved splits them into batches of twelve. Each batch takes 12 hours of work and 2 of release, so the first is out at hour 14 and the second at hour 28; the average wait is 21 hours and the overhead is 4. The first feedback arrives 12 hours sooner and the last change leaves 2 hours later. The preset quarter goes to batches of six: 4 releases, the first at hour 8, the last at hour 32, an average wait of 20 hours and 8 hours of overhead. The first feedback now arrives in a third of the time, and the last change is 6 hours later than in whole.

That is the trade the authors name when they say small batches add overhead, and it is why the principle is paired with the third one. The preset automated keeps batches of six but cuts the release to a quarter of an hour, as a team might by automating the build and the deploy. The first feedback arrives at hour 6.25, the last change at hour 25, the average wait falls to about 15.63 hours, and the overhead is 1 hour in all. Nothing in the changes got smaller; the cost of releasing one did, which is the "changing the economics" of the chapter in a number.

A word on flow, which this lesson adds and the chapter does not mention. Work in process, the number of changes started and not yet finished, is tied to lead time by Little's law: the average work in process equals the average rate at which work finishes times the average time it spends in the system. A team with 18 items in process that finishes 3 a day has an average lead time of 6 days. The System Design lesson "Little's Law from a Trace" proves and counts it. Here it is only a reason to want fewer changes waiting at once, which is what a smaller batch gives.

Method

Comparing batch sizes

Four steps, the last of them on a team of your own.

1

Write down the three quantities

Count the changes waiting, the hours one takes to prepare and check, and the fixed hours a release costs whatever it carries.

2

Work out one batch

A batch of b changes is out after b times the hours per change, plus the release hours. Its changes all wait until then.

3

Compare two sizes

Find the hour of the first release, the hour of the last, and the average wait for each size. Note which measure improves and which gets worse.

4

Do it on your own team

Take the changes your team has ready now, estimate an honest release cost from your last release, and type both into the lab with two batch sizes. Write down what the release cost would have to be for the smaller batch to finish first.

Common mistakes

1
Taking continuous delivery to be a pipeline you buyThe chapter defines it as a set of capabilities and builds the definition from principles that are about people as much as tools: building quality in, everyone being responsible, improving always. The deployment pipeline is mentioned only in a footnote, as the pattern that joins the feedback loops.
2
Reading small batches as always fasterThe authors say outright that working in small chunks adds overhead. The lab shows it: on the invented sample the last change leaves later with batches of six than with one batch of twenty-four, and only a cheaper release reverses that. What small batches buy is earlier feedback and less work built on a wrong guess.
3
Leaving testing and quality to the end of the workContinuous testing and building quality in say the opposite: tests run against every commit, developers can run them all locally, and no one calls work done until its automated tests pass. Waiting for a feature to be complete before testing is the dependence on inspection that Deming's point warns against.
