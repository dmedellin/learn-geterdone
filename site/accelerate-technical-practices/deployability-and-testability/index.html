Types of Systems and Deployability

Chapter 5 asks whether continuous delivery can work on mainframes, embedded systems and big balls of mud. Read what the survey found about system types and about the two architectural properties that did predict performance; then find the release groups in a shop's services and see what removing one tie does.

Types of systems in seven lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Testability

Being able to do most of a system's testing without an integrated environment, which the authors define as one where many separate services run side by side, a staging environment for example.

2

Deployability

Being able to release your own application without waiting for, or releasing with, the services it relies on.

3

Release group

A set of components joined by ties that say they must be released together, directly or through others. The lab finds the groups, the largest, and the components that belong to none.

The material

The chapter's argument, then the count

Read the opening of Chapter 5 and its next two sections, Types of Systems and Delivery Performance and Focus on Deployability and Testability, in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the finding and then finds the groups in a small system.

“We found that high performance is possible with all kinds of systems, provided that systems—and the teams that build and maintain them—are loosely coupled.” That is the answer Forsgren, Humble and Kim give at the start of Chapter 5, Architecture, to a question that sits under the whole book: continuous delivery began in web systems, so can it be applied to mainframes, firmware and the tangle of thousands of tightly coupled systems found in many enterprises?

The first section reports a search for a pattern. The survey asked about eleven kinds of system, from new systems not yet released to systems of record, packaged software, embedded software and mainframes, both as the main system under development and as a service that team's system integrates against. Low performers were more likely to say their software was custom software written by another company, such as an outsourcing partner, and more likely to work on mainframe systems, though integrating against a mainframe was not significantly correlated with performance. For all the other kinds there was no significant correlation. The authors found that surprising, since they had expected packaged software, systems of record and embedded systems to do worse, and engagement systems and new systems to do better.

Their conclusion is that it is the architecture's characteristics that count and not the implementation: achievable on packaged software and old mainframes, and no guarantee on the latest services in containers if the characteristics are ignored. They draw one more lesson from the outsourcing finding: since delivery performance affects organizational performance, as Chapter 2 argued, the capability to build the core, strategic products should be brought in-house.

The next section names the characteristics. Two properties were more common among the high performers: teams could test most of their work without a shared integrated environment, and could release on their own without lock-step with the services they use. The authors call them testability and deployability and say they follow from systems that are loosely coupled, meaning they can be changed and validated independently. The 2017 survey tested a loosely coupled, well-encapsulated architecture directly, and it turned out to be the biggest contributor to continuous delivery in that analysis, bigger than test and deployment automation. It was gauged by six questions that come to this: whether a team can change its design, finish its work, test and deploy without asking or waiting for other teams, and deploy in working hours without an outage.

The authors add that service-oriented architecture and microservices are supposed to deliver these properties, but in practice many so-called service-oriented architectures do not allow services to be tested and deployed independently, and will not lift performance. Now the count. The lab takes a list of components and a list of ties, each a pair that must be released together, and finds the release groups. The shop in the preset shop is invented: eight services and five ties. It has 3 groups: one of six services (cart, checkout, payments, pricing, catalog, search) and two of one, email and reviews. So 2 components can be released alone, and the largest group has 6.

Take away one tie and see what it buys. The preset shop carries the removal of the tie between cart and pricing. After it there are 4 groups: cart, checkout and payments; pricing, catalog and search; and the same two single services. The largest group has 3, and the number deployable alone is still 2. Removing one tie halved the biggest group but freed nobody to ship alone, which is a reminder that independence is a property of the whole set of ties. The preset ledger is a bank's mainframe ledger and four other services; the ledger has no tie, and neither has the currency service. It has 3 groups, 2 components deployable alone and a largest group of 3.

The lab is not a test of whether a tie is real. A pair joined by a tie might share a database, a library or a release calendar, and the lab sees only the list. The preset ledger shows only what the chapter says, that a mainframe component can be released alone when nothing ties it to the rest.

Exercise

  • A clinic's components: booking, notes, billing, reminders, labs.

How the lab counts. A tie a, b says the two components must be released together, in either direction; ties chain, so a release group is every component reachable from another through ties (a connected component). A component in a group of one is deployable alone. When components carry teams, a group holding more than one team's components makes those teams coordinate its release. Groups are listed in the order of their first component in the input, and the largest group is the first of the largest size. With a tie to remove, the after tiles are the same counts without it.

Method

Finding the release groups

Four steps, the last of them on a system of your own.

1

List the components

Name each part that could be deployed. Include the ones with no ties, since they are the parts that can be released alone.

2

List the ties

For each pair that must go out together, write the pair. A shared database, a shared library and a shared release calendar each count.

3

Group by following ties

Start from one component and add everything tied to it, then everything tied to those. When no tie leaves the set, it is one group.

4

Do it on your own system

List the services of a system you work on, and for each pair ask whether one can go out without the other. Type the ties in. Write down the largest group.

A group is a set in which you can walk from any component to any other along ties. Removing a tie can split a group in two, as here, or leave it whole if another path joins the same components.

Common mistakes

1
Expecting the kind of system to predict performanceThe authors expected packaged software, systems of record and embedded systems to do worse, and new and customer-facing systems to do better, and found otherwise for most kinds. Low performers were more likely to report outsourced custom software and mainframes; the rest showed no significant correlation.
2
Treating microservices as the same thing as loose couplingThe chapter says a team can get the characteristics on packaged software and mainframes, and can miss them on the newest services in containers. It asks that services be tested and deployed independently, whatever they are called.
3
Concluding that legacy systems cannot performThe chapter says the characteristics are achievable even with packaged software and mainframes. The lab's ledger preset is a small illustration, not evidence: a mainframe component with no ties can be released alone.
