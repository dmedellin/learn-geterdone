Primogeniture, Entails and Tenants

Great estates kept whole by primogeniture and entail, worked by slaves, then metayers, then farmers on short leases, gave nobody the reason to improve the land.

In brief

  • Primogeniture and entail
  • Who bears the cost, who gets the return
  • Security of the lease

Great estates, and who occupied them

Book III, Chapter 2. When the German and Scythian nations overran the western provinces of the Roman empire, the land was engrossed “by a few great proprietors”. It might have been a transitory evil, divided again by succession or sale. “The law of primogeniture hindered them from being divided by succession; the introduction of entails prevented their being broke into small parcels by alienation.” The old Roman law divided land among all the children, like moveables. Primogeniture came when land meant “power and protection”: every great landlord was a petty prince, his tenants his subjects, and “To divide it was to ruin it”.

His comment on the later survival is one of the book's best-known sentences. “Laws frequently continue in force long after the circumstances which first gave occasion to them, and which could alone render them reasonable, are no more.” In the present state of Europe the owner of one acre is as secure as the owner of 100,000, and primogeniture survives as “the fittest to support the pride of family distinctions”. Entails he finds “completely absurd”, resting on “the supposition that every successive generation of men have not an equal right to the earth, and to all that it possesses; but that the property of the present generation should be restrained and regulated according to the fancy of those who died, perhaps five hundred years ago”. In Scotland “more than one fifth, perhaps more than one third part of the whole lands in the country, are at present supposed to be under strict entail”.

What follows is the economic point. “It seldom happens, however, that a great proprietor is a great improver.” In disorderly times he had no leisure; when law and order gave him leisure, he often wanted the inclination, “and almost always the requisite abilities”. To improve land profitably “like all other commercial projects, requires an exact attention to small savings and small gains, of which a man born to a great fortune, even though naturally frugal, is very seldom capable”. He embellishes four or five hundred acres near his house at ten times what the land is worth, and would be bankrupt before he finished a tenth of the rest.

Then those who occupied the land. In the ancient state of Europe “the occupiers of land were all tenants at will. They were all, or almost all, slaves”, and whatever they improved was improved at the master's expense and for his benefit. Smith's judgment on slave labour is flat: “The experience of all ages and nations, I believe, demonstrates that the work done by slaves, though it appears to cost only their maintenance, is in the end the dearest of any.” The reason he gives is incentive: “A person who can acquire no property can have no other interest but to eat as much and to labour as little as possible.” He says slavery then survived only where the crop could bear its expense, sugar and tobacco but not corn, a statement about what the planters of the 1770s found profitable and not a defence of it; he adds that the pride of man makes him love to domineer, and prefer slaves wherever the law and the work allow.

The next occupier is the metayer, the coloni partiarii of the Latin authors. The proprietor supplied the seed, cattle and instruments, the produce was divided equally after the stock was made good, and the metayer, being a freeman, had “a plain interest that the whole produce should be as great as possible, in order that their own proportion may be so”. It was a gain over slavery. But he could not improve at his own expense: “It could never, however, be the interest even of this last species of cultivators, to lay out, in the further improvement of the land, any part of the little stock which they might save from their own share of the produce; because the landlord, who laid out nothing, was to get one half of whatever it produced.” The tithe, a mere tenth, is “a very great hindrance to improvement”, so a tax of one half must have been “an effectual bar”. In France, Smith says, five parts out of six of the kingdom were still so occupied, and the proprietors complained that metayers used their masters' cattle for carriage, where they kept the whole profit, rather than for cultivation, where they shared it.

Last came farmers properly so called, who cultivated with their own stock and paid a rent certain. “When such farmers have a lease for a term of years, they may sometimes find it for their interest to lay out part of their capital in the further improvement of the farm; because they may sometimes expect to recover it, with a large profit, before the expiration of the lease.” But their possession was long precarious: a new purchaser could oust them, and until the action of ejectment, invented about the 14th of Henry VII, a tenant turned out had only damages. “In England, therefore the security of the tenant is equal to that of the proprietor.” Smith adds that a lease for life of forty shillings a year made a tenant a freeholder with a vote, which made the yeomanry respectable to their landlords, and that those laws and customs “have perhaps contributed more to the present grandeur of England, than all their boasted regulations of commerce taken together”. Elsewhere security was short: in France a lease was good against a purchaser for only “nine years from the commencement of the lease”, lately extended to twenty-seven, “a period still too short to encourage the tenant to make the most important improvements”.

Definition · Gain from an improvement (this lab's term)

What the occupier gets back from an improvement over the years he holds it: his share of the added produce times the added produce times the years, less the part of the cost he bears. Smith argues from interest, in words. The lab puts his argument into arithmetic with one stated cost and one stated yield, both written for the lesson.

The lab takes an improvement that costs 90 pounds and adds 15 pounds of produce a year, figures written for this lesson, and asks four holders the question. The slave bears no cost and keeps no share, so his gain is nothing and he does not improve. The metayer bears the whole cost and keeps half, 7 pounds 10 shillings a year. The farmer on a lease bears the whole cost and keeps all 15. A farmer who also pays a tithe keeps nine tenths, 13 pounds 10 shillings. In the nine-years preset, France's term, the farmer gains 15 × 9 − 90 = 45, the farmer paying tithe (9/10) × 15 × 9 − 90 = 31.50, and the metayer's half share makes (1/2) × 15 × 9 − 90 = −22.50, a loss, so he does not improve. In the five-years preset no one improves, because even the farmer's 75 does not repay 90. In the twenty-seven-years preset all of them do, the metayer with a gain of 112.50. The number of years that repays an improvement is the cost divided by the yearly return the occupier keeps, 6 years for the farmer and 12 for the metayer. Halve the share and the term doubles.

Smith closes the tenants with two more grievances: arbitrary services in the lease and the purveyors who took horses, carriages and provisions at the king's price. And the taille, still in France, a tax on the supposed profits of the farmer, which they estimate from the stock he has on the farm: “It is his interest, therefore, to appear to have as little as possible, and consequently to employ as little as possible in its cultivation, and none in its improvement.” The farmer who borrows his land is, in Smith's comparison, like a merchant trading with borrowed money, whose stock must improve more slowly than that of one who trades with his own, since a large share of the profits is consumed by interest. After small proprietors, he says, rich and great farmers are in every country the principal improvers. And the older policy of Europe, prohibiting the export of corn and punishing engrossers, regraters and forestallers, did the rest. The next lesson turns to the other half of the story: how the towns came to be free.

How the lab counts. Over the years the improvement adds its yearly produce times the years. The occupier keeps the share of that and pays the share of the cost: gain = share × added × years − bears × cost. The occupier improves when the gain is above 0 (the lab's rule; Smith, III.2, argues that a tenant who keeps only part of the produce, or whose lease is short, has little interest in improving). The landlord's part is the rest of the produce less the rest of the cost. Shares are fractions from 0 to 1 (1/2 for a metayer). No interest or discounting is applied.

Method

Testing whether an occupier would improve

Four steps, for any tenure and any term.

1

State the improvement

Its cost and the extra produce it adds in a year. Both are the lesson's figures; Smith gives no table.

2

Find what the occupier keeps

Multiply the extra produce by the occupier's share, then by the years he holds.

3

Subtract what he bears

Multiply the cost by the share of it the occupier pays. The remainder is his gain; if it is above nothing, he would improve.

4

Find the term that just pays

Divide the cost he bears by what he keeps in a year. A smaller share needs a longer term, in proportion.

The metayer and the farmer, nine years

Take the nine-years preset: an improvement costing 90 pounds and adding 15 a year, on a nine-year term. The metayer bears the cost and keeps a half share; the farmer bears it and keeps all.

farmer keeps: 1 · 15 · 9 = 135 farmer's gain: 135 − 90 = 45 metayer keeps: (1/2) · 15 · 9 = 67.5 metayer's gain: 67.5 − 90 = −22.5 metayer's break-even: 90 / ((1/2) · 15) = 12 years

The farmer improves and the metayer does not, on the same land, the same improvement and the same term. Give the metayer twenty-seven years and he gains 112.50, which is why Smith writes that it could never be his interest to improve at his own expense: the share, not the land, is what stops him, and the term has to be long to make up for it.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the argument is about land rather than about who holds itIn the lab the improvement, its cost and its yield are the same for every tenure. Only the share and the term differ. Smith's claim is that the holder's reason to improve depends on what he is left with, and the four rows show it.
2
Reading Smith as saying slavery is unprofitable everywhereHe says its work is dearest in the end, and then says it can bear the expense of sugar and tobacco and not of corn. The lesson reports his claim about corn and about what the planters found. Historians of the American South and of the West Indies have since argued about how profitable slavery was; the lab has nothing to say on it.
3
Treating the cost and the yield as Smith'sThe 90 pounds and the 15 are written for the lesson. The nine and twenty-seven years are Smith's, for France. The point of the preset is the form of the comparison, not the amounts.

Note: The lab assumes the occupier sees the whole return, with no risk, no discount and no chance of being turned out sooner. Smith's own argument is looser and richer, and covers ornament, pride, habit and the disposition of the great. Later writers have asked how well it fits the evidence. Historians of agrarian change have found great estates that improved and small holders who did not, and the sharecropping literature of the twentieth century, from Alfred Marshall's discussion of the metayer to later work on incentives, takes up this passage. Capital's lesson Genesis of the Capitalist Farmer reads the farmer's rise from the other side.

Summary

After Rome fell, Smith says, the land of Western Europe was engrossed by a few great proprietors, and the laws of primogeniture and entail kept it so. He then goes through the ways land was occupied, slaves, metayers who farm for half the produce, and farmers who rent for a fixed sum, and asks of each whether the occupier had a reason to improve. Work that question as arithmetic: an improvement that costs a sum and adds a yearly produce, under four tenures and three terms.

Key ideas

  • Primogeniture and entail. When land was power, it was kept whole by descending to one heir, the eldest son, and entails bound it to a line. Smith says the reasons vanished when the lords stopped being petty princes and the laws stayed, and that a great proprietor “seldom” improves, having neither the leisure, the inclination, nor the habit of small savings and gains.
  • Who bears the cost, who gets the return. A slave acquires nothing but his maintenance; a metayer keeps half the produce, and the landlord, who lays out nothing on an improvement, takes the other half of what it yields; a tenant farmer on a lease keeps all the produce for the term but pays the cost of improving. Each holder's reason to improve is what he gets back from it.
  • Security of the lease. A farmer improves only if the lease is long enough to repay him. Smith praises England, where the tenant's security is equal to the proprietor's, and finds a French lease of nine years, lately lengthened to twenty-seven, too short.
