Optimal Binary Search Trees

Section 15.5 builds a binary search tree for keys whose search probabilities are known, including the probabilities of unsuccessful searches that end at dummy leaves. Every subtree holds a contiguous range of keys, an optimal tree has optimal subtrees, and the expected cost of a range obeys a recurrence over its possible roots, with the range's total probability added at each level. OPTIMAL-BST fills the e, w and root tables by range length in Θ(n³). Fill a four-key table by hand, then let the lab check its answer against every tree.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas: the cost being minimized, the shape of a subproblem, and the term that accounts for depth.

1

Expected search cost

Keys k₁ < ⋯ < kₙ are searched with probabilities p₁, …, pₙ, and dummy keys d₀, …, dₙ, standing for the gaps between and beyond the keys, with probabilities q₀, …, qₙ; all of them sum to 1. A search costs the depth of the node it ends at plus one, and the expected cost of a tree is the probability-weighted sum.

2

Ranges as subproblems

Any subtree of a binary search tree holds a contiguous range of keys kᵢ, …, kⱼ and has exactly the dummies dᵢ₋₁, …, dⱼ as its leaves. e[i, j] is the least expected cost of a tree on that range; the empty range j = i − 1 holds the single dummy dᵢ₋₁.

3

The weight w(i, j)

The total probability of a range, pᵢ + ⋯ + pⱼ plus qᵢ₋₁ + ⋯ + qⱼ. When a subtree becomes a child of a new root every node in it moves one level deeper, so its expected cost rises by exactly its weight.

The material

The cost, the four steps, and one table by hand

Read Section 15.5 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the cost and the recurrence in its own words, fills a four-key table by hand, and then runs the lab, which checks the table against every tree.

“This example shows that an optimal binary search tree is not necessarily a tree whose overall height is smallest.” The authors say it early in Section 15.5, and add that putting the most probable key at the root does not work either. Both shortcuts fail, and the section replaces them with the chapter's method. This lesson reads it, the last of Chapter 15's examples.

The motivating example is a translation program that looks up every word of a text in a search tree: a balanced tree guarantees O(lg n) per lookup, but frequent words should sit near the root, and some words of the text are not in the tree at all. So the model has keys with probabilities pᵢ and dummy keys with probabilities qᵢ for the unsuccessful searches. Each key is an internal node and each dummy a leaf, every search ends at one of them, and the probabilities sum to 1.

Definition · Expected search cost

With a search costing the depth of the node found plus one, the expected cost of a tree T is the sum of (depth(kᵢ) + 1)·pᵢ over the keys plus the sum of (depth(dᵢ) + 1)·qᵢ over the dummies, which equals 1 plus the probability-weighted depths. An optimal binary search tree minimizes it.

Exhaustive search is out: there are as many trees as binary trees with n nodes, which Problem 12-4 shows is Ω(4ⁿ/n^(3/2)). Step 1 is the observation that a subtree holds a contiguous range of keys and the dummies between and around them, and the cut-and-paste argument: if an optimal tree had a subtree on kᵢ, …, kⱼ that was not optimal for that range, pasting in a better one would lower the whole tree's cost. An optimal tree on the range has some root kᵣ, an optimal left subtree on kᵢ, …, kᵣ₋₁ and an optimal right subtree on kᵣ₊₁, …, kⱼ, and trying every r finds it. A root at an end of the range has an empty subtree on that side, which by convention holds one dummy.

Theorem · The recurrence for e[i, j]

e[i, i − 1] = qᵢ₋₁, and for i ≤ j, e[i, j] is the minimum over r from i to j of e[i, r − 1] + e[r + 1, j] + w(i, j). The root's own pᵣ and the one level each subtree descends add up to exactly the range's weight. root[i, j] records an r that attains the minimum.

Step 3 is OPTIMAL-BST, which has the matrix chain's shape: ranges instead of subchains, filled by length. Its tables are indexed so that the empty ranges fit, e[1..n + 1, 0..n], and a third table w saves recomputing weights, since w[i, j] = w[i, j − 1] + pⱼ + qⱼ in constant time. The loops nest three deep and the procedure takes Θ(n³). Building the tree from root is left as Exercise 15.5-1, and Exercise 15.5-4 points to a faster method due to Knuth.

Here is a four-key instance of this lesson's own, with every probability a whole number of fortieths: p = 7, 6, 5, 10 and q = 3, 2, 2, 2, 3. Key k₄ is the most likely search. The table below gives w and e in fortieths, filled by range length.

range w e root (all in 40ths) 1..1 12 17 k1 2..2 10 14 k2 3..3 9 13 k3 4..4 15 20 k4 1..2 20 37 k1 2..3 17 32 k2 3..4 22 38 k4 1..3 27 57 k2 2..4 30 64 k3 1..4 40 95 k2 e[1, 4] = 95/40 = 19/8

So the least expected cost is 19/8 = 2.375, with k₂ at the root, k₁ to its left, and k₄ to its right with k₃ below it. The likeliest key, k₄, is one level down; the best tree with k₄ at the root costs 100/40 = 2.5. The reason is the dummies and the smaller keys: three keys and four dummies lie to the left of k₄, and a root at the right end pushes all of them down.

Now the lab. It runs OPTIMAL-BST on the probabilities you type, each given as a whole-number weight divided by a common scale (the lab refuses fractions such as 7/40, so the four-key instance is typed as weights over the scale 40), and prints the expected cost e[1, n], the optimal tree in nested text, the root table row by row, and a brute-force check over every tree up to seven keys. A tie keeps the smallest root.

The preset four-keys is the table above: 19/8, the tree k2(k1(d0 d1) k4(k3(d2 d3) d4)), root rows 1: 1 1 2 2; 2: 2 2 3; 3: 3 4; 4: 4, and 14 trees tried by brute force, with one cheapest. three-keys types decimals, p = 0.2 0.1 0.25 and q = 0.1 0.15 0.1 0.1, and the optimal root is k₂, the least likely key, at expected cost 47/20 = 2.35: its dummies carry the weight. seven-equal gives seven keys equal probability and no misses, and the answer is the perfectly balanced tree at 17/7, the only cheapest of 429. eight-keys runs at n = 8, where the table answers 29/10 and the brute force declines to enumerate 1,430 trees.

That last preset marks the boundary of what a check can do. Up to seven keys the lab can compare the table with every tree and they agree; at eight it can only fill the table. The table is right at every n because of the cut-and-paste argument, which the agreement at small n illustrates and does not prove.

Method

Filling an optimal-tree table by hand

Four steps fill and read the tables; the fifth is an instance of your own.

1

Scale to whole numbers

Multiply every p and q by a common denominator so the arithmetic is in whole numbers. Write e[i, i − 1] = w[i, i − 1] = qᵢ₋₁ for the empty ranges.

2

Fill the weights

For each range, w[i, j] = w[i, j − 1] + pⱼ + qⱼ. The whole range must come to the scale, which checks your arithmetic.

3

Fill e by range length

For each range try every root r: add e[i, r − 1], e[r + 1, j] and w[i, j], keep the smallest in e and its r in root, the smaller r on a tie.

4

Build the tree from root

The whole range's root is root[1, n]; its left subtree is the range to its left, whose root is read from the table, and so on down to the dummies.

5

Do it on an instance of your own

Choose three keys and four dummies with weights summing to 20, fill the tables by hand, then type the weights and the scale 20 into the lab and compare the cost, the tree and the brute-force check.

The cell e[2, 4] of the four-key instance

In fortieths, the range k₂, k₃, k₄ has weight w[2, 4] = 30. The cells it reads are already filled: e[2, 1] = 2, e[3, 4] = 38, e[2, 2] = 14, e[4, 4] = 20, e[2, 3] = 32 and e[5, 4] = 3.

r = 2: e[2,1] + e[3,4] + 30 = 2 + 38 + 30 = 70 r = 3: e[2,2] + e[4,4] + 30 = 14 + 20 + 30 = 64 r = 4: e[2,3] + e[5,4] + 30 = 32 + 3 + 30 = 65 e[2,4] = 64, root[2,4] = 3

The middle key wins by one fortieth over k₄. The same weight 30 is added for every candidate root, so it never decides the choice; it is the price of the whole range moving one level down when it becomes a subtree.

Common mistakes

1
Putting the likeliest key at the rootIt sounds right and can be wrong, because a root at one end of the order pushes every other key and most dummies down a level. In the four-key instance the likeliest key k₄ at the root gives 100/40 at best, against 95/40 for k₂; in the lab's three-key preset the optimal root is the least likely key.
2
Leaving out the unsuccessful searchesThe dummies dᵢ are leaves, and the searches that end at them have cost too. Without them the weight of a range is too small and the tree favours the keys wrongly. In the three-key preset the q's are 45 hundredths of all searches, and they are what put k₂ at the root.
3
Forgetting the weight term in the recurrencee[r + 1, j] is the cost of the right subtree as a tree of its own; under a new root it is one level deeper, which costs w(r + 1, j) more, and the same holds on the left. Together with the root's pᵣ these add to w(i, j). Leaving the term out computes the cost of a forest, not a tree.
