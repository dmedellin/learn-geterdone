Amortized Analysis and Dynamic Tables

Chapter 17 bounds the worst-case cost of a whole sequence of operations and divides by its length. Aggregate analysis sums the sequence directly; the accounting method overcharges some operations and stores the surplus as credit on objects; the potential method stores it as a function of the whole structure. All three are applied to a stack with MULTIPOP and to a binary counter, and the potential method then designs a table that doubles when full and halves when a quarter full, with constant amortized cost per operation. Work the counter and the stack by hand, then let the lab price a table operation by operation.

Chapter 17 in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas: what an amortized bound promises, and the two ways of storing prepaid work.

1

Amortized cost

A bound on the total cost of any sequence of n operations, spread over the operations. It is a worst-case guarantee about sequences: no probability is involved, which is what separates it from average-case analysis. A single operation may cost far more than its amortized cost.

2

Credit

In the accounting method each operation is charged an amortized cost; the excess over its actual cost is stored as credit on particular objects and spent later. The total credit must never be negative, so that the charges bound the actual costs for every prefix of every sequence.

3

Potential

In the potential method the prepaid work belongs to the structure as a whole: a function Φ of the structure's state. An operation's amortized cost is its actual cost plus the change in Φ. If Φ starts at 0 and never goes negative, the amortized total bounds the actual total.

The material

Three methods on two examples, then the table

Read Chapter 17, sections 17.1 to 17.4, in your own copy first. The lesson sets out each method in its own words on the counter and the stack, works both by hand, and then lets the lab price a dynamic table one operation at a time.

Chapter 17 opens by separating its subject from average-case analysis, since no probability enters it: “an amortized analysis guarantees the average performance of each operation in the worst case.” Its average is over a sequence of operations, not over random inputs, and the guarantee holds for every sequence. The authors add a practical point. The charges and credits of an analysis are bookkeeping for the proof and have no place in the code itself. This lesson reads the whole chapter, sections 17.1 to 17.4.

Section 17.1 is aggregate analysis: show that n operations cost at most T(n) in total, and charge each T(n)/n. The first example is a stack with PUSH and POP, cost 1 each, and MULTIPOP(S, k), which pops min(s, k) objects from a stack of s and costs that much. A single MULTIPOP can cost O(n), so a careless bound on n operations is O(n²). But an object can be popped at most once for each time it was pushed, so all the pops together, inside MULTIPOP or not, number at most the pushes, at most n: the sequence costs O(n), and each operation O(1) amortized.

The second example is a k-bit binary counter with INCREMENT, whose cost is the number of bits it flips. One increment can flip all k bits, so n increments cost O(nk) by the careless bound. Counted bit by bit, bit 0 flips every time, bit 1 every second time, and bit i ⌊n/2ⁱ⌋ times in n increments from zero, so the total is less than n·(1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ⋯) = 2n.

Section 17.2 is the accounting method. Charge each operation an amortized cost; when it exceeds the actual cost, leave the difference as credit on objects in the structure, and let later operations spend it. The one rule is that total credit never goes negative, for every sequence. For the stack, charge 2 for a push, 1 to pay for it and 1 left on the pushed object, and nothing for POP or MULTIPOP, which pay with the credit on the objects they remove. For the counter, charge 2 to set a bit to 1 and leave a dollar on that bit; resetting it is paid by the dollar. INCREMENT sets at most one bit, so its amortized cost is at most 2.

Theorem · The potential method

Let Dᵢ be the structure after the i-th operation, cᵢ that operation's actual cost and Φ a potential function. The amortized cost is ĉᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁), and the amortized costs of n operations sum to the actual costs plus Φ(Dₙ) − Φ(D₀), since the middle terms telescope. If Φ(Dᵢ) ≥ Φ(D₀) for every i, the amortized total bounds the actual total.

Section 17.3 applies it twice. For the stack, let Φ be the number of objects: a push raises it by 1, amortized cost 2; a MULTIPOP of k′ objects lowers it by k′, amortized cost 0. For the counter, let Φ be the number of 1 bits: an increment that resets tᵢ bits costs at most tᵢ + 1 and changes Φ by at most 1 − tᵢ, so it costs at most 2. The potential method also handles a counter that does not start at zero: with b₀ ones at the start and bₙ at the end, the actual total is at most 2n − bₙ + b₀, which is O(n) once n is Ω(k).

Section 17.4 turns to a table that grows and shrinks. With insertions only, the common rule is to double the table when it is full, copying every item; the load factor, items over slots, then stays at least ½. The i-th insertion costs i when i − 1 is a power of 2 and 1 otherwise, and n insertions cost less than n + 2n = 3n by aggregate analysis. The accounting version charges 3: one to insert the item, one to move it at the next expansion, and one to move an item that has already moved once. The potential version uses Φ = 2·num − size, which is 0 just after an expansion and equals num just before one, exactly enough to pay for the copying; every insertion then has amortized cost 3.

Deletions bring a trap. Halving the table whenever it falls below half full keeps the load factor at least ½ but lets a sequence of insert, delete, delete, insert, insert at the boundary expand and contract again and again, each time copying Θ(n) items, Θ(n) amortized per operation. The authors' remedy is to halve only when the table falls below a quarter full, and to use a potential that is 0 at load factor ½ and grows toward both ends: 2·num − size at load factor ½ or more, size/2 − num below ½ (equation 17.6). It reaches num both when the table is full and when it is a quarter full, so either resize is prepaid. An insertion then costs at most 3 amortized and a deletion at most 2 in the cases the section works, the remaining case being Exercise 17.4-2.

Now the lab, which runs the table with doubling when full and halving when a deletion leaves it under a quarter full, freeing it when empty, and prints the actual total, the bound of 3 an insertion and 2 a deletion, Φ at the start and end, the amortized total and the largest amortized cost of each kind, the amortized total less the actual, the final count, size and load factor, and the expansions, contractions and items moved. Its table lists each operation with its actual cost, Φ after it and its amortized cost.

The preset boundary is the trap above, played against the quarter rule: eight insertions fill a table of 8, a ninth expands it, and then two deletions and two insertions take turns, ending on one insertion. Twenty operations cost 35 in all, against a bound of 54; Φ ends where it started, at 0; there are 4 expansions and no contraction. Played by hand under the half rule, the same sequence costs 72, because every round of delete, delete, insert, insert after the first expansion costs 1 + 8 + 1 + 9 = 19 instead of 4, and the closing delete, delete, insert costs 10 instead of 3. With the nine opening insertions at 24 under either rule, that is 24 + 19 + 19 + 10 = 72. grow makes 33 insertions: actual 96, bound 99, amortized 98 because Φ ends at 2, with 6 expansions moving 63 items. fill-and-empty inserts 16 items and deletes them all: actual 51 against a bound of 80, with two contractions on the way down and the table freed at the end.

Every one of these counts sits under its bound, and the lab prints the largest amortized cost of any single insertion as 3 and of any deletion as 2. Those maxima are about the sequences typed. That no sequence can do worse is the case analysis of Section 17.4, which the lab runs one case at a time and cannot run for all.

Exercise

A 3-bit counter starts at 6 (110) and is incremented 5 times, wrapping past 7 to 0. Count the bits flipped by each increment, the total, b₀ and b₅, and check the total against 2n − bₙ + b₀. Then explain why the bound needs the term b₀ at all.

Method

Running the three methods by hand

Four steps on one sequence; the fifth is a sequence of your own.

1

Write the actual cost of each operation

For each operation in the sequence, write what it actually costs: bits flipped, objects popped, items inserted and moved.

2

Bound the total by aggregate counting

Find a reason the total is small: each object popped at most once per push, each bit flipping half as often as the one below, each item moved a bounded number of times. Divide by n.

3

Charge and keep the books

Assign each kind of operation an amortized charge, place the surplus as credit on objects, and check after every operation that no credit is negative.

4

Choose Φ and check it

Write a potential that is 0 at the start and never negative, compute cᵢ + Φᵢ − Φᵢ₋₁ for each operation, and confirm the amortized total is the actual total plus Φₙ − Φ₀.

5

Do it on a sequence of your own

Write a sequence of 15 to 30 table insertions and deletions, compute each operation's actual cost and Φ by hand, then type it into the lab and compare your totals with its actual and amortized totals.

Common mistakes

1
Reading amortized cost as average-case costNothing is random. An amortized bound says every sequence of n operations costs at most so much, so it is a worst-case statement about sequences. Average-case analysis assumes a distribution over inputs and bounds an expectation.
2
Thinking a cheap amortized cost means every operation is cheapAn expansion of a full table of 32 items costs 33, and its amortized cost is 3. The bound is on the total: in the lab's grow preset 33 insertions cost 96 though one of them alone cost 33. A program that cannot tolerate one slow operation needs a worst-case bound per operation.
3
Letting the credit or the potential go negativeCharges that undercharge early and promise to repay later do not bound the actual cost of the prefix that stops before repayment. The accounting method requires non-negative credit at all times, and the potential method Φ(Dᵢ) ≥ Φ(D₀) for every i, not only at the end of the sequence.
