Integrating Infosec into the Delivery Lifecycle

Chapter 6 asks whether security slows delivery down or helps it. Read what the authors mean by shifting left, why they say the work has to move from security teams to the developers, and what they found about the time spent fixing security issues; then price one team's findings by the stage at which they were caught.

Shifting left on security in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Shifting left

Bringing information security into the work of delivery itself, rather than leaving it as a stage after development is over. The picture is a timeline running left to right from design to release: the work moves toward the left.

2

Preapproved tools

Libraries, packages, toolchains and processes that the security team has already approved and that developers and operations can pick up without asking. The authors present them as the way to make the secure choice the simple one.

3

Remediation hours

The hours a team spends fixing a security finding after it has been found. The lab adds them up by the stage at which each finding was caught and sets them beside the team's other work.

The material

The chapter's argument, then the count

Read Chapter 6 in your own copy first; it is short. The lesson sets out its argument in order, then prices a small team's security findings by the stage at which they were found.

“Our research shows that building security into software development not only improves delivery performance but also improves security quality.” That is the claim of Chapter 6, Integrating Infosec into the Delivery Lifecycle, and it answers a research question the preface lists for the 2016 survey year: does bringing security into the work speed the process up or hold it back? The chapter has three parts, a section on shifting left, a box on compliance in the federal government of the United States, and a short section on the Rugged movement. This lesson reads all three.

The problem comes first. The authors start from the DevOps movement's own aim, which was to stop developers and operations throwing work over a wall at each other, and note that the same habit appears wherever functions in the delivery chain fail to work together. Security is their example. Infosec teams are often thinly staffed: they quote James Wickett's ratio, from 2014, of about one infosec person to ten infrastructure people to a hundred developers in large companies. The team is usually brought in at the end of the lifecycle, when a change is painful and expensive to make, and many developers do not know the common risks, such as those on the OWASP Top 10.

Their answer is to shift left. The chapter lists three parts to it. Every major feature gets a security review, arranged so that development does not slow down. Security takes part in the whole lifecycle, from development through operations: the infosec experts help design applications, come to demonstrations and comment, and see that security features are covered by the automated tests. And the secure route is made the easy one for developers, by supplying libraries, packages, toolchains and processes that security has already approved and that are simple to adopt. The authors report that teams that do this are better at continuous delivery, which in turn helps delivery performance.

The authors give two reasons for the shift in who does the work. It is easier to equip the builders to get it right than to inspect nearly finished systems for large architectural defects that mean substantial rework. And infosec teams do not have the capacity to review every deployment once deployments are frequent; in many organizations, they say, security and compliance is a major bottleneck on the way from finished code to live. The chapter adds a side benefit, better communication between the teams. The box on federal systems makes the same point at the scale of a government: a platform built by a small team of civil servants, cloud.gov, takes care of most of the required controls at the platform level, so that systems on it reach production in weeks and not months.

Then the finding the lab is built around. The authors report that high performers spent 50 percent less time than low performers on fixing security problems. Read it as the book's figure: it is a comparison between the groups of teams the survey found in the years it covers, and the measure is what respondents reported. It does not say that every team will halve its time, and the chapter does not give a cost for fixing a finding at any stage.

The chapter ends with the Rugged movement and two more names for DevOps with security in it. DevSecOps is credited to several people, among them Topo Pal of Capital One and Shannon Lietz of Intuit; Rugged DevOps, from Josh Corman and James Wickett, joins DevOps to the Rugged Manifesto, whose first claim is that the developer's code is rugged, built to face attack and carry on. The authors take one point from it, in line with DevOps: being rugged is everybody's responsibility.

Now the count. The lab takes a team's year of work as records of a kind of work and its hours, with a stage on the security records, and splits the total by kind. The team is invented: 800 hours of new work, 100 unplanned and 140 rework. It found twelve security issues, and the lesson states what it cost to fix one at each stage: 2 hours at design, 4 at build, 12 at test and 40 after release. Those costs are the lesson's own, to make the arithmetic visible; the book gives none. On the preset late the team found 1 issue at build, 3 at test and 8 after release, which is 4, 36 and 320 hours: 360 hours of security remediation in a total of 1,400, about 25.7 percent, and new work is 800 of the 1,400.

The preset early is the same twelve issues found earlier, as a team that had shifted left might find them: 5 at design, 4 at build, 2 at test and 1 after release. That is 10, 16, 24 and 40 hours, 90 in all. The total falls to 1,130 hours, security is about 8 percent of it, and new work's share rises from about 57.1 percent to about 70.8. The 270 hours that are freed are the arithmetic of the costs the lesson chose; the lab cannot say what finding issues earlier takes, which is the training, the tools and the time of the infosec experts at design. The tile for hours by stage shows the split: build 4, test 36 and release 320 on late, design 10, build 16, test 24 and release 40 on early. The preset quarter reaches the same place another way: it keeps the late records and scales security to a quarter, which frees 270 hours that go to new work, so new work is 1,070 of 1,400 hours, 107/140, about 76.4 percent. The tiles for hours freed and for the shares after the scaling are the ones that preset fills in.

Exercise

  • A team's year: new, 600; unplanned, 60; rework, 90, and ten security issues.
  • Costs per issue: design 3 hours, build 6, test 15, after release 45.
  • Found late: 2 at build, 3 at test, 5 after release. Found early: 4 at design, 3 at build, 2 at test, 1 after release.

Work out the hours of security remediation for the late team and for the early team, and the hours freed. Then type the records into the lab, one record for each stage with its hours, to check your count.

Whether your own team would find its issues earlier, and at what price, is yours to answer. The next lesson, Lean Management Practices, moves from security to the way work is managed, and starts with limiting work in progress.

Method

Pricing security findings by stage

Four steps, the last of them on a team of your own.

1

List the findings with their stages

Write down each security issue the team fixed in a period, and the stage at which it was found: design, build, test or after release.

2

Put an hour cost on each fix

Take the hours from your own records where you have them. Where you do not, state a cost per stage as an assumption, as the lesson does, and keep it separate from anything the book reports.

3

Total by stage and by kind of work

Add the hours of each stage, then set the total beside the team's other work. The share that security takes is the figure to watch.

4

Move the findings and recount

On your own team, type the real records into the lab, then move some findings one stage earlier and recount. The difference is what the stated costs make of the move, not a forecast.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking shifting left means security is the developers' job aloneThe chapter moves who does the reviewing, not whether infosec takes part. Infosec experts still contribute to designing applications, attend demonstrations and give feedback, and they supply the preapproved tools. What changes is that the people building the software are given the means to build security in.
2
Reading the 50 percent as the saving your team will seeThe figure compares high and low performers across the survey, in the years it covers. It is not a price list: the chapter gives no cost for fixing an issue at any stage, and the lab's costs are the lesson's own assumptions, chosen to show the arithmetic.
3
Treating the review at the end as the safe place for itThe authors give two reasons against it: it is hard to inspect nearly finished systems for large architectural defects without substantial rework, and infosec teams cannot review every release once deployments are frequent. They describe security and compliance as a bottleneck in many organizations.
