Illusions Created by Competition

To a business, wages, interest and rent are costs fixed in advance, and the price must cover them. Marx argues that this is the real movement seen upside down. Run his own example, 400c + 100v + 150s with wages raised by 50, and see the value stay at 650 while profit and rent shrink; then try a rent rise and a smaller wage rise.

The value and its parts

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Chapter 50 is about an appearance, and Marx argues that the appearance is not a mistake anyone makes by carelessness. Three ideas.

1

The value exists first

The labour added in a year fixes the value product. Wages, profit and rent divide it; their sizes are set by different laws, but always inside that limit.

2

Price added up

The other view, Smith's natural price, treats wages, profit and rent as separate magnitudes that are added to make the price. Marx argues it explains each part by prices that contain the others, and so goes round in a circle.

3

Why it looks that way

Wages are agreed before the work is done, interest and rent by contract before the sale. To every capitalist they are costs given in advance, and so they appear to make the price rather than to divide it.

The material

The passage, then Marx's example and his reasons

The passage is Marx's summary of the chapter's argument, from its middle. His worked example comes earlier, and the reasons the illusion is necessary come after.

Volume III, Chapter 50. “In reality the value of commodities is the magnitude which exists first, and it comprises the sum of the total values of wages, profit and rent, whatever may be their relative magnitudes. In the wrong conception, wages, profit and rent are three independent magnitudes of value, whose total magnitude is supposed to produce the magnitude of the value of a commodity, to limit and to determine it.”

You know the wrong conception, because it is how prices are usually explained. Wages go up, so the firm's costs go up, so prices go up. Adam Smith put it carefully: a commodity's natural price is the sum of the natural rates of wages, profit and rent needed to bring it to market, and the market price gravitates towards it. Marx gives it its French name too, the prix nécessaire. It is the view of every business that has ever priced a job, and Marx's claim is that it reverses the order of things.

His example is a capital of 500 producing 400c + 100v + 150s = 650, with the 150 of surplus value divided into 75 of profit and 75 of rent. The labour paid with £100 has made a new value of £250. In a ten-hour day the worker works four hours for himself and six for the capitalist. Then wages rise to 150 for the same labour. The new value is still 250, because the same labour has been done, so the product is still worth 650: 400c + 150v + 100s. Marx divides the smaller surplus value, “say”, into 45 of profit and 55 of rent. The worker now works six hours for himself and four for the capitalist.

He then works out the rates, and the reader who checks finds one slip. On a capital advanced of 550, profit of 45 is 8 2/11 per cent, rent of 55 is 10 per cent, and the whole surplus value 18 2/11 per cent, as he says. Wages of 150 are 150/550 = 3/11 of the capital, 27 3/11 per cent; the Kerr text prints 27 8/11. The rate of surplus value falls from 150 to 66 2/3 per cent and the rate of profit from 30 to 18 2/11 per cent, so the rate of profit falls more than the mass of surplus value and less than its rate. Marx sets the two cases side by side in a small table on page 996 of the Kerr edition: product 650 and new value 250 in both. In the scan its rate columns read 80 per cent for the first rate of gross profit and sixty-six and one eighth for the second rate of surplus value, where his text has 30 and 66 2/3. The print is worn, and the 8s may be broken 3s, which would still leave the second figure wrong; the figures above are the text's. None of this moves the 650.

The lab's wage-rise preset is Marx's example. Wages go from £100 to £150 and the profit of enterprise tile from £75 to £50. The lab keeps rent at half of s, so rent falls to £50 too, where Marx's text splits the 100 as 45 and 55; the pair sums to £100 either way. Marx does not divide profit into interest here, so the preset gives interest no share and the profit of enterprise tile carries the whole profit.

Marx then says when a wage rise does go with a dearer product. If labour becomes less productive, each piece contains more labour and costs more, and wages may rise with it; but then the rise in wages “is not the cause, but rather a result of a change in the value of the commodities”. And if the fall in productivity is in agriculture, so that only the worker's food gets dearer, the yarn's value does not change at all; the cotton spinner pays more wages out of the same 650. He grants, too, the case taken up in “Wage Changes and Prices of Production”: a general rise in wages lowers the rate of profit and so raises some prices of production and lowers others.

Next he takes the adding-up view at its word and shows it going round in circles. Wages are the price of the necessaries the worker needs; the price of those necessaries is made of wages, profit and rent; so “The price of labor is determined by itself.” Profit fares no better. Competition can equalise rates of profit, but it cannot say why the equal rate is 10 per cent rather than 100. Marx's verdict, at his most sardonic, is that “competition has to shoulder the duty of explaining all inexplicable ideas of the economists, whereas the economists should rather explain competition.”

He adds a small sum to show where the additions really come from. Let wages be 100, profit 10 per cent of wages and rent 15 per cent, so the price is 100 + 10 + 15 = 125. If everyone sells at 125 what cost 100 in wages, every buyer pays the mark-up that every seller gains, “for circulation can never give what it did not first receive”. The 25 has to come out of the product: the worker gets 4/5 of it, the capitalist 2/25 and the landlord 3/25. Raise wages to 110 and the same percentages make 137 1/2, which is the adding-up theory's prediction that every price rises with wages.

Volume III, Chapter 50. “A given figure of 100 always remains the same, whether it is divided into 50 + 50, or into 20 + 70 + 10, or into 40 + 30 + 30.”

That is the lab's other two presets. In rent-rise, an illustrative value product of £200 has its rent raised from 3/10 to 1/2 of the surplus value: rent goes to £50, interest stays at £20, profit of enterprise falls to £30, and the price has no reason to move. In fixed-value, wages on the same £200 rise by £20 to £120; rent, interest and profit of enterprise keep their shares of a smaller s and become £24, £16 and £40. Together they lost £20, exactly what wages gained.

Why, then, does everyone see it the other way round? Marx's answer takes up the rest of the chapter. Even with competition removed, he argues, the illusion would remain, because “the wages are stipulated by contract, before the value equivalent to them has been produced”. Interest is fixed when the loan is made and rent when the lease is signed. To the capitalist each is a cost given in advance, and the price must cover them all; his own profit of enterprise is whatever is left. He sees the parts first, so they look like the makers of the whole.

It goes so far that people who are not capitalists are counted in the same terms. A small farmer who works his own land is treated as three people: “To himself as a wage worker he pays his wages, to himself as a capitalist he turns over his profit, and to himself as a landlord he pays his rent.” Marx says this is not wholly wrong, in a society where capital is the general form, and that it strengthens the illusion that these forms are natural to every kind of production.

A later result bears on this from another direction. Piero Sraffa, in Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities of 1960, derived the same inverse movement of wages and the rate of profit from the physical inputs and outputs of production, with no labour values at all, so the result Marx relies on here does not need his theory of value. And in an economy with paper money, a general rise in money wages can raise the price level, which is a question about money that the lab, reckoning in value, does not model.

If the division into wages, profit and rent is a division of something given, it is fair to ask whether the division itself is natural. The next lesson, “Distribution Relations and Production Relations”, asks what changes if the relations of production change.

Method

Dividing a fixed value product

Four steps for any change in wages or in the shares of surplus value.

1

Fix the value product

With the same labour done, v + s does not change. Write it down before anything moves.

2

Move wages

Add the change to v. Surplus value is the value product minus the new wages, so s moves by the same amount the other way.

3

Divide the new surplus value

Apply the shares of rent and interest to the new s; profit of enterprise is the remainder.

4

Check the sum and the price

The four revenues still add to the value product, and c + v + s, the product's value, is unchanged. Adding the new wages to the old profit and rent gives a figure the labour never produced.

A wage rise of twenty pounds

Take the fixed-value preset. The value product is £200: £100 of wages and £100 of surplus value, with rent 3/10 of s and interest 1/5. Wages rise by £20 for the same work. You want the four revenues after the rise.

value product: 100 + 100 = £200, unchanged wages: 100 + 20 = £120 surplus value: 200 − 120 = £80 rent: 3/10 · 80 = £24 interest: 1/5 · 80 = £16 profit of enterprise: 80 − 24 − 16 = £40

Rent, interest and profit of enterprise were £30, £20 and £50; they are now £24, £16 and £40, £20 less in all. Added up as independent parts, £120 of wages and the old £100 of surplus value would make £220, a value nobody's labour produced.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking a wage rise raises prices because wages are a component added to themIt is Smith's natural price and every business's costing, and for one firm facing given wages it describes what it sees. On the chapter's accounting the wage-rise preset refutes it: wages go from £100 to £150, profit and rent together from £150 to £100, and the product stays at 650, because the same labour was done.
2
Explaining the price of labour by the price of necessariesIf wages are the price of the worker's necessaries, and those are priced at wages plus profit plus rent, the price of labour has been explained by itself. Marx accepts that the value of labour-power is set by the necessaries; what he denies is that wages can then be a separate element added to make every price.
3
Thinking the illusion is just a mistake of the carelessMarx argues it would appear even without competition, because wages, interest and rent are fixed by contract before the value is produced. The appearance is real; what he disputes is the explanation built on it.
