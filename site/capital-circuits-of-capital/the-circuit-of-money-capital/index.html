The Circuit of Money Capital

Volume II opens by following one capital round its whole path: money buys means of production and labour-power, production turns them into a commodity of greater value, and the sale turns that commodity back into money. Trace Marx's spinner through the three stages, give the capital's value in each form, compute the surplus value from M and M′, and then sell less than the whole product to see what the money at the end does and does not show.

The first circuit in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Volume I went inside the factory and assumed the buying and selling around it. Chapter 1 puts the buying and selling back and follows the capital through all of it.

1

Three stages, three forms

In the first stage the capital is money and buys; in the second it is means of production and labour-power at work; in the third it is a product to be sold. Marx calls the three forms money capital, productive capital and commodity capital.

2

Industrial capital

The capital that passes through all three forms and drops each in turn is industrial capital, and “industrial” covers every branch run on a capitalist basis. The three forms are its functions, not three kinds of capital.

3

Produced and realised

Surplus value is produced in the second stage and exists in the product before anything is sold. The sale realises it: turns it, with the capital-value, into money. The two are different events, and the lab separates them.

The material

The formula first, then the three stages in order

The chapter states its formula on its first page. Its verdict on that formula comes near the end, after Marx has walked the spinner's £422 through every stage.

Volume II, Chapter 1. “Hence the formula for the circulation process of money capital is: M-C...P...C'-M', the dots indicating the points where the process of circulation was interrupted, and C' and M' designating C and M increased by surplus value.”

Volume I followed capital into the factory and assumed everything around it: that the capitalist could find labour-power and cotton for sale, and that the yarn would sell. Volume II takes those assumptions one at a time. It begins with the shape of the whole movement, M–C…P…C′–M′, read as money, commodities, production, more commodities, more money. A word on the translation first: Untermann calls this the “circulation” of money capital, and in the next chapter the “rotation” of productive capital. Both render one German word, Kreislauf, which later translations give as circuit, and these lessons use that word.

The first stage is M–C, a purchase, and Marx's figures are the ones the chapter keeps throughout: £422, of which £372 buys means of production and £50 buys labour-power. Marx writes the two purchases M–Pm and M–L, Pm for the means of production and L for labour-power, and the lessons keep his letters. The split is not arbitrary. The cotton, coal and wear of the spindles must be enough to soak up the whole of the labour bought, surplus labour included; too little and some of the labour cannot be used, too much and some of the cotton sits unspun. And the purchase of labour-power is different in kind from the purchase of cotton. “The class relation between the capitalist and the wage laborer is therefore established from the moment that they meet in the act M-L”, because money can buy labour-power only where the people who own it have nothing else to sell.

Marx has a story about that. After the emancipation of the serfs in 1861, Russian landowners who now had to hire their labour complained of two things. They were short of ready money to pay wages before the harvest was sold. And even with money, they could not find enough labourers, because a peasant who still held a share of the village land was not yet a “free” wage-worker. The second complaint is the more telling one: M–C presupposes a class with nothing to sell but its labour-power.

The second stage is …P…, production, and the dots mean that circulation has stopped. The spinners transfer the £372 of means of production into the yarn and add £128 of new value, so 10,000 lb of yarn are worth £500. Of the £128, £50 replaces the wages and £78 is surplus value, “representing an exploitation of 156 per cent”, which you can check: 78/50 = 156/100. All of the increase happens here. Nothing has been sold yet.

The third stage is C′–M′, a sale. Marx asks what makes this ordinary sale a function of capital, and answers that nothing happens inside it: the yarn's value “has not experienced any change of magnitude, but only of form”. What makes the yarn commodity capital is where it came from. It is worth £500 against £422 laid out, and the £78 sits in it already, as 1,560 lb of the 10,000.

Marx then does the arithmetic of a partial sale. Sell 7,440 lb at their value and the capitalist has £372 back, only his constant capital. “If he sells 8,440 pounds of yarn, he recovers only the value of his total capital.” Only the whole 10,000 lb bring the £78 home. The lab runs these sales: the money returned is the product's value times the share sold, and M′ − M is zero at 8,440 lb and negative below it. The marx preset sells everything, £500 back and £78 realised; the partly preset sells 8,440 lb, £422 back and £0 realised; the constant preset sells 7,440 lb, £372 back and −£50; and the unsold preset sells nothing, £0 back and −£422. A negative figure here is not a loss of value. It is capital-value still sitting in the warehouse as yarn.

Once the sale is made, the result looks different from how it was made. In the yarn, the capital and the surplus were distinct parts of a product that came out of production. In £500 of money they are just £422 and £78 side by side, and nothing in the coins says which is which. Marx's smaller example: of £110, any £10 is 1/11 of the sum and also 1/10 of the £100 advanced, and the money carries no mark of either. That is why he calls commodity capital “more rational and less difficult to understand than money-capital, in which every trace of this process has disappeared”.

Volume II, Chapter 1. “The circulation of money-capital is therefore the most one-sided, and thus the most convincing and typical form of the circulation of industrial capital. Its aim and compelling motive, the utilization of value, the making and accumulation of money, is thus most clearly revealed. Buying in order to sell dearer is its slogan.”

One-sided, because it starts and ends in money and so shows only the end in view, more money, with production as the passage in between. Typical, for the same reason: it shows most plainly the motive of the whole business, the making of money. Marx's dry aside is that this is how whole economies sometimes see it too: “All nations with a capitalist mode of production are seized periodically by a feverish attempt to make money without the mediation of the process of production.”

The formula invites a reading Marx wants to block. The earliest mercantilist writers, of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, measured a nation's gain by the gold its trade brought in, and Marx says their “so-called monetary system” read M–C–M′ as “a movement which takes place exclusively in the circulation”, so that it “cannot explain the two phases M-C and C-M' in any other way than by declaring that C is sold above its value”. That the gain is made in selling, by selling dear, is the reading M…M′ invites when it is taken alone, and that is why Marx calls the formula “in a certain sense, deceptive”; the later mercantilists, he adds, at least put production inside the formula. The chapter's answer is the yarn: it was worth £500 before it left the mill, and a sale at value turns £500 of yarn into £500 of money. Whether value comes from labour at all is a separate dispute, set out in Commodities and Money; here the claim is narrower, that on Marx's definitions the sale adds nothing.

One industry fits the formula differently, and it matters later. Transport sells a change of place, which is used up as it is produced, so its circuit is M–C…P–M′, with no product waiting to be sold. The next lesson, “The Circuit of Productive Capital”, starts the same movement from production instead of money and asks what that makes visible.

Method

Tracing a capital round its circuit

Four steps, the same for any industrial capital.

1

Split M

Divide the money advanced into the part that buys means of production and the part that buys labour-power. The two must add up to M; the lab refuses otherwise.

2

Value the product

The product's value is the means of production transferred plus the new value the labour adds. Subtract M from it for the surplus value produced.

3

Sell

M′ is the product's value times the share sold, on the chapter's assumption that everything sells at its value.

4

Compare M′ with M

M′ − M is the surplus value realised. Below the break-even share it is negative: that much capital-value is still in commodity form, not lost.

Sell 8,440 lb instead and M′ is £422, so M′ − M is £0, though the £78 of surplus value is still there in the 1,560 lb unsold. It was produced in the second line and is only realised in the last.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the money at the end is where the surplus is madeIt is the natural reading of M…M′, and the monetary system Marx names made it: the gain is made by selling dear. The unsold preset refutes it. With nothing sold, the table still shows a product worth £500 against £422 advanced, while the tile for money returned is £0. The £78 was produced in the mill; the sale, at value, only changes its form, and at 8,440 lb sold none of it has been realised yet.
2
Treating money, productive and commodity capital as three kinds of businessThey are the forms one industrial capital takes in turn, “special functions of industrial capital, assumed by it seriatim”. The spinner's £422 is money capital on Monday and productive capital once the cotton and the spinners are in the mill. Banks and merchants come later, as these functions made independent.
3
Reading M–L as only a money transactionPaying wages is a purchase like any other in form. Marx's point is that money can buy labour-power on a social scale only where workers are separated from the means of production, which is why the Russian landowners could have money and still find no hands. The relation is in the conditions, not in the money.
