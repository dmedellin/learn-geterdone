The Tacit Cogito

Reading Descartes, I reach a cogito put into words, which the chapter says should read one thinks, therefore one is. Beneath it lies a tacit cogito that words presuppose and that becomes a cogito only when it finds expression. The tacit cogito in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The last third of the chapter is about language. Three ideas carry it.

1

The spoken cogito

The cogito as it is reached by reading Descartes's words or by putting one's own life into concepts. It is real, and it is mediated by language, which Descartes never mentions.

2

The tacit cogito

The presence of oneself to oneself that the spoken cogito presupposes: a comprehensive, inarticulate grasp of oneself and the world, prior to any philosophy, which becomes a cogito only when it finds expression.

3

Originating speech

Speech that brings a thought into existence rather than rendering one already acquired. The chapter distinguishes it from secondary speech, the use of words whose meanings are settled.

The material

The passage first, then the chapter's pages on expression

The passage names the tacit cogito and its limit in two sentences a page apart. The lesson goes back to the chapter's account of speech, then to its reading of the Second Meditation, then to the passage and the chapter's last pages.

Part Three, Chapter 1, pp. 469–470. “Behind the spoken cogito, the one which is converted into discourse and into essential truth, there lies a tacit cogito, myself experienced by myself.” And a page later: “The tacit cogito is a cogito only when it has found expression for itself.” The first sentence is on page 469 of Smith's translation, the second on page 470. Read from the paragraph that begins on page 451, where the chapter turns from geometry to expression, to the end of the chapter on page 475, in your own copy before going on.

The case is a reader with a book. On page 466 the chapter reads the Second Meditation: by following the meaning of the words and the argument, I reach the conclusion that because I think, I am. But I have grasped my thought and my existence only through language, and the conclusion is about a me in idea, anyone's who reflects. The true formula of this cogito, the chapter says, would be “One thinks, therefore one is.” Language has the strange property of making us forget it: my eyes follow the lines and lose sight of them, and that is why Descartes nowhere mentions it.

The chapter has prepared this with an account of speech (pp. 451–458). It is obvious, it says, that “speech cannot be regarded as a mere clothing for thought”, the translation into signs of a meaning already clear to itself; if it were, language could teach us nothing. A good book imposes its meaning on us, and the speaker, too, must be able “to find in his own words more than the thought he was putting into them”, or we would not see thought, even solitary thought, seeking expression so persistently (p. 452). The poet Paul Claudel is quoted for the writer's side of this. Secondary speech renders a thought already acquired; originating speech brings one into existence, and every settled word was once originating.

So ideas are cultural objects, like a church or the Ninth Symphony, and their seeming independence of time is an effect of expression. Once the quadratic equation has appeared, a good or bad later statement of it adds or subtracts nothing, and it lives on among us; but it has its historical and geographical area, and would need fresh acts of expression if its tradition were destroyed. “The non-temporal is the acquired” (p. 457). That sentence is the hinge to the next chapter, on time.

Definition · The tacit cogito

Myself experienced by myself, before and beneath any words: a presence to oneself that the spoken cogito presupposes, that knows itself only under threat, and that is a cogito in the full sense only once expressed.

The lab sets two opponent accounts against the chapter's cases. The names are the lesson's. On the first, thought before language, thought is complete and clear to itself and words are its clothing; this is the view of expression the chapter, on page 452, says is obviously untenable. On the second, language before thought, thought is nothing but words, and consciousness is led by language as the realist believes he is led by the world; the chapter raises that conclusion itself on pages 467–468 and answers: “This would be to forget half the truth.”

The cases, as the lesson codes them, thought-first then language-first. First, Descartes's book: I should be unable even to read it, the chapter says, “were I not, before any speech can begin, in contact with my own life and thought” (p. 468). Thought-first absorbs that; language-first cannot. Second, the speaker who finds in his words more than he put into them (p. 452): language-first absorbs it, and thought-first, for which the meaning was there complete beforehand, cannot. Third, the illusory love of the chapter's earlier pages (pp. 440–443), lived and not known: a thought transparent to itself would have known it, so thought-first fails it, and language-first, which says nothing about feeling, is silent.

The all preset shows the three. The record tile counts, for each account, the observations it absorbs, fails and is silent on: thought before language absorbs 1 of 3 and fails 2; language before thought absorbs 1, fails 1 and is silent on 1. The survivors tile lists the accounts that fail none, and prints none. The split tile lists the observations on which the pair stand y against n, and prints the first two; the third is n against silent, which is not a split. The books preset keeps only the first case, and thought before language survives it alone. The expression preset keeps the second and third, and language before thought survives them. Each survives only the cases chosen for it.

Neither account is the tacit cogito, and the tacit cogito is not a column. It is not a silent voice saying I think before I say it aloud. It does not constitute the world, the word or the word's meaning; it “speaks as we sing when we are happy” (p. 470). The word sleet, the chapter says, I caught on to as one imitates a gesture, as a modulation of my body, never inspected as an idea. The tacit cogito knows itself only in extreme situations, in the dread of death or of another's gaze on me; it is a grasp on the world like the infant's at its first breath, and it waits to be won back by perception and speech.

If ultimate subjectivity cannot think itself the moment it exists, how can it ever do so? The chapter puts the objection to itself (p. 470) and answers that it has withheld from the primordial I only objective, positing thought, the kind that sets an object before itself, and not all self-presence. What am I, then, beneath my particular thoughts? “I am a field, an experience” (p. 473). With my birth something began that can no longer cease to see or not to see, a new possibility of situations, and the cogito retrieves that event.

This is the most disputed idea in the chapter, and among the first to dispute it was Merleau-Ponty himself. In a working note of his last years, published after his death in The Visible and the Invisible (1964), he wrote that the tacit cogito is impossible: to have even the idea of thinking, of returning to oneself, one already needs words. Readers disagree over whether the note retracts the 1945 idea or only the way this chapter states it, as a self-presence that seems to need no world of speech.

The chapter ends with rationality: classical thought explains why appearances agree by a world in itself or an absolute mind, and both explanations borrow their force from the agreement they explain (p. 475). It has restored to the cogito a temporal thickness, and the next chapter asks what time is. The next lesson, “Time Is Not in Things”, begins with a river.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the tacit cogito is a silent inner voice that thinks in words before speakingThe chapter says the opposite. The tacit cogito is not yet a thought in words at all: it speaks as we sing when we are happy, knows itself only under threat, and “is a cogito only when it has found expression for itself”. An inner voice saying I think would already be a spoken cogito, spoken silently. The lab cannot show the difference, which is why the tacit cogito is in the prose and not in the table, where both coded accounts fail.
2
Thinking the chapter puts language before thoughtIt raises that conclusion and rejects it: it would be to forget half the truth. Words such as cogito and sum would be meaningless to me, the chapter says, if I were not already in contact with my own life and thought, and that is why languages can be translated and learned.
3
Taking the table's result for the chapter's verdictSurvivors none is a count over three cases the lesson chose and coded. It shows that each account fails somewhere on that coding; it does not show that the tacit cogito is right, which is never a column and never scored.
