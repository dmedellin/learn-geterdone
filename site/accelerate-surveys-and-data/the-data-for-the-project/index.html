The Data for the Project

Chapter 15 describes the target population, why random sampling was impossible, why snowball sampling fitted, and the three limitations the authors name with their remedies. Read it as the answer to who was asked, then follow twenty invented people from their seeds, and see what a missing seed would have left out.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Snowball sampling

Also called referral sampling: a method in which the first people invited are asked to pass the invitation on, so that the sample grows as it spreads. It is a nonprobability method, because not everyone in the population has a known chance of being asked.

2

Probability sampling

Any method of drawing a sample by random selection, so that every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen. The authors say it is generally preferred and was impossible here, because it needs a list of the whole population and there is no list.

3

Seed and wave

A seed is a person invited directly. Wave 0 is the seeds; wave 1 is those a seed referred, wave 2 those a wave-1 person referred, and so on. The lab counts a person's tree as the seed they descend from.

The material

Who was asked, then the count

Read Chapter 15 in your own copy first; it is short. The lesson sets out the research design and the case for snowball sampling, then follows an invented sample through its waves.

“Snowball sampling is a method well suited for studying specific groups whose populations cannot be easily identified.” Forsgren, Humble and Kim write that in Chapter 15, The Data for the Project, which answers the question Chapter 14 left open: whom the survey asked. This lesson reads the whole chapter, and points at the two pages of Part III's introduction that follow it.

The research began in 2014 and collected data in a cross-sectional design, a single point in time each year, for four years. The target population was people and organizations that knew the word DevOps, or would at least open an invitation that used it. The authors give two reasons: it let them focus on people in the business of developing and delivering software, in any industry that is driven by technology, and on people who already knew terms such as continuous integration, so that a survey did not spend its length on definitions that could make a respondent give up.

The chapter states the trade-off. The group not surveyed, people unfamiliar with these practices, probably performs worse than the authors' lowest performers, so their comparisons are limited and do not show how large a change could be. In return they gain explanatory power by defining the population tightly. The familiarity also called for care in wording: they never asked whether a team practiced continuous integration, a claim respondents might make to look good, but asked about the practices at its core, such as whether automated tests run when code is checked in. The authors believe many findings apply beyond this population, on prior research and experience; that is their belief, and the sample cannot test it.

Next, the sampling method. Probability sampling needs every member of the population to be known and to have an equal chance of being asked, and no exhaustive list of the world's DevOps professionals exists. So the authors invited by email, to their own mailing lists, which included past respondents, Puppet's marketing databases and the databases of Gene Kim's and Jez Humble's readers, and to the mailing lists of professional groups, with particular care to reach groups of underrepresented people in technology. They also posted on social media. Then they asked recipients to refer others.

That last step is snowball sampling, and the chapter gives two reasons it fitted. First, the population is hard or impossible to identify: unlike accountancy or civil engineering, software work has no certifying body with a list, job titles vary or are too generic to select on, and early on many held nontraditional titles. Second, the population is often averse to being studied, after a history of studies of technical workers that led to cuts in the workforce, so a referral lets a colleague vouch for the questions and the researchers.

The chapter then names three limitations and what the authors did. The first is that the people invited first may not represent their communities, and the remedy was an initial set as large and diverse as possible, combining several lists. The second is that the sample is strongly influenced by the initial invitations, a concern if a small group is targeted and the sample grows from it, and the remedy was again a very large and diverse set. The third is that findings may not represent the industry, and the remedies were to engage the community and compare notes, to have outside experts review the hypotheses each year, to read the literature, and to ask for input. A footnote adds a limit of the design: because responses are not linked from year to year, the data cannot be analysed longitudinally, and the authors say they are pursuing that. The lesson Inferential Predictive Analysis, in the previous course, discusses what this means for claims of cause.

Now the count, on twenty invented people. The lab's snowball view takes a list of people, who referred each, and the community each belongs to, here where they work. A person with no referrer is a seed. The preset three-seeds has three seeds: ana from retail, bo from a bank and cy from health. The lab counts 3, 6, 8 and 3 people in waves 0 to 3, 20 in all. Ana's tree holds 8 of the 20, 40 percent; Bo's holds 9, 45 percent; Cy's holds 3, 15 percent. By community the sample is 6 retail, 2 startup, 9 bank and 3 health, which is 30, 10, 45 and 15 percent.

The preset six-seeds invites six people across the same four communities, again 20 people in all. The waves count 6, 8, 5 and 1; no seed's tree holds more than 5, a quarter; and the community shares are 5 retail, 5 bank, 6 health and 4 startup, which is 25, 25, 30 and 20 percent. The sample sizes are equal and the pictures are not. This is the second limitation made visible, and the authors' remedy, more and more varied invitations, is what changed it, not the size of the sample.

The preset without-cy is three-seeds with the seed cy dropped, as if the health community's mailing list had never been used. The lab counts the people lost, cy, ida and quin, leaving 17, and the community with no one left, health. The shares change because the denominator does: bank rises from 45 to 52.9 percent, retail from 30 to 35.3 percent and startup from 10 to 11.8 percent, although no bank, retail or startup person was added.

Read the counts as the authors would, as a reason for caution. The shares are shares of the sample. The population has no list, so nobody can say what the shares of the whole would be, and the lab cannot either; it also records only those who were reached and answered, not those who were asked and did not. Whether a sample resembles a population is something the design argues for, as the chapter does, and does not prove.

By hand, count the people in each wave, the share of the sample in each seed's tree and the share in each group. Then drop seed b and recount the sample and the group shares. Type the records into the lab to check. Say what happened to the west group.

Method

Following a snowball sample

Four steps, the last of them on a sample of your own.

1

Mark the seeds

Find everyone with no referrer. They are wave 0; each later wave is the people referred by the wave before.

2

Count each seed's tree

Follow referrals down from each seed and count the people, the seed included. Divide by the sample size for the seed's share.

3

Count the communities

Tally the group of each person and divide by the sample size. Compare the tallies with the seeds' shares.

4

Drop a seed and recount

Remove one seed and everyone it reached, then recount the sample and the communities. List the communities that vanish and the ones whose shares rise.

ana: dee eli fay, then jon kim lee, then ray ana's tree: 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8, 8 / 20 = 40 percent bo: gus hal, then max ned oli pam, then sue tom bo's tree: 1 + 2 + 4 + 2 = 9, 9 / 20 = 45 percent cy: ida, then quin; tree 1 + 1 + 1 = 3, 15 percent waves: 3 seeds, 6, 8, 3: total 20 drop cy: 20 − 3 = 17; bank 9 / 17 = 52.9 percent

The three trees partition the sample: 8 + 9 + 3 = 20, so the shares add to 100 percent. Dropping a seed removes its whole tree, including people who might have been reached another way; the lab counts only the referrals it is given.

Common mistakes

1
Treating a large snowball sample as a random sample of the industryRandom selection needs a list of everyone, and the chapter says none exists. The more than 23,000 responses are a large nonprobability sample. Its size makes its means precise about the people reached and says nothing about the people never reached. The authors argue for the sample's reach, and the foreword says Fowler would have more confidence once other teams, using different approaches, confirm the reasoning.
2
Believing a big sample makes the first invitations irrelevantThe authors name this limitation: the data are strongly influenced by the initial invitations. The lab's two presets have the same size and different pictures, with one seed's tree holding 45 percent in one and no more than 25 percent in the other. The remedy the chapter gives is a very large and diverse set of first invitations.
3
Reading the targeted population as something the authors hidThe chapter states it openly as a design decision, with its cost: the sample omits people likely to perform worse than the low performers, which limits the comparisons. The authors call it a strength for explanatory power and also say they believe many findings apply more widely. A reader may weigh the two differently; the lesson does not settle it.
