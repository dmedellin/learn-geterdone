Tests of the Measurement Model

The appendix lists the tests a construct must pass before the authors use it: loadings above 0.60, average variance extracted above 0.50, its square root above the construct's correlations, correlations below 0.85, and reliability of at least 0.70. The measurement model in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the appendix's, the third the lab's.

1

Loading

How strongly an item is tied to the construct it is meant to measure; in the lab, a number from 0 to 1 for each item. The appendix asks that items load above 0.60 on their own construct, and not also strongly on another, which is called cross-loading.

2

Average variance extracted

The appendix describes it as the variance a construct captures against the variance due to measurement error, to be greater than 0.50. From standardized loadings it is the mean of their squares. The lab shortens it to AVE.

3

Composite reliability

A measure of a construct's internal consistency computed from the loadings, with 0.70 as the acceptable cutoff. Cronbach's alpha is its counterpart, computed from the answers; the appendix asks for either to pass. The lab abbreviates composite reliability to CR.

The material

The tests in the appendix's order, then the count

Read the appendix's Testing for Relationships and Tests of the Measurement Model in your own copy first. The lesson takes each test, gives the arithmetic the appendix leaves out, and computes it.

“All of the above tests must pass for a construct to be considered suitable for use in further analysis.” Forsgren, Humble and Kim write that in Appendix C, in the section Tests of the Measurement Model. The lesson reads that section and the short section Testing for Relationships that introduces it. The authors work in two stages, following Gefen and Straub (2005): first validate the measures and form the latent constructs of Chapter 13, then, with only the constructs that pass, test the hypotheses.

The first test looks at the items. Principal components analysis with varimax rotation, run separately for the independent and the dependent variables, helps confirm convergent validity, the property that items meant to measure one construct do. The authors almost always used the exploratory kind, which finds the structure without imposing one in advance; the notable exception was transformational leadership, whose items are well established, where the confirmatory kind was used. Items should load on their construct above 0.60 and not cross-load. The appendix names no threshold for cross-loading.

The second is the average variance extracted. The appendix does not give a formula; the standard one, for standardized loadings, is the mean of the squared loadings, and the lab uses it. A loading of 0.71 squares to about one half, so an AVE above 0.50 means the construct accounts, on average, for more than half of the variance of its items. The appendix requires it above 0.50 for convergent validity. It also asks that the square root of the AVE, set along the diagonal of a table of correlations, exceed the correlations in its row and column, which supports discriminant validity, the appendix's divergent validity: a construct should share more with its own items than with another construct. The lab tests it as AVE greater than r squared, which is the same test and avoids a square root.

AVE = (λ₁² + λ₂² + … + λₖ²) / k root test: √AVE must exceed the absolute value of r

The third is the correlation between constructs, which the appendix wants below 0.85 to help confirm discriminant validity. Two constructs correlated at 0.90 are hard to call two things. This test and the root test are separate: a pair can pass one and fail the other.

The fourth is reliability, internal consistency. Cronbach's alpha has a cutoff of 0.70 (the appendix's sentence names composite reliability where it plainly means alpha). The authors add a caution about alpha: it is biased against short scales, those with few items, so they ran both alpha and composite reliability, and every construct met one of the two cutoffs; composite reliability also has a cutoff of 0.70. The lesson Latent Constructs and System Data computes alpha; this lab computes composite reliability, which from standardized loadings is the squared sum of the loadings over that squared sum plus the error left by each item.

CR = (Σλ)² / ((Σλ)² + Σ(1 − λ²))

All of these tests must pass, and the authors report that every construct used in the research did, so each exhibits what they call good psychometric properties. Now the count. The lab's ave view takes each item's construct and loading, and the correlation between constructs. On the preset two-sound-constructs, release-confidence has loadings 0.85, 0.80, 0.75 and 0.70: AVE 0.604, CR 0.858 and a root of 0.777. team-trust has 0.90, 0.85 and 0.80: AVE 0.724, CR 0.887, root 0.851. Their correlation is 0.45, so both roots exceed it and every test passes; the lab's last tile counts 14 of 14 tests passed, an AVE and a CR for each construct, a test for each item's loading, and three for the pair.

The preset weak-construct swaps in tool-fit, with loadings 0.70, 0.60 and 0.55, correlated 0.30 with release-confidence. Its AVE is 0.384, below 0.50; its CR is 0.649, below 0.70; and two loadings, 0.60 and 0.55, are not above 0.60. Its root is 0.620, above 0.30, and 0.30 is below 0.85, so the root test and the correlation test pass for a construct that fails three kinds of test, and the tests tile reads 10 of 14, naming them. That is the point of the appendix's rule that all must pass.

The preset too-close pairs release-confidence with release-readiness, loadings 0.85, 0.80, 0.80 and 0.75: AVE 0.641, CR 0.877, root 0.801. Each construct passes the loading, AVE and reliability tests. They are correlated at 0.80. That is below 0.85, but 0.80 squared is 0.64, which is above the 0.604 AVE of release-confidence, so its root, 0.777, is below 0.80 and the root test fails. The pair is a case where a construct shares as much with another as with its own items. The tests tile reads 14 of 15 and names the one that failed.

The lab takes the loadings you type. The authors estimated theirs from thousands of answers, and so can you only from your own. Passing these tests shows that, in one sample, the items hang together and stay apart; it does not show that the name given to a construct describes what it measures, and the foreword's caution that these are perceptions still applies. The lesson Several Views, and a Safeguard Against Rogue Data works the same questions from an item correlation matrix.

  • As records: c1, scope, 0.80; c2, scope, 0.70; c3, scope, 0.65; d1, pace, 0.90; d2, pace, 0.85; d3, pace, 0.80, and between: scope, pace, 0.75.

By hand, compute the AVE and CR of scope and test each of the appendix's thresholds: loadings, AVE, CR, the roots against 0.75, and 0.75 against 0.85. Then type the records into the lab. Find the one test that fails and the pair it concerns.

Method

Testing a measurement model

Five steps, the last of them on constructs of your own.

1

Check the loadings

List every item's loading on its own construct. Flag any that is 0.60 or lower, since the appendix asks for loadings higher than 0.60.

2

Compute AVE and CR for each construct

AVE is the mean of the squared loadings. For CR, square the sum of the loadings and divide by that plus the sum of one minus each squared loading.

3

Test each construct against the thresholds

AVE above 0.50, CR at least 0.70. A construct that fails either is not used, whatever else it passes.

4

Test each pair

Check that AVE is above r squared for both constructs, which is the root test, and that r is below 0.85. Write down which of the pair fails.

5

Try it on constructs you use

Take a short scale your team scores with its loadings, from a factor analysis or a vendor report. List which of the appendix's tests it passes and which it cannot be tested on from what you have.

AVE and CR for release-confidence

Take the loadings 0.85, 0.80, 0.75 and 0.70 of release-confidence.

squares: 0.7225, 0.64, 0.5625, 0.49 sum of squares 2.415 AVE = 2.415 / 4 = 0.60375 root: 0.777 · 0.777 = 0.6037, so root = 0.777 sum of loadings 3.10; squared 9.61 errors: 4 − 2.415 = 1.585 CR = 9.61 / (9.61 + 1.585) = 9.61 / 11.195 = 0.858 0.60375 > 0.50 and 0.858 > 0.70: both pass

The error sum is the sum of one minus each squared loading, which is four items less their squares: 4 − 2.415. The lab prints AVE to three decimals, 0.604; the exact value is 483 / 800.

Common mistakes

1
Passing one test and calling the construct validThe appendix says all of the tests must pass. On weak-construct the root test and the correlation test pass for a construct whose AVE is 0.384, whose CR is 0.649 and with two loadings not above 0.60. A single passed test answers a single question.
2
Treating the correlation limit of 0.85 and the root test as one testOn too-close the correlation, 0.80, is below 0.85 and passes, while the root test fails for release-confidence because 0.80 squared, 0.64, exceeds its AVE of 0.604. The appendix lists them separately, and each can fail without the other.
3
Reading AVE as a score of how good a construct isAVE is the share of its items' variance that the construct accounts for, on average, from standardized loadings, so 0.604 means about 60 percent, in this sample. Whether the items measure what the construct's name says is a question the tests cannot answer.
