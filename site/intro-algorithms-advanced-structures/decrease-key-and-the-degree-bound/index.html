Decreasing a Key and Bounding the Degree

Section 19.3 decreases a key by cutting the node from its parent and, when the parent has already lost a child, cutting the parent too, in a chain of cascading cuts that stops at a root or an unmarked node. The marked nodes in the potential pay for the chain, so DECREASE-KEY costs O(1) amortized, and DELETE is a decrease followed by an extraction. Section 19.4 then proves the degree bound: a node of degree k heads a subtree of at least F(k + 2) ≥ φ^k nodes. Cut by hand, then let the lab count cuts, marks and the lemma at every node.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas: the operation that breaks trees apart, the one bit of history that limits it, and the quantity that history protects.

1

Cut

Removing a node, with its whole subtree, from its parent's child list and adding it to the root list as a new tree, unmarked. DECREASE-KEY cuts a node only when its new key is smaller than its parent's, the one case where heap order is broken.

2

Mark

A boolean on each node recording that it has lost one child since it was last made a child of another node. A new node is unmarked, linking clears the mark, and a marked node that loses a second child is itself cut by CASCADING-CUT.

3

Size of a node

How many nodes the subtree rooted at x contains, x included, written size(x). Section 19.4 shows it is exponential in x's degree, which is what bounds the degree of every node by a logarithm of n.

The material

The section's argument first, then the count

Read sections 19.3 and 19.4 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the argument in its own words, cuts one tree by hand, and then lets the lab count cuts, marks and the size lemma on longer sequences.

“One unit of potential pays for the cut and the clearing of the mark bit, and the other unit compensates for the unit increase in potential due to node y becoming a root.” That is the authors' answer, at the end of their analysis of DECREASE-KEY, to the question the last lesson left open: why the potential counts each marked node twice. This lesson reads sections 19.3 and 19.4: how a key is decreased and a node deleted, what the marks are for, and why a node of degree k must have a Fibonacci number of nodes below it.

FIB-HEAP-DECREASE-KEY lowers x's key to a value no larger than the old one. If x is a root, or its new key is still at least its parent y's, nothing is violated and nothing moves. Otherwise CUT removes x from y's child list, makes it an unmarked root, and CASCADING-CUT is called on y. That procedure does nothing at a root; marks y if y was unmarked, since y has just lost its first child; and if y was already marked, cuts y as well and calls itself on y's parent. The chain climbs until it reaches a root or an unmarked node. Finally H.min is updated, since the only key that changed is x's.

The mark is a small piece of history. Suppose a node was a root, was then linked under another node, and then lost two children to cuts. The authors' rule is that the second loss cuts the node from its parent at once. The mark records the state between the first loss and the second, which is why CUT clears it (the node becomes a root again) and why FIB-HEAP-LINK clears it (the node has just been made a child).

Theorem · DECREASE-KEY is O(1) amortized

Suppose one call makes c calls of CASCADING-CUT. Its actual cost is O(c). Afterwards there are t(H) + c trees: the original ones, x's, and the c − 1 cut by cascading. At most m(H) − c + 2 nodes are marked: c − 1 lost their mark when cut, and the last call may have marked one. So the potential rises by at most c + 2·(2 − c) = 4 − c, and with the unit of potential scaled to cover the constant in O(c), the amortized cost is O(1).

The lab's units make the constant visible. It charges one step for the decrease and one for each cut, so a call with c calls of CASCADING-CUT, which makes c cuts when its last call cuts nothing, costs 1 + c; with one unit of potential a root the change is at most 4 − c; so no DECREASE-KEY can cost more than 1 + c + 4 − c = 5 amortized, however long its cascade. A decrease that breaks no heap order costs 1. FIB-HEAP-DELETE gives x the smallest possible key, which the lab writes as minus infinity, by DECREASE-KEY and then calls EXTRACT-MIN, so its amortized cost is O(1) + O(D(n)).

Section 19.4 earns the degree bound. Take a node x of degree k and list its children in the order they were linked to it. Lemma 19.1 says the first has degree at least 0 and the i-th, for i ≥ 2, degree at least i − 2: when the i-th was linked, x already had the i − 1 earlier ones, and CONSOLIDATE links only equal degrees, so the child had degree at least i − 1 then; since then it can have lost at most one child, or it would have been cut away from x.

Theorem · Lemma 19.4 and Corollary 19.5

For any node x of degree k, size(x) ≥ F(k + 2) ≥ φᵏ, where F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1 and each later Fibonacci number adds the two before it, and φ = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618. The proof bounds the least possible size s(k) by 2 + s(0) + s(1) + … + s(k − 2) using Lemma 19.1, and Lemmas 19.2 and 19.3 turn that sum into F(k + 2) and then into φᵏ. Since n ≥ size(x) ≥ φᵏ, every degree is at most ⌊log_φ n⌋, so D(n) = O(lg n).

The last row is the size of a binomial tree, the shape consolidation alone produces: a tree linked from two trees of degree k − 1. Cuts let a node of degree k fall well below 2ᵏ, and the marks stop the fall at F(k + 2). The structure is named for that Fibonacci row, and the base φ of the logarithm the last lesson's lab printed comes from it too.

Every preset starts from the same tree: seventeen insertions of 10, 20, …, 170 and one extraction leave 16 nodes in a single tree rooted at 20, whose children are 30; 40 over 50; 60 over 70 and over 80, which is over 90; and 100, whose own children 110; 120 over 130; and 140 over 150 and 160, which is over 170, repeat the first three in shape.

The worked example fresh-tree stops there: no cuts, nothing marked, one root, degree 4 against the bound 5, and the lemma holds at all 16 nodes, closest at 40, of degree 1 and size 2, which is exactly F(3) = 2 (the leaves, each of size 1 = F(2), are just as close; the lab breaks the tie toward the larger degree). Choose one-cascade, the worked example below: three decreases make 4 cuts, 1 of them cascading, in 4 calls of CASCADING-CUT, and leave no node marked; the largest amortized DECREASE-KEY is 5, the ceiling, and the root list read from H.min is 3, 60, 4, 5, 20.

two-cascades marks 160, 140 and 100 by cutting one child from each, then decreases 160 itself: 160 is cut, its parent 140 is marked and is cut, and 140's parent 100 is marked and is cut, stopping at the root 20. That makes 6 cuts in all, 2 cascading, and the largest amortized decrease is still 5; the long cascade itself was cheap, because the three marks it cleared had been paid for when they were set. cascade-then-extract adds an extraction, which leaves 15 nodes in three trees, the lemma holding at every node. two-deletes deletes 140 and then 60: 2 cuts, no cascade, and 100 is left marked, having lost 140.

Actual cost, in steps: INSERT 1; MIN 1; EXTRACT-MIN 1 for the minimum + 1 for each child moved to the root list + 1 for each root CONSOLIDATE takes (a link is part of taking a root; the pass over the degree array is not counted); DECREASE-KEY 1 + 1 for each cut, its own and the cascading ones; DELETE the sum of its two parts. Φ = t(H) + 2m(H), roots plus twice the marked nodes; amortized cost = actual + ΔΦ. Method

Decreasing keys by hand

Four steps on paper, then one on a sequence of your own.

1

Draw the tree with its marks

Write each node with its children below it and a star on each marked node. Roots are never cut, so a star on a root does no work.

2

Decrease and test the parent

Lower the key. If it is still at least its parent's, or the node is a root, stop: actual cost 1, no change in potential.

3

Cut and climb

Otherwise move the node to the root list unmarked. Go to its old parent: at a root stop; if unmarked, star it and stop; if starred, cut it too, unstar it, and go to its parent. Count each visit as one call of CASCADING-CUT and each removal as one cut.

4

Price it

Actual cost is 1 plus the cuts. The change in potential is the new roots plus twice the change in stars. Check the sum is at most 5.

5

Do it on a sequence of your own

Starting from the lab's 16-node tree, choose four decreases that produce a cascade of length two, predict the cuts and marks, then add them to a preset in the lab and compare.

Three decreases under 60

Start from the 16-node tree rooted at 20, with Φ = 1. Node 60 has children 70 and 80, and 80 has the child 90. Run decrease 70 5, decrease 90 4, decrease 80 3.

70 → 5: below 60, so cut; 60 marked actual 2, ΔΦ = 1 + 2 = 3, amortized 5 90 → 4: below 80, so cut; 80 marked actual 2, ΔΦ = 3, amortized 5 80 → 3: cut 80, clearing its mark 60 is marked: cut 60 too 60's parent 20 is a root: stop actual 3, ΔΦ = 2 − 2·2 = −2 amortized 1 4 cuts, 1 cascading, 4 calls

The roots are now 3, 60, 4, 5 and 20, read from H.min, nothing is marked, and Φ = 5. The third decrease did the most work and cost the least amortized, because it cleared two marks the first two had paid for. Choose one-cascade in the lab to see each step in its table.

Common mistakes

1
Cutting every node whose key is decreasedDECREASE-KEY cuts only when the new key is below the parent's, the one case where min-heap order breaks; a decrease that keeps order changes one field and costs 1. On the lab's tree, decreasing 70 to 65 moves nothing, since 65 is still above 60; decreasing it to 5 cuts it. A root is never cut, whatever its new key.
2
Fearing that cascading cuts make DECREASE-KEY expensiveOne call can climb a long chain, and on two-cascades the last decrease cuts three nodes. But every node a cascade cuts was marked, and its mark had stored two units of potential; the amortized cost of every decrease in the lab is at most 5, the constant the analysis predicts for its units. The actual cost of one call is not bounded by a constant; the amortized cost is.
3
Assuming every tree in the heap is a binomial treeConsolidation alone builds binomial shapes, with 2ᵏ nodes at degree k, and the last lesson's bound ⌊lg n⌋ holds for them. Cuts break the shape, and the bound Section 19.4 proves is size(x) ≥ F(k + 2), so the degree bound becomes ⌊log_φ n⌋, larger than ⌊lg n⌋. The lab's lemma tile checks the Fibonacci bound, not the binomial one.
