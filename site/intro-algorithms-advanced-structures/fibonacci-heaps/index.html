Fibonacci Heaps

Sections 19.1 and 19.2 build a mergeable heap out of min-heap-ordered trees whose roots sit in one circular list. Insertion and union add roots and do nothing else; EXTRACT-MIN removes the minimum, promotes its children and consolidates the root list until no two roots share a degree. The potential t(H) + 2m(H) turns that bargain into amortized bounds: constant for insertion and union, O(D(n)) for extraction. Run one extraction by hand, then watch the lab charge every operation its actual cost and its change in potential.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas: the interface the chapter implements, the shape that implements it, and the bookkeeping that prices it.

1

Mergeable heap

A structure supporting MAKE-HEAP, INSERT, MINIMUM, EXTRACT-MIN and UNION, the last combining two heaps into one. Fibonacci heaps add DECREASE-KEY and DELETE, which are the next lesson's. A binary heap does everything but UNION in O(lg n); its union costs Θ(n), which is the gap this chapter closes.

2

Root list

The roots of all the heap's trees, linked in a circular, doubly linked list so that a node can be added or removed anywhere, and two lists spliced together, in constant time. The pointer H.min marks the root with the least key; every other root is unordered, waiting for the next extraction to tidy it.

3

Potential

The function Φ(H) = t(H) + 2m(H): how many trees the root list holds, plus twice the number of marked nodes. It starts at 0 and never goes negative, so by Chapter 17's potential method the amortized costs, each the actual cost plus the change in Φ, add up to at least the actual total.

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the count

Read the chapter opening and sections 19.1 and 19.2 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the argument in its own words, runs one extraction by hand, and then lets the lab price every operation of a longer sequence.

“The mergeable-heap operations on Fibonacci heaps delay work as long as possible.” That sentence opens Section 19.2, and the whole chapter is an account of what the delay buys and who pays for it. This lesson reads the chapter opening and sections 19.1 and 19.2: the operations a mergeable heap supports, the trees and lists a Fibonacci heap is made of, the potential function, and the three operations that leave all the work to the fourth.

The chapter opens with a comparison. A binary heap, the structure of Chapter 6, inserts, extracts and decreases keys in Θ(lg n) worst-case time, but uniting two of them means concatenating the arrays and rebuilding, Θ(n). A Fibonacci heap inserts, unites and decreases a key in Θ(1) amortized time, and extracts or deletes in O(lg n) amortized. The authors name two cautions of their own. These are amortized bounds, not per-operation ones; and the constant factors and the programming effort make the structure, in their words, “predominantly of theoretical interest.” Its appeal is greatest where extractions are few beside the other operations, as in graph algorithms that may decrease a key once per edge (Chapters 23 and 24).

Definition · Fibonacci heap

A collection of rooted trees, each obeying the min-heap property, whose roots form a circular, doubly linked root list. Each node keeps a parent pointer, a pointer to one child, its siblings in a circular child list, its degree (the number of children) and a boolean mark. The heap keeps H.min, the root with the least key, and H.n, the number of nodes. Siblings and roots may appear in any order.

Section 19.1 sets up the analysis. With t(H) trees in the root list and m(H) marked nodes, the potential is t(H) + 2m(H); the marks play no part until the next lesson, and here every mark is false. The analysis also needs a bound D(n) on the largest degree a node can have in a heap of n nodes. When only the mergeable-heap operations are used, D(n) ≤ ⌊lg n⌋ (Problem 19-2 asks for the proof); the next lesson shows D(n) = O(lg n) in general.

Section 19.2's first three operations are short. FIB-HEAP-INSERT makes the new node a one-node tree, adds it to the root list beside H.min, and updates H.min if the new key is smaller; t rises by one, so the amortized cost is the constant actual cost plus 1. Finding the minimum reads H.min and changes nothing. FIB-HEAP-UNION splices two root lists and keeps the smaller minimum; the trees and marks of the result are those of the two heaps together, so the potential does not change at all. None of the three ever looks at a tree's shape.

FIB-HEAP-EXTRACT-MIN is where the delayed work is done. It moves every child of the minimum node z into the root list, removes z, and calls CONSOLIDATE, which takes the roots one at a time and keeps an array A indexed by degree. When a root x arrives and A[d] already holds a root y of the same degree d, the two are linked: FIB-HEAP-LINK makes the one with the larger key a child of the other, clears its mark, and raises the survivor's degree to d + 1, and the step repeats at the new degree. The authors prove the inner loop with an invariant, that at the start of each iteration d is x's degree. At the end at most one root of each degree is left, and the root list is rebuilt from A.

Theorem · Extraction is O(D(n)) amortized

The actual cost is O(D(n) + t(H)): at most D(n) children are moved, and CONSOLIDATE handles at most D(n) + t(H) − 1 roots, each link removing one. Afterwards at most D(n) + 1 roots remain and no node has been marked, so the potential falls from t(H) + 2m(H) to at most D(n) + 1 + 2m(H). Scaling the unit of potential to cover the constant in O(t(H)), the amortized cost is O(D(n)).

The lab does not scale the unit: it charges one step for the minimum, one for each child moved, and one for each root CONSOLIDATE takes, and one unit of potential for each root. On those terms the sum can be done exactly. With c children and t roots before and t′ after, the actual cost is 1 + c + (t − 1 + c) and the potential changes by t′ − t when no node is marked, so the amortized cost is 2c + t′: the children counted twice and the surviving roots once, both bounded by the degree bound, and the t roots that were waiting cost nothing net.

The worked example eight-inserts inserts eight keys and stops. Nothing has been tidied: the root list is all eight keys, the potential is 8, the actual cost is 8 and the amortized cost 16, two per insertion, one spent and one stored. Choose then-extract, which adds one EXTRACT-MIN. The extraction scans seven roots, actual cost 8, and links them into three trees of degrees 0, 1 and 2, so the potential falls from 8 to 3 and the amortized cost is 3; the totals are 16 actual and 19 amortized, and 19 is 16 plus the potential left over, as it must be when the heap started empty.

extract-twice extracts again. Now the minimum has two children, so the extraction moves 2, scans 4 roots and costs 7, and leaves two roots, so its amortized cost is 2·2 + 2 = 6. seventeen-inserts inserts 17 keys and extracts the least: the single extraction costs 17 actual steps and links all 16 survivors into one tree of degree 4, so the potential falls from 17 to 1 and the amortized cost is 1. The 16 units the insertions stored paid for the 16 roots scanned. mixed interleaves extractions, a min and new insertions, and ends with 7 nodes in three trees, 31 actual steps and 34 amortized; its dearest extraction is charged 9.

Method

Running a Fibonacci heap by hand

Four steps on paper, then one on a sequence of your own.

1

Keep the root list as a circle

Write the roots in a row, read round from H.min. Put each inserted key just left of H.min and move H.min if the new key is smaller. Add 1 to t for each.

2

Extract: promote, then remove

Move the minimum's children into the root list where it stood, remove it, and count 1 plus the number of children.

3

Consolidate by degree

Starting at the old minimum's right neighbour and going round, take each root once. If an earlier root has the same degree, link the larger key under the smaller and check again at the new degree. Count one step for each root taken.

4

Price it

Add the steps for the actual cost, subtract t before from t after for the change in potential, and add the two. With no marks, the result is twice the children plus the roots left.

5

Do it on a sequence of your own

Write ten insertions and two extractions, run them by hand, then type them into the lab and compare your root lists and costs with its table.

Common mistakes

1
Reading a constant amortized bound as a cheap operation every timeInsertion's amortized cost is 2 in the lab's units, but the amortized bound promises only that the total stays within the sum of the amortized costs. Single operations can be expensive: on seventeen-inserts one extraction costs 17 actual steps. It is cheap amortized, 1, because the insertions before it stored the potential that pays for it.
2
Expecting the root list to stay shortNothing tidies the root list except EXTRACT-MIN. Insertions and unions only add roots, so after eight insertions there are eight roots, and a long run of them can leave thousands. Every one carries a unit of potential, and the next extraction spends it; the shortness of the list is a fact about the moment after an extraction, not a rule.
3
Linking any two roots to shorten the listCONSOLIDATE links only roots of equal degree, and that restriction is what keeps degrees small: two trees of degree d make one of degree d + 1, much as a binomial tree doubles. On the exercise, roots of degrees 2 and 1 are left side by side. Linking unequal roots would shorten the list and lose the bound on degree that the amortized cost of extraction depends on.
