Chapter 20 in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas: the way a key is split, the recurrence the split is designed to obey, and the two fields that let the structure obey it.

1

Universe, clusters and summary

The universe is the set of possible keys, 0 to u − 1. It is divided into √u clusters of √u consecutive values; key x lies in cluster high(x) = ⌊x/√u⌋ at position low(x) = x mod √u. A summary records which clusters are nonempty, and is itself a set over a universe of √u values.

2

The square-root recurrence

T(u) = T(√u) + O(1). Substituting m = lg u turns it into S(m) = S(m/2) + O(1), which the master method solves as O(lg m), so T(u) = O(lg lg u). An operation obeys it when it does constant work at a level and recurses at most once.

3

min and max

Fields that a van Emde Boas tree keeps at every level. The minimum is stored only there, not in any cluster; the maximum is stored there and in its cluster. They answer MINIMUM and MAXIMUM without recursion, tell whether a successor lies in x's own cluster, and make inserting into an empty tree constant work.

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the count

Read the chapter opening and sections 20.1 to 20.3 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the argument in its own words, counts the calls of four operations by hand, and then sets the two recursive structures side by side in the lab.

“Clearly, we need to modify the proto-vEB structure to get each operation down to making at most one recursive call.” The authors write it at the end of Section 20.2, having built a recursive structure that has exactly the right shape and still runs too slowly, and Section 20.3 is the modification. This lesson reads Chapter 20: why integer keys allow a faster priority queue, the recurrence that sets the target, the structure that misses it, and the tree that meets it.

The chapter opens with a limit. Binary heaps, red-black trees and Fibonacci heaps all spend Θ(lg n) on some operation, and they must: a structure that only compares keys and could both insert and extract the minimum in o(lg n) time could sort n keys with o(n lg n) comparisons, which Section 8.1 forbids. Counting sort escaped that bound by using the keys as array indices, and van Emde Boas trees escape it the same way: if the keys are distinct integers from 0 to u − 1, with u a power of 2, each dynamic-set operation takes O(lg lg u) time in the worst case. From here on n counts the elements and u the universe, and MEMBER replaces SEARCH, since only keys are stored.

Section 20.1 tries three warm-ups. A bit vector of u bits inserts, deletes and tests membership in constant time, but its minimum and successor may scan Θ(u) bits. A binary tree of bits laid over the vector, each internal bit the logical or of its children, brings every operation to O(lg u), one walk up and one down. A tree of degree √u laid over it instead has height 2: a summary of √u bits, one per cluster, over the clusters themselves, and its queries take O(√u). That looks like a step backwards, and the authors say so; but it is the split the rest of the chapter makes recursive.

Definition · high, low and index

For a universe of size u, high(x) = ⌊x/√u⌋ is x's cluster number, the upper half of its lg u bits; low(x) = x mod √u is its position inside the cluster, the lower half; and index(x, y) = x·√u + y puts them back together, so x = index(high(x), low(x)). Inside a cluster or a summary the same functions are taken with that structure's own, smaller u.

Section 20.2 sets the target before building anything. A structure that spends constant time at a level and then recurses once into a universe of √u obeys T(u) = T(√u) + O(1), and the change of variables m = lg u gives S(m) = S(m/2) + O(1), so O(lg m), which is O(lg lg u). The other way to see it: the number of bits in the universe size halves at each level, from lg u down to 1, and halving lg u reaches 1 after lg lg u steps.

The proto van Emde Boas structure, proto-vEB(u), follows the plan literally for u = 2^(2^k). At u = 2 it is an array of two bits; above that it holds a summary and √u clusters, each a proto-vEB(√u). PROTO-vEB-MEMBER drills straight down through cluster[high(x)] and meets the target, O(lg lg u). PROTO-vEB-MINIMUM must ask the summary for the first nonempty cluster and then ask that cluster for its minimum: two calls a level, T(u) = 2T(√u) + O(1), which solves to Θ(lg u). PROTO-vEB-SUCCESSOR can search x's cluster, then the summary, then call MINIMUM, and costs Θ(lg u·lg lg u); PROTO-vEB-INSERT inserts into the cluster and into the summary, Θ(lg u). Deletion is harder still, since a summary bit can be cleared only when its cluster is empty, and the book leaves it to Exercises 20.2-2 and 20.2-3.

Theorem · Two calls a level

An insertion into proto-vEB(u) always recurses into both a cluster and the summary, so at u = 2^(2^k) it makes 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 2ᵏ = 2^(k+1) − 1 calls, which is 2·lg u − 1: 7 at u = 16, 15 at u = 256, 31 at u = 65,536. Doubling the calls at each of lg lg u levels multiplies them by 2^(lg lg u) = lg u.

Section 20.3's van Emde Boas tree, vEB(u), keeps the shape and adds two fields at every level, min and max, with one twist: the minimum is not stored in any cluster. (It also allows any power of 2 as u, splitting the bits into an upper and a lower square root when lg u is odd; the lab keeps to u = 2^(2^k).) Now MINIMUM and MAXIMUM read a field. SUCCESSOR compares low(x) with the maximum of x's cluster and so knows, without a call, whether to recurse into the cluster or into the summary, never both. INSERT into an empty tree sets min and max; otherwise it first swaps x with min when x is smaller, so that the old minimum is the element carried down, and then, if x's cluster is empty, it recurses into the summary and fills the cluster in constant time, and if the cluster is not empty the summary already knows it, and it recurses into the cluster only. The price is space: a vEB tree takes O(u) space and O(u) time to create (Problem 20-1).

DELETE is the one operation that can make two recursive calls, one into x's cluster and one into the summary, and the authors show it still obeys the recurrence. The summary call happens only when the cluster has just become empty, which means x was its only element, and deleting the only element of a vEB tree is constant work, setting min and max to nothing. So at any level one of the two calls is cheap. Deleting the minimum itself needs one more step: the next smallest element is fetched from the first nonempty cluster, becomes the new min, and is deleted from its cluster in its place.

Method

Counting a vEB tree's calls by hand

Four steps on paper, then one on a sequence of your own.

1

Draw the levels

From u write the universe at each level, u, √u, …, 2, and for each element you use, high and low at every level.

2

Keep min out of the clusters

At each level hold min and max apart. When a smaller element arrives, swap it into min and carry the old minimum down instead.

3

Recurse once

For an insertion into a nonempty tree: if x's cluster is empty, recurse into the summary with high(x) and set the cluster's min and max to low(x); otherwise recurse into the cluster. For a successor: compare low(x) with the cluster's max and recurse into the cluster or the summary, not both. Count one call per level entered.

4

Compare with proto-vEB

For the same operation count the proto-vEB calls: insertion always enters both the cluster and the summary, 2·lg u − 1 calls in all.

5

Do it on a sequence of your own

Write five operations at u = 16, count both structures' calls by hand, then type them into the lab and compare with its table.

Common mistakes

1
Expecting O(lg lg n) in the number of elementsThe bound is in u, the size of the universe, not n, the number of keys stored, and it holds only because the keys are integers below u. The space is O(u) too, however few keys there are: the lab's spread-u65536 stores four keys in a universe of 65,536, and creating the tree costs O(u) time, which, the authors note, can exceed what a few operations save.
2
Thinking the square-root split alone gives lg lg uproto-vEB has exactly the split and the levels, and still costs Θ(lg u) to insert or find a minimum, because it recurses twice at a level. The recurrence T(u) = T(√u) + O(1) needs at most one recursive call. On the lab's universe of 65,536 every proto-vEB insertion makes 31 calls, 2·lg u − 1, while the vEB insertions there make at most 4.
3
Treating min like the other elementsA vEB tree's minimum lives only in its min field, not in a cluster, which is what makes insertion into an empty tree constant work. Forgetting it gives wrong successors and wrong deletions: deleting the minimum must fetch the next smallest from the first nonempty cluster, make it the new min, and delete it from its cluster. The lab's top-level tile lists min apart from the clusters for this reason.
