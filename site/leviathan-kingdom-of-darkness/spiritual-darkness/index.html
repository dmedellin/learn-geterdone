Spiritual Darkness from Misinterpretation of Scripture

Hobbes calls the kingdom of darkness a confederacy of deceivers, and says its chief error is to take the present church for the kingdom of God.

The passage first, then the chapter's argument

Chapter 44. “This considered, the Kingdome of Darknesse, as it is set forth in these, and other places of the Scripture, is nothing else but a Confederacy of Deceivers, that to obtain dominion over men in this present world, endeavour by dark, and erroneous Doctrines, to extinguish in them the Light, both of Nature, and of the Gospell; and so to dis-prepare them for the Kingdome of God to come.”

Three Parts of the book have gone to building a commonwealth with one sovereign, and then to showing, from Scripture, that a Christian commonwealth is no different. You might think the work is finished. It is not, because there is a claim abroad that makes two sovereigns of one, and in the England of 1651, after a civil war fought partly over bishops and presbyters, you did not have to look far for it. Hobbes wrote the book in exile in Paris. When he asks why Christendom has had, “almost from the time of the Apostles”, so much jostling by foreign and civil war, his answer is that it is “Night amongst us, or at least a Mist”, and that “wee are therefore yet in the Dark”.

Notice what the darkness is made of. Scripture speaks of Satan as prince of the power of the air and of Beelzebub as prince of demons, which Hobbes takes to mean prince of phantasms, the appearances that fill the air. The children of darkness, the demons and the spirits of illusion, he says, “signifie allegorically the same thing”. So the kingdom is not a place. It is people, and what they want is dominion. He says the enemy has been at work in four ways, sowing tares in “the Night of our naturall Ignorance”: by abusing the Scriptures, by bringing in the demonology of the heathen poets, by mixing in the vain philosophy of the Greeks, Aristotle above all, and by false or uncertain traditions and feigned history. Chapter 44 takes the first. The next two lessons take the second and third, and the fourth lesson takes up who gained by all four.

The first abuse comes with a claim that Hobbes ranks above the rest. “The greatest, and main abuse of Scripture, and to which almost all the rest are either consequent, or subservient,” he writes, “is the wresting of it, to prove that the Kingdome of God, mentioned so often in the Scripture, is the present Church”. His reply has three steps, all on one page. The kingdom of God “was first instituted by the Ministery of Moses, over the Jews onely” and ended when they chose Saul. Since then God has been king only as he always was, of all creatures “by his infinite Power”. And God promised through the prophets to restore his government, and to send his Son. Then comes the sentence the lab is built on: “Which second coming not yet being, the Kingdome of God is not yet come, and wee are not now under any other Kings by Pact, but our Civill Soveraigns”.

That is an argument, so write it as one. Let k be the claim under test, that the present church is the kingdom of God. Let c be that the kingdom of God has come, and s that Christ's second coming has happened. If the church is the kingdom, the kingdom has come: k → c. The kingdom comes only with the second coming: c → s. And the second coming has not happened: ¬s, read not s. From those three, not k. The lab's hobbes preset tests exactly this. It is valid: of the eight rows of truth values for k, c and s, only one makes all three premises true, the row where all three letters are false, and there the conclusion is true. There is no counterexample.

Validity is cheap, though, so ask which premise earns it. Take away c → s and run the no-link preset, with only k → c and ¬s. The argument is now invalid, and the lab finds the row: the church is the kingdom, the kingdom has come, and the second coming has not. Nothing in those two premises rules it out. Take away k → c instead, in no-bridge, and it fails again, with one counterexample: the kingdom has not come, and the church is the kingdom anyway. Each premise is needed. But k → c is hardly in dispute, since it only says that a kingdom that is present has come. The premise that does the work is the middle one, and it is the one Hobbes's opponents deny.

Who were they? Cardinal Robert Bellarmine, the Jesuit whose texts for the present kingdom of God, administered by the pope, Hobbes says he has already answered, held that Christ's kingdom is the church now on earth. Theodore Beza, who followed Calvin at Geneva, read Christ's words in Mark 9 about some who would not taste death before they saw the kingdom of God come with power as saying that it began at the Resurrection. Hobbes does not say what Beza meant it to entitle: whether the Presbytery of Geneva to supreme ecclesiastical power, or princes. He observes that the Presbytery has claimed power to excommunicate its own kings, “no lesse than the Pope challengeth it universally”. Both deny c → s, and for both the church's present authority is what hangs on denying it.

Hobbes answers Beza from the Gospels. Before the Ascension the apostles ask “Wilt thou at this time restore again the Kingdome to Israel”, and are told that it is not for them to know the times. St. Paul says Christians were to wait for the Son from heaven, and Christians pray “Let thy Kingdome Come”, which would be pointless if the kingdom were already here. The words in Mark he reads, with some hesitation, as fulfilled in the Transfiguration, a vision. That is the weak point a critic would press: all of it depends on taking kingdom as a kingdom in the ordinary sense, with a king and a time of beginning. If Christ's kingdom is a spiritual rule over the faithful, as Bellarmine and Beza each in their own way held, then c → s is not obviously true and the lab cannot decide between them.

Hobbes then lists what follows from the error. Someone must speak for Christ, now in heaven, and the pope claims to. A Christian king must be crowned by a bishop. The Fourth Lateran Council under Innocent III, as Hobbes reports it, released the subjects of a king from obedience if he would not purge his kingdom of heresy after excommunication. The result, he says, is “such a Mist” that subjects “know not a stranger that thrusteth himself into the throne of their lawfull Prince, from him whom they had themselves placed there”. Then the clergy take their name from the Levites' inheritance, and with it the tithe, which he says is double what an Athenian king, counted a tyrant, took to pay for the whole commonwealth. And there is canon law beside civil law, and priests exempt from the civil tribunals. You can see why he calls the first error the main one: each of these is the church acting as a second government.

The second abuse is the turning of consecration into conjuration. His definition is plain. To consecrate is to separate a thing from common use to God, “to change, not the thing Consecrated, but onely the use of it”. A bishop who consecrates a church changes its use. A priest who says that the bread is no longer bread, though no one sees any difference, is, for Hobbes, in the same case as the Egyptian magicians, only without the show. He takes “This is my Body” to be “aequivalent” to “This signifies, or represents my Body”, a figure of speech. Catholic theologians answered that the doctrine itself says the senses see nothing change, because substance is one thing and the appearances another. Hobbes's reply to that is in Chapter 46, where the distinction itself is what he attacks, and the third lesson of this course comes to it.

The third error is about eternal life. Hobbes reads Genesis as saying that Adam lived on condition he did not sin, that Christ's passion restores eternal life to the faithful, and that the doctrine that every man has eternity by nature, because his soul is immortal, is later and wrong. For him “the Soule in Scripture, signifieth alwaies, either the Life, or the Living Creature”, and God, who raised dust and clay into a living creature, can as easily raise a dead body. From the rejected doctrine, he says, come eternal torments, purgatory, ghosts that walk, exorcism, prayers to the dead and indulgences. He goes through Bellarmine's texts one by one and finds none that proves purgatory. He is also candid about what he cannot explain. On Christ's saying that a sin against the Holy Ghost will not be forgiven in this world or the next, he writes that it is “very hardly to bee reconciled with all the Doctrines now unanimously received”, and adds that it is not “any shame, to confesse the profoundnesse of the Scripture”. He offers his own readings of it only “to the consideration of more learned Divines”. On eternal torment he is as plain as he is on purgatory: he can “find no where that any man shall live in torments Everlastingly”.

The hinge of the whole Part is this. If the church were the kingdom of God, the clergy would be its officers and every Christian a subject of two governments, and if eternal torment waited on the favour of the priests, nobody could afford to disobey them. Hobbes is arguing that the authority of Scripture, properly read, leaves the civil sovereign as the only king on earth. Whether the premise c → s is true is a question about the texts, and the lab can only tell you how much hangs on it. The next lesson, Demonology and the Relics of the Gentiles, follows the second of the four causes, the heathen poets' ghosts and demons.

Setting out an argument about a kingdom

Five steps; the lab does the fourth and cannot do the fifth.

  1. Give each claim a letter. Write down what each letter means and keep it fixed: k for the present church being the kingdom of God, c for the kingdom having come, s for the second coming having happened.
  2. Write each premise as a conditional or a denial. State the premises as Hobbes's sentences allow: k → c, c → s, ¬s. Find the sentence in the chapter each one comes from, and drop any premise you cannot find.
  3. Test the whole for validity. Look for a row in which every premise is true and the conclusion false. If there is none, the argument is valid.
  4. Remove one premise at a time. Rerun the test without each premise. A premise whose removal leaves a counterexample is needed; here all three are, and the middle one is the one a reader can contest.
  5. Ask who would deny the premise, and on what text. Valid is not sound. For c → s the deniers are the ones who say the kingdom is the church now on earth, and the text they cite is Mark 9.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the argument is good because the lab calls it validThe hobbes preset has no counterexample, and that shows only that the conclusion follows if the three premises are true. The middle premise is the whole quarrel: Bellarmine and Beza each deny that the kingdom comes only at the second coming, and the lab, which sees letters, treats their denial and Hobbes's assertion alike.
2
Thinking Hobbes denies that God reigns nowHe says God “ever was, is, and shall be King, of all men, and of all creatures”, by his power. What has lapsed is God's kingdom by pact, which began with Moses and ended with Saul and will be restored at Christ's return. Christians are meanwhile in the kingdom of grace, which is a promise.
3
Reading the chapter as a denial of resurrection or of eternal lifeHobbes affirms both for the faithful. His claim is that eternal life is a gift of grace restored by Christ, so that it is not a natural property of every soul, and that Scripture's soul means the life or the living creature. The doctrines he rejects, torments without end, purgatory and ghosts, are the ones he says rest on the immortal soul.

Before you go

Say each idea in your own words, then open it to check.

The kingdom of darkness

Hobbes reads the Scripture's rulers of the darkness of this world, Satan and Beelzebub, prince of the phantasms of the air, as an allegory with a meaning here and now. The kingdom of darkness is a confederacy of deceivers who use dark and erroneous doctrines to win dominion over men, and so put out the light of nature and of the Gospel.

The kingdom of God, strictly taken

On Hobbes's account in Chapter 35, God's kingdom by pact was instituted by Moses over the Jews alone and ended when they asked Samuel for a king like the other nations. Since then God reigns over all creatures by his power, but he has no kingdom by pact until Christ comes again. Meanwhile Christians are in the kingdom of grace, which is a promise, and their only kings are their civil sovereigns.

Consecration and conjuration

To consecrate is to set a person or thing apart from common use for God, which changes the use and not the nature of the thing. Words that pretend to change its nature are, on Hobbes's reckoning, an incantation. The same chapter treats eternal life as a gift of grace to the faithful and not a property of every soul.

Summary

Part IV opens with a kingdom nobody can see, and Hobbes means it politically: men who want dominion over other men and get it by false doctrine. Set out his argument that the present church is not the kingdom of God as three premises and a conclusion, test it for validity, and find the one premise that does all the work and that his Catholic and Presbyterian opponents would not grant.
