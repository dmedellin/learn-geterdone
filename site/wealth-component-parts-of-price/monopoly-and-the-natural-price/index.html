Monopoly and the Natural Price

A secret, a scarce soil or a privilege can keep the market under-stocked, and the price of monopoly is the highest which can be got.

In brief

  • Secrets
  • Natural scarcity
  • Monopoly and privilege

What keeps a price up, and for how long

Book I, Chapter 7. “The price of monopoly is upon every occasion the highest which can be got. The natural price, or the price of free competition, on the contrary, is the lowest which can be taken, not upon every occasion indeed, but for any considerable time together.”

Smith comes to this sentence last, after three kinds of case. The first is the secret. When a rise in the effectual demand lifts the market price well above the natural price, “those who employ their stocks in supplying that market, are generally careful to conceal this change”, because if it were known, so many rivals would come in that the price would fall again, perhaps below the natural price for a while. If the market is far away, the secret may be kept for years, but “Secrets of this kind, however, it must be acknowledged, can seldom be long kept”. Secrets in manufactures last longer. His example is a dyer who finds a way of producing a colour with materials that cost half as much, and may enjoy the advantage as long as he lives and leave it to his heirs.

Smith says exactly what these gains are, and that deserves a second look. The dyer's extraordinary gains “arise from the high price which is paid for his private labour. They properly consist in the high wages of that labour.” But the advantage repeats on every part of his stock, so the whole amount is in proportion to his stock, “they are commonly considered as extraordinary profits of stock”. That is the sort of mix-up the last lesson ended on: a revenue of one name that belongs to another. Later writers made more of this kind of gain. Schumpeter argued in 1942 that the prospect of a temporary advantage of this kind is what drives invention and that a market with no such prize would have less of it; Smith does not take that step, and treats the gain as a departure from the natural price.

The second kind is the natural scarcity. Some natural productions need “such a singularity of soil and situation” that all the land fit to produce them may not supply the effectual demand, and then the whole quantity is sold to those willing to pay more than the natural price. “Such commodities may continue for whole centuries together to be sold at this high price; and that part of it which resolves itself into the rent of land, is in this case the part which is generally paid above its natural rate.” The French vineyards of a peculiarly happy soil pay a rent that “bears no regular proportion to the rent of other equally fertile and equally well cultivated land in its neighbourhood”, while the wages and the profits employed there are “seldom out of their natural proportion”. This is the first appearance of an idea that Chapter 11, in the course on rent, builds on: that rent can be a consequence of price and not its cause.

The third kind is monopoly itself. “The monopolists, by keeping the market constantly understocked by never fully supplying the effectual demand, sell their commodities much above the natural price, and raise their emoluments, whether they consist in wages or profit, greatly above their natural rate.” Corporations and the statutes of apprenticeship “are a sort of enlarged monopolies” and may keep wages and profits above their natural rate for ages together. The course after next, in its lesson Apprenticeship and Corporations, takes these up with their own figures.

The lab can show the shape of this, with a schedule that is the lab's and not Smith's. The natural price is 10 shillings a yard and the effectual demand 1,000 yards, and buyers would take 1,300 yards at 8 shillings, 1,000 at 10, 900 at 12, 800 at 14, 700 at 16, 600 at 18, 500 at 20 and 400 at 22. Bring the effectual demand to market, in the free preset, and the price is the natural 10 and there is nothing over. Keep 800 back in the m800 preset and the price rises to 14; keep 500 back in the m500 preset and it is 20; in the m400 preset it is 22. The excess over the natural price, taken over the quantity brought, is 3,200, 5,000 and 4,800 shillings.

Notice that the highest price is not the largest gain. Smith's sentence says the price of monopoly is “upon every occasion the highest which can be got”, and it is, for the quantity actually brought. Keep back more and the price per yard is higher still, in this schedule. But fewer yards are sold, and the total excess over the natural price rises to 5,000 shillings at 500 yards and then falls to 4,800 at 400. Taking the natural price as the cost of a yard at every quantity, which is a simplification of the lab and not Smith's, the monopolist who wants the most would stop at 500. Augustin Cournot, in 1838, worked this out as a problem of choosing a quantity; Smith's chapter states the result in its plainest form and does not set the problem.

The last pages are about the other side. “The market price of any particular commodity, though it may continue long above, can seldom continue long below, its natural price”: whoever is paid below the natural rate withdraws land, labour or stock at once. The statutes of apprenticeship that raise a workman's wages in a prosperous trade may hold them down when it decays, but with less force, for their effect “is not near so durable in sinking the workman's wages below, as in raising them above their natural rate”. Only a policy as violent as the castes of Indostan or ancient Egypt could keep wages or profit below the natural rate for generations.

Smith closes the chapter by saying that the natural price itself “varies with the natural rate of each of its component parts” and listing four tasks for the four chapters that follow: the rate of wages, the rate of profit, the proportions between wages and profits in different employments, and the rent of land. There is a question his chapter leaves open that critics have kept asking: in which case is a price above the natural price a monopoly, and in which a reward? The chapter marks it out, but does not draw the line.

The next course, Wages and Profits, begins with the first of those tasks: what determines the wages of labour.

Common mistakes

1
Reading “the highest which can be got” as the monopolist's best choice“the highest which can be got” is the highest for the quantity brought. The lab shows that a still smaller quantity gets a higher price and can bring a smaller total excess. Smith's sentence is about what a monopoly's price is, not about how it picks a quantity.
2
Treating every price above the natural price as a monopolySmith lists three causes of different lengths: a secret, a natural scarcity and a legal privilege. The dyer has no privilege; the vineyard has no seller's design. Each keeps the market under-supplied, for different reasons.
3
Expecting the same force below the natural priceThe market price can stay above the natural price for centuries, and can only briefly stay below it, because those who are paid less withdraw land, labour or stock. Smith makes an exception only for a policy as violent as caste.

The lab cannot say whether a given price above the natural price is a monopoly, a reward for a secret or a scarcity; it does not know why the market is under-supplied. Cournot's quantity rule is a later one, and Smith stated none.

Summary

The end of Chapter 7 asks what can hold a market price above the natural price for years: a trade or manufacturing secret, a natural scarcity such as a particular vineyard, and a monopoly or a corporation's privilege. Run a monopolist's choice of quantity against a buyers' schedule written for the lesson and see the price and the excess over the natural price at each.

Key ideas

  • Secrets. A trader who learns that demand has risen conceals it, so that rivals do not crowd in; a manufacturer with a secret process can hold it for life. The extra gains last as long as the secret.
  • Natural scarcity. Some goods, a wine from one slope for instance, can be grown on so little land that no one can ever supply the whole effectual demand. The excess price is paid to the land, as rent, and the high price may last for centuries.
  • Monopoly and privilege. A monopoly keeps the market understocked by never fully supplying the demand. Corporations and statutes of apprenticeship are “a sort of enlarged monopolies”. Their effect lasts as long as the law that gives them.
