Five Forms Make Twelve

A tense table has twelve boxes. Books print them and ask you to learn them. You do not have to. Every box is built from five forms of the verb, and four of those five follow rules you can state in one line each. This lesson gives you the rules, counts how often they are right on 1,356 real verbs, and shows you every word they get wrong.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas carry the whole lesson.

1

Twelve boxes, but not twelve things to learn

The table has three times across the top and four shapes down the side. That makes twelve boxes. But you never learn twelve forms of a verb, because nine of the boxes are the same few forms with have, be or will placed in front of them. Learn five forms and you can write any box.

2

Four of the five follow a rule

Only the base form has to be learned. The other four are made from it: add -s, add -ing, add -ed. What makes English feel hard is that each rule has a small change for certain endings, and those changes are what this lesson states exactly.

3

A rule is only as good as the count beside it

Anyone can give you a rule. The question no book answers is how often it is right. Here the rules are run over 1,356 verbs and the share they get right is printed next to them, with every word they miss listed. A rule you can trust 99 times in 100 is worth more than a page of advice.

The material

What the rules got wrong

The rules were run over every regular verb in a list of the 2,800 most common words, and checked against the forms that list itself records. Here is how they did, and what they missed.

Open any book for learners and you will find a page with twelve boxes on it. Three times across the top: now, before, later. Four shapes down the side. Every box has a name, and the book asks you to learn the names and the forms that go in them.

You do not have to do that, and this lesson is about why.

The five forms

Every verb in English has five forms, and only five. For walk they are walk, walks, walking, walked, and the form used after have, which for this verb is also walked.

Those five fill all twelve boxes. Nine of the boxes are simply one of these forms with a small word in front of it. Have gives you the shape that looks back from now. Be with the -ing form gives you the shape that is still going on. Will moves the whole thing later. That is the entire table.

The rules that make four of the five

You learn the base form when you learn the word. The other four come from rules, and here they are.

  • For he, she or it, add -s. After a hissing sound, add -es. After a consonant and -y, the -y becomes -ies.
  • For the -ing form, add -ing, dropping a silent -e first.
  • For the past, add -ed, with the same -y change.
  • If the word ends consonant, vowel, consonant and the last part is the strong part, double that last letter before any of the above.

That last rule is the only one needing the sound of the word and not only its letters. Stop is one part, so the last part is the strong part, and it doubles: stopping. Listen has two parts and the strong one is the first, so nothing doubles: listening.

How often the rules are right

A rule with no number beside it is only an opinion. So the rules above were run over the 1,356 regular verbs in a list of the 2,800 most common English words, and their answers were checked against the forms that list already records.

They were right 99.85% of the time for -s, 98.97% for -ing, and 98.67% for the past. The words they missed are shown above, and there are not many. Several are words where British and American writers simply spell the same verb differently, so no rule could pick one.

A rule that sounded right and was not

When the -s rule first ran it missed radio and video, turning them into forms nobody writes. The fix was easy: -o takes -es only after a consonant, as in potatoes, and plain -s after a vowel.

At the same time a second rule was added, because it also looked right: -f becomes -ves. For nouns that is often true. Added here it made the whole thing worse, not better, because brief, golf, proof and roof are verbs and they simply take -s.

This is worth more than any single rule in the lesson. A rule can sound right, come from a real pattern, and still cost you accuracy. The only way to know is to run it over real words and count. That is what every lesson on this path does.

Method

Building a box

To write any box of the table, do this.

1

Start from the base form

The form you would find in a word list. This is the one form you have to learn; the rest are made from it.

2

Make the other four forms

Add -s, -ing and -ed by the rules below, or look the verb up if it is one of the few that break them. The lab names the rule it used beside each form.

3

Choose the time you mean

Now, before, or later. This picks the row of the table.

4

Put the small word in front

Have to look back, be with the -ing form for something still going on, will to move it later. This picks the column, and the box is written.

-s 1354 of 1356 right 99.85% missed: shelf, stomach -ing 1342 of 1356 right 98.97% missed: busing, counselling, formatting, inputting -ed 1338 of 1356 right 98.67% missed: bred, bused, counselled

The misses are not random. Several are words written one way in British English and another way in American English, and both are right. The rules cannot choose for you, and saying so is more use than pretending there is one answer.

Common mistakes

1
Learning the table instead of the rulesTwelve boxes for every verb is a very large number of things to hold. Five forms and four rules is a small one, and it works on a verb you have never met.
2
Doubling the last letter when the stress is earlyStop becomes stopping, but listen does not become listenning. The letter only doubles when the last part of the word is the strong part, which is why the rule needs the sound and not only the letters.
3
Believing a rule because it sounds rightA rule turning -f into -ves looks correct, and for nouns it often is. Added here it made the results worse, because brief, golf, proof and roof are all verbs that simply add -s. The count caught it; the ear did not.

Check your understanding

✓
Finish when you can build the full table for a verb you have never seen, and name the rule that made each form.

You should be able to take any regular verb, write its five forms by rule, say which of the four rules fired and why, fill all twelve boxes by placing have, be and will in front of them, and name the short list of words each rule misses.

Note: The three lists of missed words are short enough to learn directly. That is the whole saving: a rule plus a short list beats a long list.
