Five Forms Make Twelve

A tense table has twelve boxes. Books print them and ask you to learn them. You do not have to. Every box is built from five forms of the verb, and four of those five follow rules you can state in one line each. This lesson gives you the rules, counts how often they are right on 1,210 real verbs, and shows you every word they get wrong.

The five forms, and what fills the table

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas carry the whole lesson.

1

Twelve boxes, but not twelve things to learn

The table has three times down the side and four shapes across the top. That makes twelve boxes. But you never learn twelve forms of a verb, because ten of the boxes are the same few forms with have, be or will placed in front of them, and the other two are a form on its own. Learn five forms and you can write any box.

2

Four of the five follow a rule

Only the base form has to be learned. The other four are made from it: add -s, add -ing, add -ed. What makes English feel hard is that each rule has a small change for certain endings, and those changes are what this lesson states exactly.

3

A rule is only as good as the count beside it

Anyone can give you a rule. The question no book answers is how often it is right. Here the rules are run, in your browser, over 1,210 verbs, and the share they get right is printed next to them with every word they miss. A rule you can trust 99 times in 100 is worth more than a page of advice.

The material

What the rules got wrong

The rules are run over every regular verb in a list of the 2,800 most common words, and checked against the forms that list itself records. The list had to be cleaned first, and the lab prints what was left out and why. Here is how the rules did, and what they missed.

Open any book for learners and you will find a page with twelve boxes on it. Three times: now, before, later. Four shapes for each. Every box has a name, and the book asks you to learn the names and the forms that go in them.

You do not have to do that, and this lesson is about why.

The five forms

A verb in English has five forms. For walk they are walk, walks, walking, walked, and the form used after have, which for this verb is also walked. One verb has more: be has eight (am, is, are, was, were, be, being, been), and the second course sets it apart from every other verb. The small helping words, will among them, have fewer, and they only ever stand in front.

Those five fill all twelve boxes. Two of the boxes, walk and walked, are a form on its own. The other ten are simply one of these forms with a small word in front of it. Have gives you the shape that looks back from now. Be with the -ing form gives you the shape that is still going on. Will moves the whole thing later. That is the entire table.

The rules that make four of the five

You learn the base form when you learn the word. The other four come from rules, and here they are, exactly as the lab runs them.

  • For he, she or it, add -s. After a hissing sound, add -es: passes, wishes, watches. After a consonant and -y, the -y becomes -ies: carries. After a consonant and -o, add -es as well: goes.
  • For the -ing form, add -ing, dropping a silent -e first: hope, hoping.
  • For the past, add -ed. After a silent -e only -d is needed, hope, hoped, and the -y change is the same: carry, carried.
  • If the word ends consonant, vowel, consonant, the last letter is not w, x or y, and the last part is the strong part, double that last letter before -ing and -ed: stop, stopping, stopped. Nothing ever doubles before -s.

That last rule is the only one needing the sound of the word and not only its letters. Stop is one part, so the last part is the strong part, and it doubles: stopping. Listen has two parts and the strong one is the first, so nothing doubles: listening.

How often the rules are right

A rule with no number beside it is only an opinion. So the rules above are run, on this page, over the 1,210 regular verbs in a list of the 2,800 most common English words, and their answers are checked against the forms that list already records.

They are right 99.92% of the time for -s, 99.34% for -ing, and 99.26% for the past. The words they miss are listed in the lab and again in the worked example further down, and there are not many. Each is there for a reason you can name: a British spelling the list records and an American one it does not, a -k the rule does not know about, or a word that doubles when the strong part says it should not.

The list had to be cleaned first

The word list was made by a machine, and a machine can record a spelling nobody writes. The raw list had offerring, commiting and councilling; it gave verb forms to words that are not verbs, such as able and son; and it recorded comed and maked, so the past rule was being marked right for forms nobody writes. Scored against that list, a right rule looks wrong and a wrong rule looks right. So the list was cleaned by one test, applied to every spelling the same way: a spelling stays if a dictionary, a book that records the real spellings of a language, has it, or if it is the British spelling of one the dictionary has. The 90 verbs the second course calls irregular were taken out, because their past is not made by any rule. In all, 146 words were left out, each with its reason, and the lab prints them all under List.

A rule that sounded right and was not

When the -s rule first ran, every -o took -es, and it turned radio and video into forms nobody writes. Choose the -s rule before the -o fix in the lab and you can watch it: 1207 of 1210, 99.75%, with radio and video among the misses. The fix was easy: -o takes -es only after a consonant, as in goes, and plain -s after a vowel.

At the same time a second rule was added, because it also looked right: -f becomes -ves. For nouns that is often true: one leaf, two leaves. Choose it in the lab and the count falls to 1205 of 1210, 99.59%, because brief, golf, proof and roof are verbs and they simply take -s.

This is worth more than any single rule in the lesson. A rule can sound right, come from a real pattern, and still cost you accuracy. The only way to know is to run it over real words and count. That is what every lesson on this path does.

Method

Building a box

To write any box of the table, do this.

1

Start from the base form

The form you would find in a word list. This is the one form you have to learn; the rest are made from it.

2

Make the other four forms

Add -s, -ing and -ed by the rules below, or look the verb up if it is one of the few that break them. The lab names the rule it used beside each form.

3

Choose the time you mean

Now, before, or later. This picks the row of the table.

4

Put the small word in front

Have to look back, be with the -ing form for something still going on, will to move it later. This picks the column, and the box is written.

Three rules, 1,210 verbs, and the words that beat them

The lab runs these same rules on the page. Choose a rule under Score a rule on the list and the count below appears, with every missed word in the table under it.

-s 1209 of 1210 right 99.92% missed: stomach -ing 1202 of 1210 right 99.34% missed: bus, format, initial, input, output, panic, traffic, up -ed 1201 of 1210 right 99.26% missed: bus, counsel, format, initial, input, output, panic, traffic, up

The misses are not random, and not all of them are mistakes. Initial, and counsel in the past, are written one way in British English and another in American English; the list records only the British -ll-, so the rule's American form is marked wrong where nothing is wrong. Panic and traffic take a -k before the ending, panicking, which no rule above knows. Format, input and output double their last letter though the strong part comes first. Bus is busing in the list where the rule writes bussing, and writers use both. Stomach ends in -ch but does not hiss, so stomachs: the rule reads letters and cannot hear. And up has no consonant before its vowel, so the rule never doubles it.

Common mistakes

1
Learning the table instead of the rulesTwelve boxes for every verb is a very large number of things to hold. Five forms and four rules is a small one, and it works on a verb you have never met.
2
Doubling the last letter when the stress is earlyStop becomes stopping, but listen does not become listenning. The letter only doubles when the last part of the word is the strong part, which is why the rule needs the sound and not only the letters.
3
Believing a rule because it sounds rightA rule turning -f into -ves looks correct, and for nouns it often is. Added here it made the results worse, because brief, golf, proof and roof are all verbs that simply add -s. The count caught it; the ear did not.

Check your understanding

✓
Finish when you can build the full table for a verb you have never seen, and name the rule that made each form.

You should be able to take any regular verb, write its five forms by rule, say which of the four rules fired and why, fill all twelve boxes by placing have, be and will in front of them, and name the short list of words each rule misses.

Note: The three lists of missed words are short enough to learn directly. That is the whole saving: a rule plus a short list beats a long list.
