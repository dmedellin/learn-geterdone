Evolving Order and System Metaphor

Chapter 16 asks how a model too large to hold in one head can still be understood as a whole. Read Evans's case for a large-scale structure, his warning against fixing one up front, and his qualified case for a System Metaphor; then count how far four changes to a bike-share reach under a packaging decided early and one that grew with the changes.

Evolving Order in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Large-scale structure

A small set of high-level rules, roles or concepts that applies across a whole system, so that a developer can say what part a piece plays without knowing its details. It shapes the design but usually does not appear in it as a class or a package.

2

Evolving Order

Evans's pattern for how such a structure should be found: let it grow and change with the application, even into a different kind of structure, instead of deciding it before the domain is understood.

3

Modules touched by a change

For one change, the number of distinct modules holding an element the change edits. The lab adds these up across a list of changes and counts the changes that stay inside one module.

The material

The chapter's problem, then two patterns, then the count

Read the opening of Chapter 16, Evolving Order and System Metaphor in your own copy first. The lesson reads the opening as the chapter's problem and the two patterns as Evans's first answers to it, and then counts one consequence of fixing a structure early.

“The problem is not the existence of guiding rules, but rather the rigidity and source of those rules.” The sentence is from Evolving Order, the first pattern of Chapter 16, Large-Scale Structure. This lesson reads the chapter's opening, that pattern, and System Metaphor. Read them in your own copy first.

The chapter opens on a team building a simulator of a satellite communications network. They had broken a hard model into many sensible modules, and the modules had multiplied until nobody could say where a new class belonged. Their way out was not another package but a story about the system as a whole, a stack of conceptual layers from the physical links upward, which they refined while refactoring the code to fit it. Evans uses the story to name what was missing: not a class, but a structure for the model as a whole.

His argument for needing one runs through the strategic tools you have already met. Modules cut a design into pieces of a manageable size, but there may be a great many of them, and they need not be designed alike. Bounded Contexts keep models from corrupting one another but do not by themselves show the whole. Distillation picks out the Core Domain, yet the supporting parts still have to be understood in relation to it. On a project of any size people work apart, and without some shared rules they solve the same problem in different ways, become specialists who cannot help one another, and lose sight of how the parts fit.

A large-scale structure, in Evans's terms, is a language for discussing the system in broad strokes: rules or roles that span the design and let anyone say what a part is for without reading its details. It may live in one Bounded Context but usually spans several. It is mostly not drawn in UML and does not need to be; it explains the model rather than appearing in it. And he is plain that a small team with a model of modest size may need nothing more than well-named modules, some distillation and informal coordination.

Evolving Order is the pattern for how the structure arrives. Evans sets two failures against each other. A design free-for-all produces a system no one can understand as a whole. An architecture imposed up front, especially one that reaches into the domain layer, freezes decisions made before the domain was understood, and as requirements change it fights the developers: they simplify the application to fit it, or route around it until there is no structure at all. His resolution is the quotation's: keep guiding rules, but let them grow from the application and change with it, perhaps into a different kind of structure altogether, and leave detailed modelling decisions to the people who have the detailed knowledge.

He does not pretend the choice is free. A structure favours the manageability of the whole over the best shape of each part, so some parts will be expressed less naturally than they could be. A structure that really fits pays that back: it rules out many options at once, gives shortcuts to decisions that would otherwise be argued object by object, and helps different people reach consistent answers. It must leave teams in different contexts room for their local models, and it must bend around parts nobody controls, such as an external or legacy system.

Two more qualifications close the pattern. A Context Map is always needed; a large-scale structure is not, worth imposing only when one is found that clarifies the system without forcing unnatural constraints; because a structure that fits badly is worse than none, Evans advises a minimal set aimed at the problems that have actually appeared. A structure may tolerate a few exceptions if they are marked, but when they become numerous the structure should be changed or dropped. He then offers four structures he has seen emerge on projects, not as a catalogue but as starting points: this lesson's metaphor, and the three that follow.

System Metaphor comes from Extreme Programming, where it is one of the core practices, and Evans credits Ward Cunningham's firewall with making it click for him. The firewall picture made a whole product category easy to grasp, yet its power cuts both ways: it suggested a barrier at the edge and so said nothing about threats from inside. A metaphor, Evans says, is a loose structure that sits comfortably with objects, and because it is only an analogy, models in several contexts can map to it approximately. The risk is that a persuasive analogy carries in features the problem does not have, and he notes that few projects have found a really useful one.

So the pattern is conditional. Evans would take up a metaphor only when one has turned up that the team finds compelling and that points its thinking somewhere productive. Then it shapes the design and enters the Ubiquitous Language, and because no analogy is exact it stays under review, to be abandoned once it misleads. A sidebar disposes of the XP community's “naive metaphor”, the domain model itself: a model refined through many rounds of knowledge crunching is anything but naive, and Evans would retire the term. Among the twelve XP practices, he says, a Ubiquitous Language can play the metaphor's role.

Now the count. The lab holds one small bike-share model of eight elements under two packagings. The first was fixed before the domain was understood, one package for each screen of the app: rent-screen, return-screen and admin-screen. The second grew as the team learned which things change together: fleet for bikes, docks and stations, riding for riders, memberships and trips, and pricing for fares and tariffs. Four changes arrive, each touching two elements.

  • By screen: rent-screen holds Bike, Rider, Fare; return-screen holds Dock, Trip, Tariff; admin-screen holds Station, Membership.
  • Grown: fleet holds Bike, Dock, Station; riding holds Rider, Membership, Trip; pricing holds Fare, Tariff.
  • Changes: weekend-tariff touches Fare and Tariff; dock-sensor touches Dock and Station; family-plan touches Membership and Rider; ride-time-cap touches Trip and Fare.

On the preset screens-against-grown the screen packaging gives 4 changes, 2 modules for every one of them, 8 module touches in all, and 0 changes confined to one module. The grown packaging gives 5 touches: three changes stay in one module, and ride-time-cap, the largest, reaches 2, riding and pricing. The count is not a verdict on screens as packages. It shows one consequence of deciding the structure before the changes were known: the screens were a guess about what would vary, and the changes did not vary that way.

The preset a-change-neither-fits adds a fifth change, rebalancing-credit, which pays riders who return a bike to an emptying station and touches Station, Trip, Tariff and Rider. It reaches 3 modules under both packagings, so the totals become 11 against 8 and the largest change is the new one under each. The grown structure was found from the first four changes and does not help with a kind of change it never saw. That is the case Evolving Order is written for: the structure is a current best reading of the domain, and a new kind of requirement is a reason to look at it again.

Exercise

  • A seed bank, packaged by department up front: intake holds SeedLot and Donor; storage holds VaultShelf and GerminationTest; outreach holds Request and Packet.
  • The same elements, packaged as the team learned: viability holds SeedLot and GerminationTest; holdings holds VaultShelf and Packet; members holds Donor and Request.
  • Changes: retest-interval touches GerminationTest and SeedLot; member-quota touches Request and Donor; packet-size touches Packet, VaultShelf and Request.

By hand, find the modules each change touches under each packaging, the total module touches, and the changes confined to one module. Then type the model into the lab to check, the department packaging in the column the lab labels by screen and the learned one in the column labelled as it grew, and say in a sentence what the department packaging assumed about how the seed bank would change.

A structure that grows with the system still has to be some particular structure. The next lesson, Responsibility Layers, reads the one Evans develops at greatest length: layers cut along the domain's own strata.

Method

Reading a packaging against its changes

Four steps, the last of them on a system of your own.

1

Write each packaging as a table

List every element once with the module it sits in, one column a packaging, so that both can be read against the same changes.

2

Mark each change's modules

For each change, look up the module of every element it touches and count the distinct modules. A change that touches two elements in one module counts one.

3

Add up, and look at the largest

Sum the touches for each packaging, count the changes confined to one module, and read the largest change: it is the one the structure fits worst.

4

Do it on your own system

Take the last five changes merged into a system you know. Write down the packages each touched, total the touches, and say in one sentence what kind of change the current packaging seems to expect.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking Evolving Order means having no structure until late in the projectEvans argues against a design free-for-all as firmly as against a straitjacket; the problem he names is the rigidity and source of the rules, not their existence. Refactoring Toward a Fitting Structure suggests starting early with something loose, a metaphor or a couple of layers, and changing it as understanding grows. On the lesson's illustration the grown packaging is itself a structure; what evolves is which one.
2
Adopting a System Metaphor because every project should have oneEvans says few projects have found a really useful metaphor, that people have pushed the idea where it does harm, and that no project strictly needs a large-scale structure. The pattern is for the case where a compelling analogy has already turned up, and it tells the team to abandon the analogy once it misleads.
3
Reading a lower touch count as proof that one packaging is betterThe lab counts how far the lesson's changes reach under each packaging. A different list of changes gives different figures, and the second preset shows one change that both packagings handle equally badly. Evans chooses a structure by whether it clarifies the domain for the people working in it, which no count of four changes settles.
