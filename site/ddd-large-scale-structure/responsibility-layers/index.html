Responsibility Layers

Responsibility Layers joins two ideas, layering and responsibility-driven design, at the scale of the whole model. Read Evans's case for layers that tell the domain's story, the five he has seen recur, and his advice on choosing them; then classify a theatre box office's dependencies against four layers and find the two that run upward.

Responsibility Layers in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter's, the third the lab's.

1

Responsibility layer

A broad responsibility, such as what the business could do or what it is doing now, cast as a layer. Every object, aggregate and module is factored to sit inside one of them.

2

Relaxed layering

The variant of the layering pattern Evans prefers here: a layer may use any layer below it, not only the one directly beneath. Only dependencies that run upward break it.

3

Upward dependency

A dependency from an element in one layer to an element in a higher layer. The lab lists each one; a dependency that skips over a layer on its way down is counted separately and is allowed.

The material

The pattern, the layers Evans has seen, then the count

Read Responsibility Layers and Choosing Appropriate Layers in your own copy first. The lesson reads the pattern as a tool, summarises the book's long example, and then classifies every dependency of a small model of its own.

“Choosing a large-scale structure is less a technical decision than a business modeling decision.” That line is from Choosing Appropriate Layers, at the end of the second pattern of Chapter 16, Responsibility Layers. Read the pattern and that section in your own copy first.

Evans starts from objects. Throughout the book each object has a narrow set of related responsibilities, but when every one of them is handcrafted there is no way to reason about large regions of the model at once. In some domains a deep understanding reveals a natural stratification: some concepts sit against a background of others that change independently, at a different rate and for different reasons. Layering is the established way to exploit that, and the chapter contrasts it with the layering that sorts itself out when you draw a module dependency diagram, which eases tracing but says little about the model.

The pattern unites the two ideas. Name a few broad responsibilities the domain itself shows, far wider than any object's, and refactor until every object, aggregate and module belongs to exactly one of them. Members of a layer may use the layers beneath and know nothing of those above. Evans prefers the relaxed variant, Buschmann and his co-authors' Relaxed Layered System, in which a layer may reach any lower layer, not only the next. The layers should tell the story of the system's purpose. This is a different cut from the Layered Architecture of Chapter 4, which separates user interface, application, domain and infrastructure; Responsibility Layers divides the domain layer itself.

The book's example is long, and only its shape is summarised here. Evans takes the shipping model of earlier chapters and has its team notice that schedules can be discussed without the cargo they carry, but not the other way round. They name two layers, then a third for the routing service once they see it helps decide rather than records what is. One attribute turns out to be a policy hiding in a capability object, and moving it out makes the model clearer. Then a new regulation arrives, and the first design that occurs to them would make an operational object depend on a decision-support service; they choose another that respects the layers. Evans is careful to say the second design is not simply better. Consistency across a team is worth modest trade-offs, and if the structure keeps forcing awkward choices, Evolving Order says to revise it.

Choosing Appropriate Layers gives three things to look for and preserve as layers are split, merged and redefined. Storytelling: the layers should bring out the business's priorities, which is the point of the quotation. Conceptual dependency: upper-layer concepts should have meaning against the lower ones, while lower ones make sense alone. And conceptual contours: where objects change at different rates or for different reasons, a layer boundary should run along that shearing line.

Then come five layers Evans has seen recur in enterprise systems, offered as examples rather than a template. Potential asks what the business could do: its resources, people and contracts. Operations asks what it is doing, and like Potential records reality as it is; an operational object may refer to potential ones, never the reverse. Decision Support asks what should be done, analysing the layers beneath. Policy holds rules and goals that constrain the other layers, sometimes passed into a lower method, sometimes as a Strategy. Commitment, which comes to the fore where capacity depends on current business, as in finance or insurance, records what has been promised.

He ends on restraint. Keep the layering simple: beyond four, or possibly five, layers stop telling a story, and the complexity they were meant to tame returns in another form. The five layers fit a range of enterprise systems, but a domain unlike those may need layers of its own, and forcing it into these would do harm. Start somewhere, he says, and let the order evolve.

Now the count. The lab holds a theatre box office in four layers, top to bottom decision, policy, operations and potential, with eight elements: Auditorium, Seat and Usher are potential; Performance, Booking and Ticket are operations; ConcessionRule is policy; PricingAdvisor is decision support. The first version has eleven dependencies, and among them a booking that holds its concession rule, like this:

// Operations: one booking of seats for one performance.
class Booking {
    private final Performance performance;
    private final List<Ticket> tickets = new ArrayList<>();
    private final ConcessionRule concessions;   // a Policy object

    long priceCents() {
        long total = 0;
        for (Ticket t : tickets) {
            total += concessions.priceCents(t); // operations asks policy
        }
        return total;
    }
}

On the preset as-first-written the lab counts 11 dependencies: 6 downward, 3 within a layer (Seat on Auditorium, Booking on Performance and on Ticket) and 2 upward, listed as Booking on ConcessionRule and Auditorium on Performance, the second because each auditorium kept a list of its performances. One downward dependency, PricingAdvisor on Booking, skips over the policy layer, which relaxed layering allows; the lab counts it, 1, and does not list it as a fault. No layer is empty: the tile for layers with no element reads none, and a layer the story names but nothing fills would be worth asking about.

The preset refactored turns the first upward dependency round, as below: the rule reads the booking, and the booking never names the rule. The auditorium's list goes, replaced by asking a repository for an auditorium's performances. The lab then counts 10 dependencies, 7 downward, 3 within and 0 upward, with the same one skip. Whether the policy should own the price at all is a modelling question the count does not answer; what it shows is that every remaining dependency now runs in the direction the layers' story says.

// Policy: the rule reads the booking; the booking never names the rule.
class ConcessionRule {
    long priceCents(Booking booking) {
        long total = 0;
        for (Ticket t : booking.tickets()) {
            total += priceCents(t);
        }
        return total;
    }

    long priceCents(Ticket t) {
        return t.isConcession() ? t.faceCents() / 2 : t.faceCents();
    }
}

Exercise

  • A clinic in the same four layers: Clinician and Room are potential; Appointment and Visit are operations; BookingLimit is policy; ScheduleOptimizer is decision.
  • Dependencies, written dependent first: Appointment on Clinician; Appointment on Room; Visit on Appointment; Clinician on Appointment; BookingLimit on Appointment; ScheduleOptimizer on Room; Appointment on BookingLimit.

By hand, sort the seven dependencies into downward, within a layer and upward, name the upward ones, and count the downward ones that skip a layer. Then type the model into the lab and compare.

How the lab counts. The layers are listed top to bottom. A dependency from an element in a higher layer to one in a lower layer runs downward, one between two elements of the same layer is within it, and one from a lower layer to a higher runs upward, which a strict layering forbids; upward ones are listed in the order given. Downward counts every downward dependency, and of those, skipping a layer counts the ones that pass over at least one layer between (allowed in a relaxed layering, counted separately, not as violations). So downward + within + upward = all.

Method

Checking a model against its layers

Four steps, the last of them on a system of your own.

1

Write the layers and their members

List the layers top to bottom and put every element in exactly one. An element you cannot place is a question about the layers, not a mistake in the list.

2

Classify each dependency

For each dependency, compare the layer of the dependent with the layer of the one it uses: higher to lower is downward, the same is within, lower to higher is upward.

3

Decide what each upward one means

For every upward dependency, say whether the dependency can be turned round, replaced by a query, or is evidence that an element sits in the wrong layer or that the layers need revising.

4

Do it on your own system

Pick four broad responsibilities in a system you know, assign ten of its classes to them, list the dependencies among those ten, and count the upward ones.

Common mistakes

1
Taking Responsibility Layers for the user interface, application, domain and infrastructure layersThose are Chapter 4's Layered Architecture, which isolates the domain from everything else in the program. Responsibility Layers divides the domain layer itself, by broad responsibilities in the business such as what could be done and what is being done, and the choice of them is, in the quotation's words, a business modelling decision.
2
Treating Evans's five layers as a template every system should fillChoosing Appropriate Layers offers Potential, Operations, Decision Support, Policy and Commitment as layers seen in a range of enterprise systems, warns that forcing a different domain into them would be counterproductive, and says a domain unlike these may need layers of its own. Evans's advice is to start somewhere and let the order evolve.
3
Counting a dependency that skips a layer as a violationEvans adopts the relaxed variant of layering, in which a layer may use any layer below it. Only dependencies that run upward break the structure. The lab counts skipping dependencies so that you can see them, and lists only the upward ones as faults.

Check your understanding

✓
Finish when you can check a model against its responsibility layers.

Given layers, an assignment of elements and a list of dependencies, sort every dependency into downward, within or upward, name the upward ones, and count the downward ones that skip a layer.

Note: The lab checks dependencies against the layers you give it. It cannot say whether those layers tell the domain's story, whether an element belongs in the layer you put it in, or which of two designs that both respect the layers is better; Evans treats those as modelling decisions made with domain experts.
