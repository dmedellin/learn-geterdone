Services

Evans's third kind of model element is not an object of the domain at all but something done on request. Read his three marks of a good service, how to tell domain services from application and infrastructure ones, and what a service does for granularity; then count the uses an exchange of theatre tickets drags into the ticket, and where they go when the exchange becomes a service.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the section's, the third the lab's, from Code Complete's counts of a design.

1

Service

An operation that stands on its own in the model, offered through an interface; it holds no state of its own, defined by what it does for a client. A domain service carries a meaning of the business; application and infrastructure services do not.

2

Stateless

In Evans's sense, any client can use any instance of the service without regard to its history. The service may read and even change information held elsewhere; it keeps no state of its own that alters its behaviour.

3

Fan-in and fan-out

For one class, the number of classes that use it (fan-in) and the number it uses (fan-out). The lab reports the largest of each and the share of possible pairs of classes that are connected.

The material

The pattern, the layers it lives in, then the count

Read Services and its three subsections in your own copy first. The lesson reads the pattern as a tool, then counts what it moves.

“Sometimes, it just isn't a thing.” That is the first line of the pattern Services, the last of Chapter 5's three kinds of model element. This lesson reads it with Services and the Isolated Domain Layer, Granularity, and Access to Services. Enterprise Application Patterns reads Randy Stafford's Service Layer in its lesson Service Layer, a boundary of application operations over the domain; Evans's service is narrower in scope, can live in any layer, and the one he cares most about is a concept of the domain itself.

Some important operations of a domain have no natural home in an entity or a value, because they are activities rather than things. Evans names two mistakes. The more common one is to give up too soon on finding the right object and drift into procedural code. The other is to force the operation onto an object it does not fit: the object loses its clarity, the operation can swamp it, and since such operations usually bring several domain objects together, the host acquires dependencies on all of them. A third shape he notes with some sympathy: the “doer” with a name ending in Manager, a class with no state and no meaning beyond the operation it hosts, which at least spares the real objects.

A service is defined purely by what it does for a client, and it tends to be named for an activity, a verb rather than a noun. It still has a defined responsibility, part of the model, with an operation name from the Ubiquitous Language and parameters and results that are domain objects. Evans asks that services be used judiciously, never to strip entities and values of their behaviour. A good one has three characteristics: the operation stands for a concept of the domain that does not belong naturally to an entity or a value; its interface is expressed through other elements of the model; and it is stateless.

Services and the Isolated Domain Layer sorts them by layer. Most services in the literature are technical and belong in the infrastructure, such as the interface to an e-mail system. Application and domain services are harder to tell apart, and Evans's test is business meaning. In his banking example, exporting transactions to a spreadsheet is an application service, since file formats mean nothing to banking, while a funds transfer is a domain service, because it is a term of the business and embeds its rules. Putting the transfer on an account would be awkward, since it involves two accounts and some global rules; the service itself asks the accounts to do most of the work. Deciding that a balance has fallen below a threshold is not a service at all; it fits the account.

Granularity adds a second reason for the pattern. Medium-grained, stateless services put significant behaviour behind a simple interface, which is easier to reuse in a large system and cheaper to call across a network than many fine-grained objects. Without them the application layer coordinates the domain objects in detail, and domain knowledge leaks up into it. The pattern favours a simple interface over the client's control. Access to Services is brief: how a service is reached matters less than the decision to carve it off, and a plain object or a Singleton will often do, with elaborate distribution frameworks kept for when they are actually needed.

Now the count, on a theatre box office where a customer exchanges a ticket for another performance. Exchange is a word the box office uses, and it involves the ticket held, the performance wanted, its seat plan, the price list and the refunds. The lab here is Code Complete's dependency count, its coupling view: each record is one use, written user, used. On the preset exchange-on-the-ticket the exchange is a method of Ticket, as below. The lab counts 6 classes, 6 uses, connected pairs 2/5 = 40% of the 15 possible, the largest fan-out Ticket at 4 and the largest fan-in SeatPlan at 2.

// Forced onto the entity: a ticket that knows prices and refunds.
class Ticket {
    private final Buyer buyer;
    private Performance performance;
    private Seat seat;
    private final PriceList prices;   // here only for exchange
    private final Refunds refunds;    // here only for exchange

    void exchangeFor(Performance wanted, SeatChoice choice) {
        Seat next = wanted.seatPlan().reserve(choice);
        Money diff = prices.priceOf(next).minus(prices.priceOf(seat));
        refunds.settle(buyer, diff);
        performance.seatPlan().release(seat);
        performance = wanted;
        seat = next;
    }
}

The ticket now holds a price list and a refunds account for the sake of one operation, which is the dependency Evans warns of. The preset exchange-as-a-service moves the operation into a domain service, TicketExchange, and the ticket keeps only its own performance. The lab counts 7 classes, 8 uses, connected pairs 8/21 ≈ 38.1%, the largest fan-out TicketExchange at 5, and the largest fan-in Performance at 2, tied with SeatPlan. Ticket's own fan-out, which you can read off the records, falls from 4 to 1.

// A domain service: named for the activity, in the model's terms.
public interface TicketExchange {
    Ticket exchange(Ticket held, Performance wanted, SeatChoice choice);
}

final class BoxOfficeExchange implements TicketExchange {
    private final PriceList prices;
    private final Refunds refunds;

    public Ticket exchange(Ticket held, Performance wanted, SeatChoice choice) {
        Seat seat = wanted.seatPlan().reserve(choice);
        refunds.settle(held.buyer(),
                prices.priceOf(seat).minus(held.pricePaid()));
        held.surrender();
        return Ticket.issue(held.buyer(), wanted, seat);
    }
}

Read the numbers honestly. The design with a service has more classes and more uses, not fewer; the connections have moved, from an entity that should be about who holds which seat to an operation that is about exchanging. That is the trade Evans describes, and the count shows it without judging it. The service holds a price list and a refunds account, but nothing that changes how it behaves from one call to the next, so it is stateless in his sense. Whether the box office really thinks of exchange as an activity, rather than something a ticket does, is the question that decides it, and it is asked of the experts, not the lab.

Exercise

  • Four operations in a bike-share: (a) when a ride ends, close it, have the fare worked out, charge the member and ask for a receipt to be sent; (b) choose which dock the rebalancing van should empty next, from the fill level of every dock and the network's rebalancing rules; (c) send a push notification to a phone; (d) work out a ride's fare from its length.

Sort each into an application service, a domain service, an infrastructure service, or no service at all, and give the reason Evans's test supplies. For the domain service, write its interface in the model's terms.

Method

Deciding whether an operation is a service

Four steps, the last of them on a design of your own.

1

Try the objects first

Ask whether the operation is a natural responsibility of one entity or value. If it is, put it there; the common mistake is to give up too soon.

2

Apply the three marks

If not, check that the operation is a domain concept, that its interface can be written in the model's terms, and that it can be stateless.

3

Find its layer

Ask whether it means something to the business. If it embeds business rules it is a domain service; if it only coordinates or formats, an application service; if it is purely technical, infrastructure.

4

Do it on your own code

Find a class in a system you know that holds a field only one method uses. Type its uses into the lab, then the uses with that method moved into a service, and write down the class's fan-out before and after.

Common mistakes

1
Making every operation a serviceEvans calls the more common mistake giving up too easily on fitting behaviour into the right object, and drifting toward procedural code. Services are to be used judiciously and never allowed to strip entities and values of their behaviour; a balance check belongs to the account, not to a service.
2
Calling any class with Service in its name a domain serviceMost services are technical and belong to the infrastructure, and many others are application services that coordinate and format. A domain service carries a meaning of the business and embeds its rules, like a funds transfer; a spreadsheet export does not, whatever it is called.
3
Thinking a stateless service cannot change anythingStatelessness, for Evans, means any client can use any instance without regard to its history. The service may read global information and change it as a side effect; it holds no state of its own that changes how it behaves.
