The Flat Vowel

The strong part of a word keeps its vowel. The parts that are not strong are said with one short, plain sound, the flat vowel, whether they are spelled a, e, o or u. Counted on every word of two or more parts in the 2,800, it is more than half of all the weak parts, and the two kinds that are not flat are easy to name.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas. The first two connect this lesson to the last three.

1

The weak part is every part that is not strong

The first three lessons found the strong part. The rest of the word is weak, apart from a part that carries a smaller beat, as the first part of education does. The strong part keeps a clear vowel; a weak part gives up its vowel.

2

The vowel letter does not tell you the sound

About, today, listen and minute have a weak part spelled with a, o, e and u. In all four it is said the same, with a short, plain sound that a mouth makes when it does not try. This is the flat vowel.

3

Two kinds of weak part are not flat

The y at the end of happy is said as the vowel of see. The ending of active and basic takes the vowel of sit. A weak part is not always flat, but it is never as clear as a strong one.

The material

What the weak parts say

Out of 2628 weak parts in 1779 words, four kinds of vowel account for nearly all.

Say about slowly: a-bout. Now say it as you would in a sentence. The first part is so short that you hardly hear a vowel in it. The second carries the word. The first lesson of this course found where the strong part goes. This one is about the parts around it.

Weak parts

Every part of a word that is not the strong part is weak. A weak part is said shorter and quieter, and its vowel loses its clear shape. The sound it falls to most often has a name in this course: the flat vowel. It is the short, plain sound at the start of about, said with no effort, and it is the commonest vowel among the weak parts counted here.

What was counted

The lab takes every word of two or more parts in the 2,800 and keeps the parts the dictionary marks with no beat at all. A part with a smaller beat, such as the first part of education, keeps a clear vowel and is not counted, and a word whose other parts all carry one, such as airline, is not in the table. That leaves 1779 words and 2628 weak parts. For each weak part the lab reads the vowel from the pronouncing dictionary.

  • The flat vowel: 1387 of 2628, 52.8%. About, today, listen.
  • The flat vowel with r: 400, 15.2%. The ending of another and number.
  • The vowel of sit: 352, 13.4%. Active, basic, morning.
  • The vowel of see: 348, 13.2%. Happy, city, money.
  • Another vowel: the other 141, 5.4%. A part with no beat that keeps a clear vowel all the same, as the first part of accept and employ does.

Why the spelling hides it

The flat vowel has no letter of its own. It is written with whichever vowel letter the word had when it was written down. In the lab, find about, today, listen and minute in the table, and read the kind of their weak parts. They are all the same kind, and the four spellings are the four letters in the worked example.

The two kinds that are not flat

Most of the 348 parts that say the vowel of see are the -y at the end of a word such as happy, city or money. It is weak, and it is not flat. The rest are mostly an i or e said before another vowel, as in radio, area and create, or the front piece re- of remind and report. The vowel of sit belongs to endings such as -ive in active, -ic in basic and -ing in morning, evening and nothing. The list holds few words that end in -ing, since it holds base forms, but the ones it holds follow the rule.

Both are still weak. Say the y of happy quickly and lightly, and give the push to the first part.

What this does to the lessons before it

The rules of the last three lessons say where the strong part is. This lesson says what the rest sounds like. A word said with a clear strong part and flat, quiet parts around it is a word a listener hears at once, even if one sound is wrong.

Two limits. The sounds are American, from the dictionary's first reading of each word, and a British speaker may use a different vowel in some weak parts. And the table counts the vowel of a part, not how long or how loud the part is.

Method

Saying the weak parts of a word

Four steps, in this order.

1

Find the strong part

Use the rules of the last three lessons, or the dictionary. In about it is bout.

2

Mark every other part as weak

The first part of about, the second of listen and the last of minute.

3

Say each weak part with the flat vowel, not the letter

Short, quiet, and the same sound for every vowel letter.

4

Check the end of the word

A y at the end says the vowel of see. -ive, -ic and -ing say the vowel of sit. A part ending in r is the flat vowel with an r.

Common mistakes

1
Reading every vowel letter as itselfA reader who says a in about, o in today and e in listen as those letters gives every part the same weight. In a weak part the letter mostly collapses to one sound, 1387 times in 2628, and 400 more times with an r after it.
2
Thinking the flat vowel is a careless way to speakIt is the standard way. The dictionary marks it for more than half of all the weak parts in the 2,800 words. A speaker who leaves it out is the one who sounds odd.
3
Thinking every weak part is flatThe end of happy is the vowel of see and the end of active is the vowel of sit. These two kinds are 700 of the 2628, and the table lists them word by word.

Check your understanding

✓
Finish when you can say a weak part of a word as the flat vowel instead of reading the letter, and name the endings that do not use it.

You should be able to pick out the weak parts of a word, say each as the flat vowel, read the share of weak parts that take it off the table, and name the two kinds that take another vowel.

Note: The flat vowel is the commonest vowel among the weak parts counted here. A student who says it well sounds clearer than one who says every vowel clearly, because the strong parts stand out.
