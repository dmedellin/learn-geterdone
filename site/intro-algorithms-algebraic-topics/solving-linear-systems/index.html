Solving Systems of Linear Equations

Section 28.1 solves Ax = b by an LUP decomposition: a permutation P, a unit lower-triangular L and an upper-triangular U with PA = LU, computed in Θ(n³) by elimination with pivoting, after which forward and back substitution solve for x in Θ(n²). Section 28.1 in seven lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas: the factorization, the entry it divides by, and the two cheap solves it makes possible.

1

LUP decomposition

Three n × n matrices with PA = LU: L is unit lower-triangular, with ones on the diagonal and zeros above; U is upper-triangular; P is a permutation matrix, stored as an array π in which π[i] names the row of A that becomes row i of PA. Every nonsingular matrix has one.

2

Pivot

The entry elimination divides by at each step, the top-left entry of what is left of the matrix. A zero pivot is a division by zero and a tiny one magnifies rounding error, so LUP-DECOMPOSITION first swaps up the entry of largest absolute value in the current column.

3

Forward and back substitution

A triangular system is solved one unknown at a time. With L lower-triangular, y₁ comes from the first equation, then y₂ from the second, and so on down; with U upper-triangular, xₙ comes first and the rest follow upward. Each costs Θ(n²).

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the arithmetic

Read section 28.1 closely and sections 28.2 and 28.3 for their results. The lesson sets out the decomposition and both substitutions in its own words, works one solve by hand, and decomposes matrices exactly in the lab.

“Because operations on matrices lie at the heart of scientific computing, efficient algorithms for working with matrices have many practical applications.” Chapter 28 takes up the most common of them, solving n linear equations in n unknowns, and two relatives: inverting a matrix, and fitting a curve by least squares. The authors flag numerical stability, the way rounding errors can grow in floating point, as a real concern they mostly leave to specialist texts. The lab here sidesteps it by computing in exact fractions.

Write the system as Ax = b with A nonsingular, so that x exists and is unique. One could compute A⁻¹ and multiply, but the authors point out that this is numerically unstable in practice, and LUP decomposition is both stable and faster. The plan is to find P, L and U with PA = LU. Multiplying Ax = b by P permutes the equations, so LUx = Pb. Name y = Ux; solve Ly = Pb for y by forward substitution, then Ux = y for x by back substitution. LUP-SOLVE is those two loops, Θ(n²) in all.

Theorem · Forward and back substitution

With L unit lower-triangular, yᵢ = b[π[i]] − (lᵢ₁·y₁ + … + lᵢ,ᵢ₋₁·yᵢ₋₁) for i from 1 up to n. With U upper-triangular, xᵢ = (yᵢ − (uᵢ,ᵢ₊₁·xᵢ₊₁ + … + uᵢₙ·xₙ))/uᵢᵢ for i from n down to 1. Each unknown costs one pass over a row, so each solve is Θ(n²).

The factoring is Gaussian elimination, written recursively. Split A into its top-left entry a₁₁, the rest of the first column v, the rest of the first row wᵀ, and the remaining (n − 1) × (n − 1) block A′. Then the first column of L is v divided by a₁₁, the first row of U is a₁₁ followed by wᵀ, and what remains to factor is the Schur complement A′ − v·wᵀ/a₁₁: A′ with a multiple of the first row subtracted from each later row to clear the first column. LU-DECOMPOSITION repeats this on the Schur complement, Θ(n³) in all, and can store L and U in place of A.

The recursion divides by a₁₁. If it is zero the method fails even when A is nonsingular, and if it is small the division magnifies rounding error. LUP-DECOMPOSITION therefore exchanges rows before each step, bringing up the entry of greatest absolute value in the current column, and records the exchange in π. If every candidate in the column is zero, the matrix is singular and the procedure says so. The authors show that the Schur complement of a nonsingular matrix is nonsingular, so the recursion never runs out of pivots on a nonsingular input. The cost is still Θ(n³).

Example · A zero in the corner

Take A = [[0, 2], [3, 1]] and b = (4, 5). Elimination without a row exchange would divide by 0. With the exchange, π = 2, 1, PA is [[3, 1], [0, 2]], which is already upper-triangular, so L = I and U = [[3, 1], [0, 2]]. Then Pb = (5, 4), forward substitution gives y = (5, 4), and back substitution gives x₂ = 4/2 = 2 and x₁ = (5 − 1·2)/3 = 1.

Section 28.2 asks how inversion compares with multiplication. Inverting A with an LUP decomposition means solving Ax = eᵢ for each column eᵢ of the identity: one Θ(n³) factoring and n solves of Θ(n²), Θ(n³) in all. Theorems 28.1 and 28.2 then show that, under mild regularity conditions, multiplying and inverting n × n matrices have the same asymptotic cost, so a faster multiplication such as Strassen's gives a faster inversion.

Section 28.3 treats symmetric positive-definite matrices, those with xᵀAx > 0 for every nonzero x. Their Schur complements are again symmetric positive-definite, so LU decomposition never divides by zero and needs no pivoting (Corollary 28.6). The section ends with least squares: given more data points than unknowns, choose the coefficients c that minimize the squared error, which leads to the normal equations AᵀA·c = Aᵀy. When A has full column rank, AᵀA is symmetric positive-definite, and the earlier machinery solves them.

Now the lab. It runs LUP-DECOMPOSITION in exact rational arithmetic, choosing the largest absolute value in each column and the first on a tie, and, given b, runs LUP-SOLVE and checks Ax = b exactly. Matrices are typed row by row, one row a line; the entries are whole numbers or decimals, and every result is a fraction. Its tiles are π, the pivots in order, L and U row by row with rows separated by semicolons, the determinant, the solution and the check.

The lesson's matrix, preset three, is [[1, 3, 2], [2, 2, 5], [4, 1, 3]] with b = (1, 7, 10). The lab reports π = 3, 1, 2, pivots 4, 11/4, 31/11, L with rows 1, 0, 0; 1/4, 1, 0; 1/2, 6/11, 1, U with rows 4, 1, 3; 0, 11/4, 5/4; 0, 0, 31/11, determinant 31 and x = 2, −1, 1, checked exactly. Preset zero is the example above: π = 2, 1 and x = 1, 2. Preset four is a 4 × 4 system whose solution is all ones; its third pivot is −2/3, chosen over −1/3 because the rule compares absolute values.

The determinant tile uses a fact the decomposition gives for free: det A is the product of the pivots, with its sign changed once for each row exchange. For preset three, 4 · 11/4 · 31/11 = 31, and π is a cycle of three rows, made by two exchanges, so the sign stays. Type a singular matrix such as [[1, 2], [2, 4]] and the lab refuses it, naming the column that has no nonzero pivot.

Method

An LUP solve by hand

Keep π beside the matrix; every row exchange is recorded there.

1

Choose the pivot

In the current column, on and below the diagonal, find the entry of largest absolute value and swap its whole row up, recording the swap in π. If all are zero, stop: the matrix is singular.

2

Eliminate below it

For each row below, the multiplier is its entry divided by the pivot. Store the multiplier as the entry of L, and subtract the multiplier times the pivot row from the rest of that row.

3

Repeat on the smaller block

Move one row down and one column right and do the same until the last pivot. What is left on and above the diagonal is U.

4

Solve the two triangles

Permute b by π, solve Ly = Pb from the top down, then Ux = y from the bottom up, and substitute x back into the original equations.

5

Do it on a system of your own

Write a 3 × 3 matrix of your own with a 0 in the top-left corner, choose an integer x, compute b = Ax, and check that the lab recovers your x and swaps the row you expected.

The lesson's system, solved from its factors

Use the factors the lab prints for preset three and solve for b = (1, 7, 10).

Pb = (b₃, b₁, b₂) = (10, 1, 7) y₁ = 10 y₂ = 1 − (1/4)·10 = −3/2 y₃ = 7 − (1/2)·10 − (6/11)·(−3/2) = 31/11 x₃ = (31/11)/(31/11) = 1 x₂ = (−3/2 − (5/4)·1)/(11/4) = −1 x₁ = (10 − 1·(−1) − 3·1)/4 = 2

Six short steps once the factors are known, against a fresh elimination for each new b. That is why the factoring, Θ(n³), is done once and reused: each further right-hand side costs only Θ(n²).

Common mistakes

1
Expecting elimination to work without row exchangesLU decomposition without pivoting divides by the top-left entry, and a nonsingular matrix can have 0 there. Preset zero, [[0, 2], [3, 1]], is invertible with determinant −6 and cannot be factored as LU at all; one row exchange fixes it. Only some classes, such as symmetric positive-definite matrices, are safe without pivoting.
2
Solving by computing the inversex = A⁻¹b is correct algebra and poor computing. The authors note it is numerically unstable in practice, and it is no faster: the inverse itself costs an LUP decomposition plus n solves. Keep the factors and solve two triangular systems for each b.
3
Forgetting to permute bThe factors describe PA, not A, so the right-hand side must be permuted the same way before forward substitution. Solving Ly = b with the original order gives the solution of a different system, and nothing in the arithmetic signals the mistake until x is substituted back into Ax = b.
