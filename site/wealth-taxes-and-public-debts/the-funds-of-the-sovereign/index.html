The Funds of the Sovereign

A sovereign can live on stock or on land of his own, and Smith says neither will do for a great state: the crown lands cost the country more than any other equal revenue.

The passage first, then the Part line by line

Book V, Chapter 2, Part I. The funds, or sources, of revenue which may peculiarly belong to the sovereign or commonwealth, Smith says in his first sentence, “must consist, either in stock, or in land”.

Before the money comes in, a word about the money itself. Smith writes pounds, shillings and pence, and so does this course. There are twelve pence to a shilling and twenty shillings to a pound, so that “four shillings in the pound” is a fifth. Where a lesson says a million it means pounds sterling. Chapter 2 opened by saying that the money for the state's wants comes either from a fund peculiar to the sovereign or from the revenue of the people; this Part takes the first, and the rest of the chapter takes the second.

Stock first. A sovereign may use it himself, which earns profit, or lend it, which earns interest, and Smith goes through the people who have tried. The Tartar chief lived on the increase of his own herds, and that was the earliest and rudest government. The republic of Hamburg is said to draw something from a public wine-cellar and an apothecary's shop, on which Smith remarks that a state cannot be very great whose sovereign has leisure to be a wine-merchant. Venice and Amsterdam draw a profit from a public bank, and Smith reckons what Britain might draw from the Bank of England.

His figures are worth running. The bank's capital is £10,780,000 and its dividend 5½ per cent, so the annual profit after management comes to £592,900. The government, it is pretended, could borrow the capital at 3 per cent, which is £323,400 a year in interest, and so keep £269,500 clear. Smith does not dispute the sum. His doubt is whether a government like that of England, which “has never been famous for good economy”, could be trusted with the business. The one state enterprise he praises outright is the post-office, and for reasons the chapter gives: the capital to be advanced is not very considerable, there is no mystery in the business, and the returns are certain and immediate.

Everywhere else his verdict is the one he gives in a single line about trading princes: “No two characters seem more inconsistent than those of trader and sovereign.” A prince's agents regard their master's wealth as inexhaustible and are careless what they pay and what they charge. The English East India Company is his latest case, and he turns it both ways: while they were traders only they managed their trade, and since they became sovereigns they have been obliged to beg assistance of government to avoid immediate bankruptcy.

Land fares better, but not much. It was the great fund of the ancient republics and kings, when war cost the state almost nothing and a king's household was his main expense. In modern Europe war is the main expense. Smith puts a figure on the mismatch: the ordinary revenue of Great Britain is above ten millions a year, while the rent of all the land in the country, managed as it would be if it all belonged to one owner, would scarce reach that, and the land tax at four shillings in the pound falls short of two millions.

Then comes the claim that has made this Part famous. Crown lands, he says, are generally forest, “a mere waste and loss of country, in respect both of produce and population”. In countries where improved land “commonly sell at thirty years purchase”, such estates “might well be expected to sell at forty, fifty, or sixty years purchase”. Run the improved case first. Take an estate that rents for £1,000 a year; at thirty years' purchase, a rate of 10/3 per cent, it sells for £30,000. That is the improved preset, which also prices the same rent at forty years' purchase, £40,000, to show what ten more years' purchase does to a price. The crown's own estate is a different sum, because the crown draws little from it: in the crown-sale preset it yields £250 a year, and at forty years' purchase (5/2 per cent) the price is £10,000, which is forty years of what the crown draws, and at sixty (5/3 per cent) £15,000.

Now what would the crown do with the price? Smith says: pay off debts, and “deliver from mortgage a much greater revenue” than the lands ever afforded. £10,000 of debt at 3 per cent, the rate at which Smith's Bank of England example supposes the government could borrow, costs £300 a year in interest; so selling an estate that yielded £250 would leave the crown £50 better off, and every extra year's purchase widens the gap. In the improved preset the same land, in private hands at four times the crown's rent, is worth £30,000, which is 120 years of what it yielded the crown, and that is the arithmetic of Smith's guess that the crown lands “do not at present afford the fourth part of the rent which could probably be drawn from them”. The fourth part is his estimate, not a count, and the lab takes it as stated.

The second half of his argument goes from the sovereign's rent to the people's produce, and it is the one to remember. The revenue the great body of the people draws from land is in proportion, he says, not to the rent, but to the produce, and rent is scarce anywhere more than a third of it. So land that pays ten millions in rent and would pay twenty under better cultivation has a produce of thirty millions and would have sixty. The proprietors lose ten millions by the poorer cultivation, and the produce of the country falls by thirty, the seed aside. Whatever keeps down the produce keeps down the revenue of the people “still more than it does that of the proprietors of land”, and so the crown's rent, though it appears “to cost nothing to individuals, in reality costs more to the society than perhaps any other equal revenue which the crown enjoys”.

His conclusion is brisk: sell the crown lands by public auction, replace the revenue from some other source, and keep only the parks and gardens, which are everywhere reckoned causes of expense and not sources of revenue. The state's own stock and own lands are, in his phrase, “improper and insufficient funds for defraying the necessary expense of any great and civilized state”, and so most of the expense must come from taxes. A century later Henry George argued the opposite about land: that the rent of land belongs to the community and ought to be the state's main revenue. George does not dispute that rent is a surplus, and Smith himself says in the next Part that ground-rents are a proper subject of peculiar taxation. What they differ on is who should own the land and collect the rent, and the labs here cannot say which gets the better husbandry.

That is where the book turns to taxes. The next lesson, The Four Maxims of Taxation, takes up the four rules Smith lays down before he examines a single tax.

Pricing a rent and asking what the sale would redeem

Four steps, the same for any estate Smith wants sold.

  1. Fix the yearly rent. Take what the owner receives a year. For the crown's own estate that is what the crown draws, not what a private owner could draw.
  2. Turn the rate into years' purchase. Years' purchase is 100 divided by the rate of interest in per cent. Thirty years' purchase is 10/3 per cent, forty is 5/2, sixty is 5/3.
  3. Multiply for the price. Price is the yearly rent times the years' purchase. A lower rate of interest gives a higher price for the same rent.
  4. Set the price against what it could redeem. Multiply the price by the interest rate on the debt it would pay off. If that interest is more than the rent the lands yielded, the sale frees revenue, which is Smith's argument; and then ask what the produce would do in new hands.

A crown estate sold to pay a debt

An estate yields the crown £250 a year. Smith expects it to sell at forty years' purchase, and suppose the debt it pays off bears 3 per cent, the rate in Smith's Bank of England example. What does the sale free, and what does the Bank of England sum look like beside it?

price: 250 · 40 = £10,000 implied rate: 100 / 40 = 5/2 per cent interest on £10,000 of debt at 3%: £300 a year against the yield of the estate: 300 − 250 = £50 more Bank of England interest at 3%: 3/100 · 10,780,000 = £323,400 profit kept: 592,900 − 323,400 = £269,500

The estate's price redeems £300 of interest against the £250 it yielded. The bank sum is the other half of the Part: £269,500 a year is what the state was told it could keep, and what Smith doubts it would.

Common mistakes

1
Reading the Part as a ban on every state businessSmith excepts the post-office, notes that Venice and Amsterdam have drawn a profit from public banks and that Berne has lent at interest. His rule is narrower: stock and credit are too unstable, and a trading sovereign is a bad trader. The Bank of England is a case he leaves doubtful rather than condemned.
2
Taking a higher years' purchase for a higher returnMore years' purchase means a smaller return, because the price is larger for the same rent. Sixty years' purchase is 5/3 per cent, forty is 5/2. A buyer who pays sixty years is content with less per pound laid out, which is why a low-rented crown estate can sell for so many.
3
Counting the crown's rent as free revenueIt “appears to cost nothing to individuals”, but Smith's measure is the produce of the land and not the rent, and rent is a third of it at most. In the example the proprietors' loss is ten million and the produce lost is thirty, so the people lose three times what the proprietors do.

Before you go

Say each idea in your own words, then open it to check.

Stock and its profit

A sovereign who owns stock gets profit if he employs it himself and interest if he lends it. Smith's examples are small and pointed: a Tartar chief's herds, the wine-cellar of Hamburg, the Bank of England, the post-office, Berne's loans to foreign states. He finds that only the post-office has been well managed by every sort of government, and that stock and credit are too “unstable and perishable” to carry a great state.

Land as the steadier fund

Land is “a fund of more stable and permanent nature”, and the rent of public lands supported the Greek and Italian republics and the old monarchies of Europe, when every citizen served at his own cost and a king's chief expense was his household. Smith's point is that those conditions have gone: war and its preparation are now the greater part of the expense, and a rent cannot meet it.

Years' purchase

A rent is sold as a number of years of itself. If land sells at thirty years' purchase, the buyer pays thirty years' rent, which is the same as saying that the going return on land is 100/30 per cent, so a lower return means more years of purchase. Smith expects the crown's low-rented, unimproved estates to fetch forty, fifty or sixty.

Summary

Chapter 2 asks where the money for government comes from, and Part I takes the first answer: from funds that belong to the sovereign himself, either stock or land. Smith finds both wanting. Work out what a crown estate fetches at forty years' purchase against thirty for improved land, what the sale would redeem, and why he says the people lose three times what the proprietors do.
