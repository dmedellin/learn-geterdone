Upstream Prerequisites

Chapter 3 argues that much of a project's success is decided before construction begins, and that the goal of preparing is to reduce risk. It backs the argument with a table of what a defect costs to fix by the phase it was made in and the phase it was found in, and qualifies it by the kind of software and by iterative and sequential approaches. Price a set of defects by the table, against the cost had each been found where it was made.

Chapter 3 in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Chapter 3 is the longest chapter in Part I, and most of it is a way of telling whether the work before construction has been done. Three ideas hold it together.

1

Prerequisites

The work construction depends on: a problem definition, requirements and an architecture. The chapter does not teach how to produce them; it says how to tell whether they are good enough to build on, and what to do when they are not.

2

The cost of a late fix

The book's Table 3-1 gives the relative cost of fixing a defect by the phase it was introduced in and the phase it was found in. Found in the phase it was made, a defect costs 1; a requirements defect found after release costs 10 to 100. The figures are averages from other people's projects.

3

Iterative and sequential

A sequential project finishes each activity before the next; an iterative one interleaves requirements, architecture, construction and testing. The chapter says iteration lowers the cost of thin upstream work and spreads it out, but does not remove it, and that the right balance depends on the kind of software.

The material

The key point first, then the chapter section by section

The quotation is the chapter's first key point. The lesson follows the six sections in order and then prices a set of defects by the chapter's table.

Chapter 3, p. 59. “The overarching goal of preparing for construction is risk reduction. Be sure your preparation activities are reducing risks, not increasing them.” It is the first of the chapter's key points, and the sentence to hold on to through nearly forty pages of argument for doing work before construction. Read Chapter 3, pages 23 to 60, in your own copy first; the lesson goes back through it section by section.

You have probably fixed a defect that was not a coding mistake at all. The routine did exactly what the specification said, the tests passed, and the specification was wrong; by the time a customer noticed, the wrong rule was in the schema, three screens and a report. Chapter 3 is about that kind of defect, and its carpenter's title, measure twice and cut once, is its argument in four words: much of a project's success or failure is settled before construction begins, and on a poor foundation the best construction can do is limit the damage. Section 3.1 (p. 24) puts it as a question of where a project puts its attention to quality. At the end, that means system testing; in the middle, construction practice; at the beginning, planning, requirements and design. Testing cannot find that you built the wrong product, McConnell says: start from the design of a Pontiac Aztek, test it as much as you like, and it will not turn into a Rolls-Royce.

The section takes on the claim that upstream work, architecture, design and project planning, is not useful on modern projects. McConnell's reply is that the claim is not well supported by data, that its holders tend to show prerequisites done badly, and that the purpose of preparation is to reduce risk: clear the major risks early, and the commonest risks in software are poor requirements and poor planning. He gives three reasons projects skip preparation anyway. The people assigned to it may lack the skills; programmers want to start coding; and managers want to see code, a condition the book names the WISCA syndrome, “Why Isn’t Sam Coding Anything?”

Then comes what the chapter calls its utterly compelling and foolproof argument, in three appeals. The appeal to logic is to know what you are building before you build it. The appeal to analogy is a food chain: the architect consumes the requirements, the designer the architecture and the coder the design, so contaminated requirements contaminate everything downstream. The appeal to data is the one the lab computes. Find a defect as close as possible to the time it was introduced, the principle runs, because it does more damage the longer it stays, and a defect made upstream tends to have broader effects. Table 3-1 (p. 29) gives the relative cost of a fix by the phase a defect was introduced in and the phase it was found in. Its figures are the book's synthesis of other people's data, after Fagan 1976, Humphrey, Snyder and Willis 1991, Boehm and Turner 2004 and others: averages over projects you did not work on. A requirements defect costs 1 to fix in requirements, 3 in architecture, 5 to 10 in construction, 10 in system test and 10 to 100 after release, and the book's own example is an architecture defect that costs $1,000 to fix while the architecture is being written and $15,000 in system test. On page 39 it adds that on smaller projects, with lower administrative costs, the post-release multiplier is closer to 5 to 10 than to 100.

Section 3.2 (p. 31) is the chapter's qualification of itself, and the part most easily skipped. Projects differ, and so does the balance between preparation and construction. The book sorts software into business systems, mission-critical systems and embedded life-critical systems, and its table of typical good practices runs from informal, highly iterative work for the first to formal, more sequential work for the last, where stable requirements are part of what makes very high reliability possible. An iterative approach interleaves planning, requirements and architecture with construction and testing; a sequential one finishes each before starting the next. Some writers, McConnell says, hold that iterative projects hardly need prerequisites, and he calls that view misinformed. Iteration lowers the cost of thin upstream work, because defects are found nearer the time they were made, and it spreads the cost over the project in small instalments instead of one bill at the end; it does not remove it. In his illustrative tables the iterative project that attends to prerequisites is the cheapest of the four, and an iterative project that skips them can cost more than a sequential one that does not.

The section ends on a choice, not a rule. A more sequential approach suits, among other things, stable requirements, a well-understood design and a high cost of later change; a more iterative one suits the opposite, and the rest of the book's criteria are in your copy. Most projects are neither wholly one nor the other, the book says, and most find value in settling at least the most critical requirements and architecture early. Given what software is like, McConnell judges iterative approaches the more useful far more often. A project can go wrong in either direction: by doing too little upstream and absorbing a stream of destabilising changes, or by doing too much and holding to plans that later discoveries have overturned.

Sections 3.3 to 3.5 (pp. 36–55) take the prerequisites one at a time, and each says how to recognise whether one has been done, not how to do it. A problem definition states the problem without reference to a solution and in the user's language: a company that cannot keep up with its orders has a problem, while a company that needs its data-entry system optimised has already chosen a solution. Requirements say in detail what the system is to do; written down, they let the user rather than the programmer decide what it does, and they settle arguments. But stable requirements, McConnell says, are a myth: customers understand what they need better as the work goes on, and holding to the requirements as first written amounts to refusing to respond to them. His advice for changes during construction is a list, which begins with checking the requirements and backing up if they are not good enough, making sure everyone knows what a change costs, and setting up a change-control procedure; the rest is in your copy.

Architecture, the high-level design that holds the detailed design together, decides the system's conceptual integrity, and Section 3.5's long account of what a good one covers, from program organisation, major classes and data design to error processing and buy-or-build decisions, ends in a checklist. Architectural errors, the book says, cost about as much to fix as requirements errors, which is more than coding errors cost. Section 3.6 (p. 55) then puts a figure on the whole: a well-run project spends about 10 to 20 percent of its effort and about 20 to 30 percent of its schedule on requirements, architecture and up-front planning, after McConnell 1998 and Kruchten 2000, figures that leave out detailed design because that is part of construction. Where requirements are unstable, the section advises treating requirements work as a project of its own and estimating the rest when it is done.

Table 3-1 is arithmetic waiting to be done, and the lab does it. You give it defects as records: the phase each was introduced in, the phase it was found in, and how many, with the phases written req, arch, cons, test and post for requirements, architecture, construction, system test and after release. It multiplies each count by the table's cell, adds the low ends and the high ends, and sets the total beside what the same defects would have cost had each been found in the phase it was made, where every cell is 1. On the found-late preset, 10 requirements defects found in system test cost 10 × 10, that is 100; 5 architecture defects found after release cost 5 × 25 to 5 × 100, that is 125 to 500; and 20 construction defects found in system test cost 20 × 10, that is 200. The cost tile reads 425–800, in units of the cost of a fix in the phase of origin; the early-cost tile reads 35, one unit for each defect; and the ratio tile divides the two, 85/7 ≈ 12.1× to 160/7 ≈ 22.9×.

found-early is the same 35 defects, each caught where it was made: 35, 35 and a ratio of 1×. requirements-late is ten requirements defects found after release: 100–1,000 against 10, a ratio of 10× to 100×, the widest cell in the table. Every figure here is the lab's arithmetic on the table's averages, and the range is the table's own spread carried through. Your project's multipliers may be smaller or larger; page 39's remark about small projects says they are often smaller after release. What the lab cannot do is tell you which phase a defect was introduced in. That is a judgement made by reading the defect, and it is the hard part of using the table at all.

Exercise

  • The specification says refunds are rounded half up; the customer's contracts require rounding half to even. Found by an auditor a year after release.
  • Tax is computed both in the order screen and in the invoice service, and the two disagree on a boundary date. Found by a customer.
  • A loop that totals a basket skips its last line. Found by a customer.

Three defects in a released system. For each, decide the phase it was introduced in by Sections 3.3 to 3.5: a rule the specification got wrong is a requirements defect, a responsibility placed in two modules is an architecture defect, and a mistake inside a routine is a construction defect. Then price the three by Table 3-1 as this lesson gives it, against the 3 units they would have cost found where each was made, and state the ratio. When you have an answer, enter your three records in the lab to check the arithmetic; it cannot check the placing.

The chapter is about what should be in place before construction. The next lesson, “Key Construction Decisions”, reads Chapter 4 on what the programmers themselves decide at its start: the language, the conventions and the practices.

Method

Pricing a set of defects by the table

Five steps, the last of them on defects of your own.

1

Place each defect

For each defect, write the phase it was introduced in and the phase it was found in: requirements, architecture, construction, system test or after release. Group defects with the same pair and count them.

2

Look up each cell

Find each pair's cell in Table 3-1. Some cells are a single figure and some a range; keep the range.

3

Multiply and add

Multiply each count by its cell and add, the low ends together and the high ends together. The result is in units of the cost of a fix in the phase of origin.

4

Divide by the early cost

The early cost is the number of defects, since each would have cost 1. Divide the low and high totals by it for the ratio, and remember that the ratio is arithmetic on other people's averages.

5

Price your own last five

Take the last five defects in your own tracker. For each, write the phase it was introduced in and the phase it was found in, enter them in the lab as introduced, detected, count records, note the cost range and the ratio, and write one sentence on the defect whose phase of origin you were least sure of.

Common mistakes

1
Reading the cost table as a rule to finish requirements firstThe table is easily read as saying every project should finish its requirements before any coding starts. Section 3.2 argues the opposite for most kinds of project. The table is an argument for finding defects close to where they were made, and an iterative project does that by doing a little of each activity at a time; McConnell says iterative approaches are the more useful far more often, and asks that at least the most critical requirements and architecture be settled early. On the lesson's illustration, catching the same 35 defects where each was made costs 35 units against 425 to 800, and that can happen in any order of work that finds defects early.
2
Leaving quality to testing at the endSection 3.1's point is that testing is one part of quality assurance and not the most influential: testing cannot find that the product is the wrong one, or the right one built the wrong way. Those flaws have to be worked out before construction. By Table 3-1, a requirements defect left for system test costs 10 times what it would have in requirements.
3
Writing a problem definition as a solutionA problem definition, Section 3.3 says, states the problem in the user's language and without reference to a solution. A statement that the company needs its data-entry system optimised has already chosen the answer, and may have chosen the wrong one: the book's example is an annual profit report that needed a calculator and four quarterly figures, not a program.

Note: The lab multiplies the table's cells; it cannot tell you whether your project's costs rise the way the table's do, and the table is an average over other people's projects, many of them large, from 1976 to 2004. This lesson is the course's one place for the later view. The agile movement, which was growing as the book was written and which Kent Beck's Extreme Programming Explained (2000), listed in the chapter's further reading, helped to start, holds that much of what Chapter 3 settles upstream is better discovered by building and showing working software, and that practices which make change cheap, such as small releases, automated tests and continual refactoring, flatten the rise the table records. The book's position, at its strength, is Section 3.2's: iterate, usually, and still settle the most critical requirements and architecture early, because iteration lowers the cost of a late defect without removing it. Which describes your project is a question about your own defects, and neither the book nor this lab settles it.
