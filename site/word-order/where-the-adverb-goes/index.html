Where the Adverb Goes

Learners often put always and never where their own language would: at the end, or before the subject. English has a favourite home for them. A word that tells how often, called a frequency adverb, sits in the middle of the verb phrase: before the main verb, after the first helping verb — have, be and will from Tense Tables, and their kind, can, could, do, did, must — or after be on its own. On 120 lines from Pride and Prejudice, printed with this lesson, that held in 85, which is 70.8%. On the whole novel, in a run this page cannot repeat, it held for 81.8% of 577 cases, and the misses fall into five named groups, the largest of which is not a failure at all.

The three middle places, and the score

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas carry this lesson.

1

The middle is the favourite, not the only place

English lets a frequency word sit at the start of a sentence or at the end, and some of them do so often. But the middle is the place most of them choose. The rule here is only about the middle, and it names exactly three spots. That is a rule you can apply to a sentence you have never seen, and a count can tell you how often it holds. The printed list also carries still, already and soon, which tell when rather than how often; they take the same middle places, and the lab scores them with the rest.

2

The score depends on what you count as a case

A word such as still or already is not always a frequency word. Still can mean quiet, and still better is about degree, not time. How soon and as soon as are phrases of their own. Those uses land in the misses without being a mistake of place. So 70.8% on the lines, and 81.8% on the novel, are if anything a little low for the true frequency words alone. The menu above the table scores one word at a time, and always alone reaches 15 of 16.

3

The misses are named groups, and the largest is not a failure

In the whole-novel run, of the 105 cases outside the rule, 51 are verbs the tool did not know, 23 sit in a phrase such as never in my life, 10 are at the end, 7 are at the start, and 14 are other. The first two are not mistakes in place. Only the last three are cases of a different place, and they total 31. The lab on this page sorts its 35 misses two ways, a preposition follows or something else does, and prints every line so you can sort the rest by eye.

The material

The cases outside the middle

On the whole novel, 105 of 577 cases were not in a middle place. Here is what they were, and beside them the 35 misses on the lines printed with this lesson.

Ask a learner to say I always drink tea and you may hear I drink always tea, or Always I drink tea. Both are understood. Neither is what a speaker would choose. English has a favourite place for words that tell how often, and this lesson counts how favourite it is.

The rule

Take the words always, usually, often, sometimes, never, ever and their kind. In the middle of a sentence they have three places:

  1. Before the main verb: she always knew.
  2. After the first helping verb: she has always known.
  3. After be: she is always late.

A speaker of Spanish may find the first one close to home. A speaker of Japanese or Korean, where the verb comes last, may expect the word near the front of the whole clause. A speaker of Arabic or Mandarin has other habits again. The rule is the same for everyone, and so is the count.

The score

The lab holds 120 lines from Pride and Prejudice, each cut around one adverb, and prints every one. In 85 of them, 70.8%, the adverb sits in one of the three places. In the other 35 it does not. Word by word the picture differs: always is in the middle in 15 lines of 16 and never in 14, while sometimes manages 7 of 16, because it so often opens or closes a clause.

The same rule was once run over the whole novel, novel text only. It found 577 cases, and in 472 of them, 81.8%, the word sat in one of the three places. That figure is quoted: no page can carry the novel. The 120 lines are the part you can check.

What the 105 whole-novel misses were

  • 51 had a verb outside the list. The tool reads a list of verb forms. When the verb was not in it, the tool could not find the middle. These are not cases of place at all.
  • 23 were inside a phrase. Never in my life is a fixed unit. The rule is about single words.
  • 10 were at the end and 7 at the start. These are real cases of the word elsewhere. English allows this, and it often adds weight to the word.
  • 14 were other. This group includes uses of still and already that have nothing to do with how often. They were counted because the tool matches the spelling, and not the meaning.

So the count of true places other than the middle may be close to 17. We say that carefully: the 14 were not all read, and the 51 are unknown, because the tool could not see the verb. A fair statement is that the rule held in 81.8% of cases and that most of the rest are not failures of place.

What the 35 misses on this page are

The lab sorts them two ways. Six have a preposition straight after the adverb: never in my life, still to think, so often to Miss Watson’s. The other 29 it calls something else between, and they are the whole-novel groups in small. Five are a verb the list does not hold. Three are as soon as. Five put the adverb before an adjective rather than a verb: more than usually insolent, still more interesting, often absent. A few are at the end of the clause, and me never, or at the start of one, and sometimes the refusal is repeated. One is the single word Sometimes. Read the verdict column and you will find very few that a speaker would call wrong.

Why a novel, and what it costs

The lab also counts the same adverbs, and rarely too, in two modern public documents, printed in full under the table: the Supreme Court opinion Stanley v. City of Sanford, 606 U.S. 46 (2025), and the Census Bureau story “U.S. Population Aging as Nation Turns 250” of 9 April 2026. In 1,723 words they contain two, one often and one still, which is 1.2 per thousand words. The novel's 577 cases in about 122,400 words, both quoted, come to about 4.7 per thousand. A reader who learns from formal modern writing meets very few of these words, and the middle place is a rule of speech and story more than of reports.

The cost is age. This is 1810s English, and a few of the misses may be habits that have changed. We cannot tell which from this count alone.

Method

Putting a frequency word

To put the word in the right place, do this.

1

Find the verb phrase

That is the main verb and any helping verbs before it: knew, or has known, or is.

2

With be, put the word after it

She is always late. They were never ready. The verb be is the exception to the first place, and it is the one most learners miss.

3

With a helping verb, put the word after the first one

She has always known. They would never agree. Not before the helping verb, and not after the main verb.

4

With no helping verb, put the word before the main verb

She always knew. He often asked. If you want to stress the word, English can move it, and then the sentence sounds like a choice.

The largest group in the novel is a limit of the tool. The word list does not hold some of the verbs Austen used, so the tool cannot say where the verb is, and it counts the case as a miss. This says nothing about where the adverb sat. The same thing happens five times on this page: they always contrived, my letters sometimes convey, who still resides, I sometimes amuse myself and ladies sometimes condescend are in the right place, and the scan does not know the verb.

The second group, 23 cases in the novel and 6 on this page, is a frequency word inside a phrase, such as never in my life. The rule did not mean to cover these.

Only 17 cases in the novel, the end and the start, are plain examples of a frequency word in a different place. The 14 “other” cases include the senses of still and already that are not about how often. We did not read all 14, so we do not claim to know how many are real differences in place. Common mistakes

1
Putting the word at the endShe knew always is a common mistake. In English the end is open to some words, such as often or sometimes, but for always and never the middle is the normal place.
2
Putting the word between the verb and its objectShe knew always the answer breaks two rules. The verb and what it acts on stay next to each other in English, and the frequency word goes before them.
3
Forgetting that be works differentlyWith be the word comes after: she is always. With every other main verb it comes before. Learn it as one short list: after be, after the first helping verb, before any other.

✓

You should be able to state the three places a frequency word can sit in the middle of a sentence, read how often that held on the 120 lines printed with this lesson, and name the kinds of line that fell outside it.

Note: A rule that holds in seven lines of ten here, in eight of ten on the whole novel, and has a clear list of reasons for the rest is a good rule. What you cannot say is that seven sentences in ten sound natural, because the count is about where the word sits and not about how a sentence sounds.
