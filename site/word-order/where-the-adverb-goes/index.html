Where the Adverb Goes

Learners often put always and never where their own language would: at the end, or before the subject. English has a favourite home for them. A word that tells how often, called a frequency adverb, sits in the middle of the verb phrase: before the main verb, after the first helping verb, or after be. On Pride and Prejudice this held for 81.8% of 577 cases. The other 105 fall into five named groups, and one of them is not a failure at all.

The three middle places, and the score

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas carry this lesson.

1

The middle is the favourite, not the only place

English lets a frequency word sit at the start of a sentence or at the end, and some of them do so often. But the middle is the place most of them choose. The rule here is only about the middle, and it names exactly three spots. That is a rule you can apply to a sentence you have never seen, and a count can tell you how often it holds.

2

The score depends on what you count as a case

A word such as still or already is not always a frequency word. Still can mean quiet, and already can tell you about time, not about how often. Those uses land in the misses without being a mistake of place. So 81.8% is, if anything, a little low for the true frequency words alone. We cannot say by how much.

3

The misses are five groups, and the largest is not a failure

Of the 105 cases outside the rule, 51 are words the tool did not know, 23 sit in a phrase such as never in my life, 10 are at the end, 7 are at the start, and 14 are other. The first two are not mistakes in place. Only the last three are cases of a different place, and they total 31.

The material

The 105 cases outside the middle

Out of 577 cases, 105 were not in a middle place. Here is what they were.

Ask a learner to say I always drink tea and you may hear I drink always tea, or Always I drink tea. Both are understood. Neither is what a speaker would choose. English has a favourite place for words that tell how often, and this lesson counts how favourite it is.

The rule

Take the words always, usually, often, sometimes, never, ever and their kind. In the middle of a sentence they have three places:

  1. Before the main verb: she always knew.
  2. After the first helping verb: she has always known.
  3. After be: she is always late.

A speaker of Spanish may find the first one close to home. A speaker of Japanese or Korean, where the verb comes last, may expect the word near the front of the whole clause. A speaker of Arabic or Mandarin has other habits again. The rule is the same for everyone, and so is the count.

The score

On Pride and Prejudice, novel text only, the rule found 577 cases. In 472 of them, which is 81.8%, the word sat in one of the three places. In the other 105 it did not.

What the 105 were

  • 51 had a word outside the list. The tool reads a printed list of verb forms. When the verb was not in it, the tool could not find the middle. These are not cases of place at all.
  • 23 were inside a phrase. Never in my life is a fixed unit. The rule is about single words.
  • 10 were at the end and 7 at the start. These are real cases of the word elsewhere. English allows this, and it often adds weight to the word.
  • 14 were other. This group includes uses of still and already that have nothing to do with how often. They were counted because the tool matches the spelling, and not the meaning.

So the count of true places other than the middle may be close to 17. We say that carefully: the 14 were not all read, and the 51 are unknown, because the tool could not see the verb. A fair statement is that the rule held in 81.8% of cases and that most of the rest are not failures of place.

Why a novel, and what it costs

Two modern public documents, a US Supreme Court opinion and a Census Bureau story, were also measured. They carry about 4.4 times fewer frequency words per thousand than Austen does. A reader who learns from formal modern writing meets very few of these words, and the middle place is a rule of speech and story more than of reports.

The cost is age. This is 1810s English, and a few of the misses may be habits that have changed. We cannot tell which from this count alone.

Method

Putting a frequency word

To put the word in the right place, do this.

1

Find the verb phrase

That is the main verb and any helping verbs before it: knew, or has known, or is.

2

With be, put the word after it

She is always late. They were never ready. The word be is the exception to the first place, and it is the one most learners miss.

3

With a helping verb, put the word after the first one

She has always known. They would never agree. Not before the helping verb, and not after the main verb.

4

With no helping verb, put the word before the main verb

She always knew. He often asked. If you want to stress the word, English can move it, and then the sentence sounds like a choice.

Five groups of misses

The counts below are counts of cases, out of 105.

word not in the printed list 51 (the tool cannot tell the verb) inside a phrase: never in my life 23 at the end of the clause 10 at the start of the clause 7 other 14

The largest group is a limit of the tool. The word list does not hold some of the verbs Austen used, so the tool cannot say where the verb is, and it counts the case as a miss. This says nothing about where the adverb sat.

The second group, 23 cases, is a frequency word inside a phrase, such as never in my life. The rule did not mean to cover these.

Only 17 cases, the end and the start, are plain examples of a frequency word in a different place. The 14 “other” cases include the senses of still and already that are not about how often. We did not read all 14, so we do not claim to know how many are real differences in place.

Common mistakes

1
Putting the word at the endShe knew always is a common mistake. In English the end is open to some words, such as often or sometimes, but for always and never the middle is the normal place.
2
Putting the word between the verb and its objectShe knew always the answer breaks two rules. The verb and what it acts on stay next to each other in English, and the frequency word goes before them.
3
Forgetting that be works differentlyWith be the word comes after: she is always. With every other verb it comes before. Learn it as one short list: after be, after the first helping verb, before any other.

Check your understanding

✓
Finish when you can place always, never, often and the words like them in the right place, and say which cases the rule does not cover.

You should be able to state the three places a frequency word can sit in the middle of a sentence, say how often that held on a whole novel, and name each group of sentences that fell outside it.

Note: A rule that holds 81.8% of the time and has a clear list of reasons for the rest is a good rule. What you cannot say is that 82 sentences in 100 sound natural, because the count is about where the word sits and not about how a sentence sounds.
