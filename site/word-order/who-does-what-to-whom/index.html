Who Does What to Whom

In many languages an ending on a word says who does the action, so the words can move. Spanish, Russian, Japanese and Korean each do this to some degree, in their own way. English has lost most of those endings and keeps position instead. The one place it still marks the subject is the pronoun, and the pairs pronouns come in: I and me, he and him. That makes one rule easy to test on printed text: a subject pronoun is followed by its verb. On the passage printed with this lesson it held for 72 of 80 subject pronouns, 90.0%. On the whole novel, in a run this page cannot repeat, it held 88.7% of the time, and the rest is four named groups.

The rule, and its score

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas carry this lesson.

1

Some languages mark the role on the word. English marks it on the pronoun

If the subject of an action is shown by an ending, the subject can stand almost anywhere and the listener still knows who it is. English cannot do this with a noun: the dog bit the man and the man bit the dog use the same words, and only the order tells you which is which. But a pronoun does carry the role. He saw them cannot mean the reverse, because he is only ever the subject and them is only ever the one it happens to. The pronoun is what lets a printed text be scored with nothing marked by hand.

2

A strict rule with an honest score

The rule is: a subject pronoun — I, he, she, we, they — is followed directly by its verb. The word you and the word it look the same in both roles, so they cannot be scored this way and are left out. On the passage printed below the lab, the rule found 80 subject pronouns and 72 were followed by a verb, which is 90.0%. A run over the whole of Pride and Prejudice, quoted here because no page can carry the novel, found 5,990 and scored 88.7%. Neither number is the end of the story. The other tenth is where the lesson is.

3

The misses are named groups, not one failure

In the whole-novel run, a modifier stood between pronoun and verb in 63% of the misses, the next word was not in the tool's word list in 16%, a speech tag, as in said he, accounted for 15%, and 6% were not sorted. The passage on this page shows the same three kinds in small: of its eight misses, five have a modifier between, two have a next word the printed scan does not know, and one is a speech tag. Each of the three has a reason that does not break the rule, and the next section shows them.

The material

Where the rule seemed to fail

On the whole novel, 677 or so of the 5,990 pronouns were not followed by a verb. Here is what they were, in order of size, and beside them the eight misses the passage on this page shows.

Say the dog bit the man in Spanish, and the words can move: a small word, a, marks the man as the one bitten, and the verb agrees with the dog. Russian puts the job in an ending on the noun itself. Japanese and Korean use a small word after the noun. English, for the most part, does none of this. This lesson is about what it does instead.

What English kept

Old English had endings on nouns. Almost all of them are gone. What is left is a small set of pairs of pronouns. I and me. He and him. She and her. We and us. They and them. The first of each pair is the subject. The second is who or what it happens to. Her also does a second job, belonging, as in her book, which is why the lab leaves it out when it counts the object forms.

Because the pronoun carries the role, there is a rule that needs no reading of meaning. A subject pronoun is followed by its verb. A machine can look for he and read the next word. Nobody has to mark up the page first.

The score

The lab runs the rule over a 936-word passage from Pride and Prejudice, printed on this page. It finds 80 of I, he, she, we and they, and in 72 of them, 90.0%, the next word is a verb. The passage also holds 46 of you and it, and the scan leaves every one out, because those two words do not change with the job.

The same rule was once run over the whole novel, novel text only, 122,396 words. It found 5,990 of the five pronouns, and in 88.7% the next word was a verb. No page can carry the novel, so that figure is quoted here, not counted in your browser; the passage is the part you can check.

A number like that is easy to read wrongly in two ways. The first is to call the other tenth a failure rate. The second is to call it noise. It is neither. It is a few things, and each can be named.

The four groups

  • A modifier in between, 63% of the misses in the whole-novel run, 5 of the 8 on this page. They both knew. We all agreed. I almost envy you. A small word sits between the pronoun and the verb, and the verb is still the next main word.
  • A word outside the list, 16%, and 2 of the 8 here. The tool only knows the words in its list. Austen wrote beg and inquired where the list has neither, and on this page I interrupt is scored a miss because the scan does not know interrupt, and so is I don’t, because the list holds no word with n’t on it. This is a limit of the tool, not of the sentence.
  • A speech tag, 15%, and 1 of the 8 here. “But it is,” returned she. “My dear sister,” said he. This is a fixed device of telling a story, and it only happens beside quoted speech. A check in the whole-novel run, quoted, found 88% of these cases sitting next to quoted speech. The lab counts it in the box called word order broken, because the verb does come before its pronoun; the lesson's claim is that it is a habit, not an error.
  • Other, 6%. We have not sorted these. The printed passage has none.

What was not found

The result worth knowing is the one that is missing. Among the cases we read, there was no sentence where a subject pronoun stood in a place English does not allow. Every apparent exception was one of the groups above. English order is strict, and the one device that bends it is a named habit of telling a story.

We say among the cases we read on purpose. A sample of each group was read and the speech tags were checked in full by a second method. The 16% word list gap and the 6% other were not read case by case. So the claim is that we found no mistake, which is a weaker claim than that there is none. On this page the claim is stronger, because the eight misses are all in the table and you can read each one.

Why a novel from the 1810s

The lab also counts the same pronouns in two modern public documents, printed in full under the passage: the opinion of the US Supreme Court in Stanley v. City of Sanford, 606 U.S. 46 (2025), and the Census Bureau story “U.S. Population Aging as Nation Turns 250” of 9 April 2026. Both are free to use. The passage has 85.5 subject pronouns for every thousand words, 80 in 936; the two documents have 13.9, 24 in 1,723. That is about 6.1 times fewer. For the object forms the gap is wider still: 18.2 against 1.7 per thousand, about 10.4 times fewer. Formal modern writing is mostly nouns, and the structures a speaker needs are thin on the page.

So the measurements use a novel, and that has a cost. It is 1810s English, and some of what looks like a rule may be the habit of that time. The speech tag is the clearest case. This lesson tells you where the age of the text limits a figure, and the figure that depends on it most is the 15%.

There is a lesson in the gap itself. If you learn English from formal reading only, you meet far fewer of the forms you need when you talk. Read stories and speech too.

Method

Finding the subject in a sentence

To read who does what, do this.

1

Look for a pronoun first

In careful English, I, he, she, we, they are always the subject, and me, him, us, them are never the subject. This needs no grammar words and no guessing. Her is the one to watch: it is the object form of she and also the word for belonging, as in her book.

2

If there is no pronoun, use the order

In a plain statement with nouns, the one before the verb is the subject and the one after is what it happens to. This is the rule your own language may not use, so say it to yourself slowly at first.

3

Allow a small word in between

They both knew and we all agreed put a word between pronoun and verb. The subject is still the pronoun, and the verb is still the next main word.

4

Check for quoted speech

If the sentence sits beside quoted speech, the subject may come after the verb: said he. This is a fixed habit of stories, not a new order.

It reads I don’t know as the pronoun I and the word don’t, a helping verb with n’t on it, and the printed list holds no word like that. The third is one device. Every case we read was a speech tag, and a second check, also quoted from the whole-novel run, found 88% of the cases beside quoted speech.

We cannot speak for the last 6%. We read a sample of it and of the other groups and found no case of a pronoun in the wrong place. We did not read every case, so the honest claim is that we found none, not that none exist. On the printed passage you can read every one of the eight.

Common mistakes

1
Treating the speech tag as a new word orderSaid he looks like a pattern you could copy. It is a habit of older stories, and only after quoted speech. In speech today you say he said. Do not use it to be correct.
2
Taking a modifier as a break in the ruleThey both knew and we all went are not exceptions. A word like both or all may sit between the pronoun and its verb. Nothing else usually does.
3
Moving the words the way your own language wouldIn a language with endings, him saw he can still be understood, so the habit is to move words for emphasis. In English the pronoun form saves you only if you also keep the order.
