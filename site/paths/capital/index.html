Capital, Read Along

Karl Marx’s Capital, read chapter by chapter, all three volumes — with the arithmetic Marx does on the page done in front of you: value and money, the working day, surplus value, wages, accumulation, turnover, reproduction, profit, prices of production, interest and rent. Fourteen courses and 119 lessons are available.

Overview

The order is Marx's, and it is the order of the argument. Volume I studies capital in production, under the simplifying assumption that everything sells at its value; Volume II studies capital in circulation, where time and balance enter; Volume III puts the two together and lets competition, credit and landed property break the simplifications one by one.

Commodities and money come first because every later category is built from them. Surplus value cannot be stated before value is, and capital cannot be distinguished from money before money is.

The historical Part Eight of Volume I comes after accumulation, not before, because Marx wants the reader to see the mechanism first and then ask where the first capital came from. Read in the book's order, the history answers a question the reader already has.

Volume III's profit, interest and rent come last because each is a share of surplus value under another name. Read without Volume I they look like three separate incomes from three separate sources, which is exactly the appearance the last course, Revenues and Their Sources, takes apart.

Courses

Each course assumes the ones before it and nothing else. From Money to Surplus Value uses the value of Commodities and Money; Relative Surplus Value uses the working day; Wages and Accumulation uses all of Volume I's arithmetic; the three Volume II courses add time and the balance of two departments; the Volume III courses re-read every one of those categories as profit, prices of production, interest and rent; Revenues and Their Sources uses all of it.

Commodities and Money

Read the hardest chapter of Capital slowly, with the arithmetic beside it. Compute a commodity's value from producers' hours, read the elementary form of value and watch it grow into money, count what a single equivalent saves, and compute the money a day's sales and a chain of debts need.

From Money to Surplus Value

Follow Moneybags from the market into the spinning mill. Compute what a mark-up adds to the sum of values, value a day's labour-power, do Marx's yarn arithmetic for six hours and for twelve, measure the surplus against the variable capital, recompute Senior's last hour, and see what a legal limit on the day does to the rate.

Relative Surplus Value

Follow capital from the crowded workshop to the factory with the arithmetic beside it. Compute relative and extra surplus value, what shared tools save, the proportions of a manufacture, a machine's two tests for adoption, the labour in a denser hour and the capital a machine fails to set free, then run Marx's cases for the working day and his two formulae for the rate.

Wages and Accumulation

Read the chapters on wages and accumulation with Marx's own sums beside them. Compute the price of an hour and of a piece, compare two countries' wages per unit of output, count the years in which a capitalist consumes his capital, run accumulation period by period, and watch employment stand still while capital triples.

9 lessonsIntermediate
So-Called Primitive Accumulation

Read the history at the end of Volume I with the mechanism of each chapter computed beside it. Time a capital saved out of a wage, turn a smallholder's day into a day with a surplus in it, price a claim on the state, and count the workers a colonist can hire when land is free. Where historians dispute Marx, each lesson says who and why.

8 lessonsIntermediate
The Circuits of Capital

Follow Marx's spinner and his £422 round the whole circuit, from money to yarn to more money. Compute the surplus value produced and the surplus value realised, restart production simply or on a larger scale, split the yarn into the parts that are and are not anyone's income, and price the weeks and the clerks that circulation costs.

6 lessonsIntermediate
The Turnover of Capital

Follow a capital round its circuit with a stopwatch. Count turnovers a year, compute what a machine passes on by wear, total the turnover of a capital with fixed and circulating parts, sort the same capital two ways, and compute the additional capital, the annual rate of surplus value and the money that realises it.

11 lessonsIntermediate
Reproduction of the Social Capital

Work through Marx's reproduction schemes with the arithmetic beside them. Sort a society's product into two departments, find the 6,000 that Smith's dogma loses, check the condition I(v + s) = IIc, price the gap between machines worn and machines renewed, and run Marx's scheme of accumulation for four years.

6 lessonsAdvanced
Cost Price and the Rate of Profit

Take Volume I's c, v and s and look at them as the owner of the business does. Compute cost price and profit, the rate of profit beside the rate of surplus value, the annual rate of a capital that turns over many times, and the rate after a saving in constant capital or a rise in the price of cotton, mostly on Marx's and Engels' own figures.

7 lessonsIntermediate
Average Profit and Its Fall

Read the two most disputed results in Capital with Marx's own tables beside them. Transform his five spheres into prices of production and check his totals, form a market value, reprice after a wage rise, run the law of the falling rate and its counteracting causes, and compute what over-accumulation and devaluation do to the rate.

8 lessonsAdvanced
Merchant's Capital and Interest

Follow surplus value out of the factory to the people who never produce it. Compute Marx's general rate with a merchant's capital in it, the mark-up a fast-turning merchant adds, a money-dealer's share, the interest on a borrowed £100 and what is left after a manager's salary, and Richard Price's penny at compound interest beside what a real product could pay.

9 lessonsAdvanced
Credit and Fictitious Capital

Settle a circle of bills with no cash, then break it. Price a bond at three rates of interest, watch a stock company turn profit into interest and its manager into a wage earner, release £600 from a business that shrinks, push notes out and see them come back, run Marx's figures for the Bank of England into a panic, and follow a peasant's debt to the usurer year by year.

12 lessonsAdvanced
Ground Rent

Do Marx's rent tables yourself, soil by soil and dose by dose. Price land from its rent, compute the waterfall's surplus profit, the rents on four soils and on one acre with four capitals, follow total rent as the price of corn falls and rises, and compute the absolute rent a low composition of capital allows.

11 lessonsAdvanced
Revenues and Their Sources

Divide one value product into wages, rent, interest and profit of enterprise, and move the shares without moving the whole. Find the part of Marx's 9,000 of annual product that is nobody's income, run his wage rise on 400c + 100v + 150s, split one product three ways under three relations of production, and count classes by source of revenue until the manuscript stops.

5 lessonsAdvanced

Recommended background

School arithmetic: fractions, percentages and a willingness to add up a column. Marx's examples are in shillings, pounds and hours, and every one of them is a sum or a ratio. The labs do the arithmetic; what is asked of you is to say what each number means.

No economics. The Subject introduces every category it uses in the chapter where Marx introduces it, and it introduces the classical economists Marx is arguing with — Smith, Ricardo, Say, Senior, Mill — at the moment he argues with them. If you have studied modern economics, expect to unlearn some vocabulary: “capital”, “profit” and “value” do not mean here what they mean in a textbook, and the first lessons say exactly how.

A copy of the book is welcome but not required. Every lesson quotes the passage it reads from the public-domain Kerr editions: Volume I in the Moore and Aveling translation as revised by Ernest Untermann, Volumes II and III in Untermann's translation. Chapter numbers follow the English edition of Volume I, which differs from the German from Chapter 4 on; the two places where that matters say so.

Patience with abstraction. Volume I assumes that commodities sell at their values; Volume III takes that assumption away. A reader who stops after Volume I has read the first half of an argument, and several lessons say so on purpose.

Educational course material. Every figure on this path is computed in your browser from what the lesson states: a value is total hours over total output, a rate of surplus value is a ratio of two sums, a scheme balances or it does not, a price of production is a cost price plus the average profit. Nothing is rounded except where a lesson says so. Quotations are from the public-domain Kerr editions, as published in the Online Library of Liberty. What the labs cannot do is tell you whether Marx's definitions are the right ones — and that is what the book is arguing about.
