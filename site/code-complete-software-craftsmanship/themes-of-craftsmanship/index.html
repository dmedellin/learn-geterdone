Themes of Craftsmanship

Chapter 34 gathers the book's themes: conquer complexity, pick your process, write for people first, program into your language, use conventions, work in the problem's terms, watch for warning signs, iterate, and keep religion out of software. Chapter 35 points to further reading. Put one routine through the decision-point and nesting counts and say which theme each count serves.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Chapter 34 names many themes. Three of them carry the lesson and the lab.

1

Conquer complexity

The book's first theme, argued in Chapter 5 as software's primary technical imperative: most good practices exist to reduce how much of a program a person must hold in mind at once.

2

Warning signs

Section 34.7's falling rocks: tricky code, a class with more errors than average, a routine with many decision points or deep nesting. Each is a reason to look skeptically, not proof of a fault.

3

Eclecticism

Section 34.9's alternative to treating a method as a religion: keep a toolbox of techniques, try new ones, rely on the dependable ones, and choose for each problem.

The material

The book's themes, then one routine measured

Read Chapter 34 in your own copy first, and look through Chapter 35's reading plan. The lesson sets out the themes, then puts one routine through two of the book's counts.

Chapter 34, Key Points, p. 853. “Dogmatic methodologies and high-quality software development don’t mix. Fill your intellectual toolbox with programming alternatives, and improve your skill at choosing the right tool for the job.” Chapter 34, Themes in Software Craftsmanship, runs from page 837 to page 853, and Chapter 35, Where to Find More Information, from page 855 to page 862. Read Chapter 34 in your own copy before going on.

Take a routine you wrote last month, one you would rather nobody reviewed. Chapter 34 names what such a routine has lost sight of. The book has been about details, classes, names, loops, layout, and this chapter picks the details up again to show what they serve. The first theme is the one Chapter 5 argued (§34.1, p. 837): managing complexity is the heart of software development. Nobody's brain spans the range of detail a program has, so the book's practices are tools for holding less of it at once: subsystems, class interfaces that let you ignore what is behind them, avoiding global data, deep inheritance and deep nesting, short routines, clear names, few parameters, conventions. Abstraction is the most powerful of them; Fred Brooks, as the book cites him, counted the move from machine language to higher-level languages the biggest single gain the field has made.

Pick your process (§34.2, p. 839): on a team, how people work together decides whether their abilities add up or cancel out, and quality has to be built in from the first step, because testing only tells you how the software is defective. Write programs for people first, computers second (§34.3, p. 841): readability improves comprehension, review, debugging and change, and the gains accumulate from many small choices. Program into your language, not in it (§34.4, p. 843): decide what you want to do, then find the way your language can do it, writing your own assertions or named constants where it lacks them.

Focus your attention with conventions (§34.5, p. 844): a convention answers an arbitrary question once, conveys information concisely, guards against a known hazard, and can make up for a weakness in the language, though large projects can go overboard with them and small ones underboard. Program in terms of the problem domain (§34.6, p. 845): top-level code should read as the problem being solved, with the files, arrays and loop indexes kept at a lower level of abstraction.

Watch for falling rocks (§34.7, p. 848) is about warning signs. Tricky code, a class with more errors than average, a project with a great deal of debugging: each suggests something to look at. Design metrics are warning signs of the same kind. The section names more than about 10 decision points in a routine and more than three levels of logical nesting, the figures of Table 19-2 after McCabe and of Section 19.4, and says plainly that none of them necessarily means a routine is badly designed, only that it should be looked at skeptically. McConnell borrows a phrase from the philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce for the feeling to heed: the irritation of doubt, the unease you struggle to pass out of into belief.

Iterate, repeatedly, again and again (§34.8, p. 850): requirements, design, estimates and tuning all improve with repeated attempts, and the trick is to iterate early, when it is cheap. The last theme (§34.9, p. 851) is that software and religion should be kept apart. Dogmatic adherence to one design method, one formatting style or one rule is always out of place, because software development is heuristic rather than deterministic. Mix methods: experiment with the new ones, rely on the dependable ones, and be ready to change your beliefs when an experiment says so. McConnell's image is a toolbox, and a contractor friend, Simple Simon, who uses vise-grip pliers for every job.

Chapter 35 is a guide to further reading. It sorts what a developer needs into core programming books, the larger technical and management contexts, and references that last about one project (p. 855), describes books on construction and on the topics beyond it, and sets out the reading plan McConnell's company, Construx, used, in three levels: introductory, practitioner and professional (pp. 860–862). The list is the book's, from 2004, and is not reproduced here; read it in your copy with your own judgement about what has been superseded since.

Now the code. The lab's complexity view counts decision points by the rule printed under the listing, McCabe's as Section 19.6 gives it: 1, plus one for each if, while, for, case, && and ||. The Decision points tile is that count, Breakdown shows its parts, and Band places it in the book's ranges: 0–5, 6–10 and 10+. Deepest nesting is the greatest brace depth below the routine's own braces, so a statement directly in the body is at depth 0; this is not the layout lesson's depth, which counted the routine's body as one level. First line at that depth says where it is reached. Here is the retry-delay preset, which decides how long to wait before retrying a failed request:

int retryDelayMillis(Request request, int attempt) {
    if (request == null || attempt < 1) {
        return 0;
    }
    int delay = 100;
    for (int i = 1; i < attempt; i++) {
        delay = delay * 2;
        if (delay > 10000) {
            delay = 10000;
        }
    }
    if (request.isIdempotent() && request.hasDeadline()) {
        delay = Math.min(delay, request.millisToDeadline());
    }
    return delay;
}

The lab counts 7 decision points: the 1, three ifs (lines 2, 8 and 12), one for (line 6), the || on line 2 and the && on line 12, printed as 1 + if 3 + for 1 + && 1 + || 1. That is in the band 6–10, where Table 19-2 says to start thinking about simplifying. The deepest nesting is 2, first reached on line 9, inside the if inside the loop. Neither figure is a verdict. What they point to is the loop of lines 6 to 11, which doubles a delay and caps it, and could be a routine of its own with a name, which would serve the complexity theme, and the bare 100 and 10000, which would read better as named constants, which would serve the theme of writing for people first.

double shippingCost(Order order) {
    double cost = 0.0;
    if (order != null) {
        if (order.weight() > 0) {
            if (order.isDomestic()) {
                if (order.weight() < 2 && !order.isExpress()) {
                    cost = 5.0;
                } else {
                    cost = 9.0 + order.weight();
                }
            } else {
                for (Item item : order.items()) {
                    if (item.isRestricted() || item.weight() > 30) {
                        return -1.0;
                    }
                }
                cost = 25.0 + 2 * order.weight();
            }
        }
    }
    return cost;
}

The nested-shipping preset is the shipping routine from the lesson “Nesting and Complexity”. The lab counts 9 decision points, 1 + if 5 + for 1 + && 1 + || 1, in the band 6–10, and a deepest nesting of 5, first reached on line 14. Five levels is past the three that Section 34.7 names as a warning sign. That lesson rewrote the routine with guard clauses; here the point is only that the two counts, set beside the chapter's warning signs, tell you which of the two routines to look at first.

Exercise

The exercise is your own code. Take a routine of about fifteen lines that you wrote, and without the lab count its decision points by the rule above and its deepest nesting. Then name two changes you would make and the theme of Chapter 34 each one serves. Paste the routine into the lab to check your counts. One quiz question applies the same counts to a changed retry-delay, so that you can check the method on a routine whose answer is fixed.

This is the last lesson of the read-along. The book's own last word is its reading plan; this course's is to go back to the first, “Laying the Foundation”, with a routine of your own and see how much of what it argued you can now measure.

How the lab reads Java
        
  • Comments run from // to the end of the line, or from /* to */; string and character literals are read as single tokens. The lab reads Java; it never runs it.
  • A line is blank, a comment line (it carries a comment, alone or after code) and/or a code line (it has code outside comments). A statement line is a code line that is not only braces and semicolons.
  • The routine is the first statement at brace depth 0 that ends in { and has the shape type name(parameters). Without one the listing is a fragment.
  • A variable is a name that begins with a lower-case letter or _, is not a keyword, is not followed by ( and does not follow a dot. Capitalised names are types or constants.
  • A declaration (a type, then a name and =, ;, , or :) sets the name when it has a value and only declares it otherwise; parameters are set on the header's line. name op expr with op one of = += -= *= /= %= reads the expression (and the name too, unless op is =) and then sets the name; x++ reads and then sets x. Setting an element or a field only reads the names in it. A for header is read as its three parts in order.
  • The lab reads the lines top to bottom and does not follow branches or loops.
Decision points: 1, plus one for each if, while, for, case, && and ||; else, default, do, ?:, try and catch add nothing. Depth is brace depth below the routine's own braces.

Method
Measuring a routine against the book's themes
Five steps, the last on a routine of your own.

      
1
Count the decision points
Start at 1 and add one for each if, while, for, case, && and ||. An else, a default and a do add nothing. An else if is an if and counts; the while of a do … while counts, the do does not; the ?: operator is not counted, which is the lab's choice and not the book's.
2
Find the deepest nesting
Count the braces open below the routine's own at each line, and note the greatest depth and where it is first reached.
3
Read the counts as warning signs
Set the count beside the bands, 0 to 5, 6 to 10 and 10+, and the depth beside the three levels Section 34.7 names. A figure past them is a reason to look, not a fault.
4
Name the theme each change serves
For each change the counts point you to, say which of Chapter 34's themes it serves: complexity, people first, conventions, the problem's terms.
5
Measure a routine of your own
Paste a routine of yours into the lab and note its decision points, band and deepest nesting. Write one sentence for each theme of Chapter 34 the routine touches, naming the line that touches it.

Common mistakes
1
Thinking craftsmanship is a set of rules to followAfter thirty-three chapters of advice it is natural to read the book as a rulebook. Its last theme argues against that reading (§34.9): dogmatic adherence to any method, style or rule is always out of place, because software development is heuristic, and the book's techniques are tools to choose among. Even its thresholds are offered as warning signs that call for a look, not as rules that settle a case (p. 848). The counts in this course are tools for judgement, which is the point of saying that a count is a reason to look.
2
Reading a count under the threshold as a passRetry-delay's 7 decision points and depth of 2 sit below the thresholds Section 34.7 names, and the counts still point at a loop that could be its own routine and two bare numbers that could be named. A count says where to look in both directions: a low one does not certify a routine, any more than a high one condemns it.
3
Thinking process is overhead that gets in the way of codeSection 34.2 states the view that code is what matters and process an abstraction, and calls it shortsighted (p. 841). Quality has to be built in from the first step, and how a team works decides whether its members' abilities add up.
