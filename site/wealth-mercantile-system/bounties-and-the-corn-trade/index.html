Bounties and the Corn Trade

A bounty on exported corn is a tax that the public pays twice, once to the treasury and once in the price at home, and Smith adds that it does not raise corn's real value.

The passage first, then the bounty, the corn trade and the laws

Book IV, Chapter 5. Smith begins with what a bounty is for. “We cannot give our workmen a monopoly in the foreign, as we have done in the home market. We cannot force foreigners to buy their goods, as we have done our own countrymen. The next best expedient, it has been thought, therefore, is to pay them for buying.” It is the system's way, he says, of putting money into all our pockets by the balance of trade.

His first argument is about what a bounty must be. “Those trades only require bounties, in which the merchant is obliged to sell his goods for a price which does not replace to him his capital, together with the ordinary profit, or in which he is obliged to sell them for less than it really cost him to send them to market.” A trade that paid would not need one. So a bounty always props up a trade that loses, and the effect of bounties, “like that of all the other expedients of the mercantile system, can only be to force the trade of a country into a channel much less advantageous than that in which it would naturally run of its own accord”.

The best defence of the corn bounty in his own day was in the Tracts upon the Corn Trade, whose author showed that since the bounty began, the value of the corn exported had exceeded that of the corn imported by far more than the bounties paid. That, on the system's principles, proved the trade beneficial. Smith's reply is that the author “does not consider that this extraordinary expense, or the bounty, is the smallest part of the expense which the exportation of corn really costs the society”. The farmer's capital must be replaced as well, with the ordinary profit, and the very reason a bounty was thought needed is that the price abroad did not do it.

Then the two taxes. “The corn bounty, it is to be observed, as well as every other bounty upon exportation, imposes two different taxes upon the people; first, the tax which they are obliged to contribute, in order to pay the bounty; and, secondly, the tax which arises from the advanced price of the commodity in the home market.” Smith gives a supposition, with figures: “Let us suppose that, taking one year with another, the bounty of 5s. upon the exportation of the quarter of wheat raises the price of that commodity in the home market only 6d. the bushel, or 4s. the quarter higher than it otherwise would have been in the actual state of the crop.” A quarter is eight bushels, so 6d. a bushel is 8 × 6 = 48 pence, which is 4s. He takes from the Tracts that the corn exported was not more than one part in thirty-one of the corn consumed at home. So for each quarter exported there were 31 consumed here, and 31 × 4 = 124 shillings of second tax for each 5 shillings of the first. In his words: “For every 5s. therefore, which they contribute to the payment of the first tax, they must contribute £6:4s. to the payment of the second.” 124 shillings is £6 4s.

The lab computes the two taxes. To keep the arithmetic in whole numbers it scales Smith's ratio: 1,000 quarters exported and 31,000 consumed at home, which is the one to thirty-one, with a bounty of 5s. and a rise of 4s. In the preset smith-corn-bounty, in shillings, the treasury pays 5 × 1,000 = 5,000, home buyers pay 4 × 31,000 = 124,000, and the two together are 5,000 + 124,000 = 129,000, or 129 shillings for each quarter exported. The preset a-smaller-rise lets the rise be 1s. and gives 1 × 31,000 = 31,000 and a total of 36,000. The preset no-rise lets nothing rise and leaves only the first tax of 5,000. The rise is Smith's supposition, and not a measurement he made, and the first preset says so.

Smith's next point is that this rise is nominal. Defenders of the bounty said that a better money price for corn would encourage its growing. He answers that it would, if it raised the real price, but nothing can: “The real effect of the bounty is not so much to raise the real value of corn, as to degrade the real value of silver.” The reason is that the money price of corn regulates the money price of labour, which must be enough to buy the labourer's corn, and through labour and the rude produce it regulates the price of nearly every home-made good, so the farmer who gets 4s. for his bushel instead of 3s. 6d. finds that 4s. buys no more of what he needs. “The nature of things has stamped upon corn a real value, which cannot be altered by merely altering its money price.” And “Woollen or linen cloth are not the regulating commodities by which the real value of all other commodities must be finally measured and determined; corn is.” A bounty on cloth does raise the real price of cloth, he says, which is the difference.

Smith then offers a picture, drawn from Spain and Portugal, who taxed or forbade the export of gold and silver. “When you dam up a stream of water, as soon as the dam is full, as much water must run over the dam-head as if there was no dam at all.” The metal is deeper behind the dam and so cheaper there, and everything priced in it dearer, and foreigners can undersell the Spaniards at home. The corn bounty, he says, operates exactly in the same way, lowering the value of silver at home and raising it a little abroad, to the advantage of the Dutch.

Who gains? “There is, perhaps, but one set of men in the whole commonwealth to whom the bounty either was or could be essentially serviceable. These were the corn merchants, the exporters and importers of corn.” It was in them, he says, that he had observed the greatest zeal for keeping the bounty. He adds that a bounty on production would operate more directly than one on exportation, and would impose only one tax, and says it has rarely been given because the system teaches that wealth comes from export. His comment on one bounty paid on tonnage is a good sample of his temper: the herring-buss bounty, paid by the ton of the ship and not by its catch, had led vessels, he was afraid, to fit out “for the sole purpose of catching, not the fish but the bounty”. In 1759, he says, at fifty shillings a ton the whole Scottish buss fishery brought in four barrels. The accounts are given at the end of Book IV and are not worked here.

Objections to him come from two sides. David Ricardo argued in 1815 that restrictions on importing corn raise rents and lower profits, so that the price of corn does change who gets what, and Smith's claim that corn's real value cannot be altered needs that distinction. Malthus defended the restriction in the same year, on the ground that a country which depends on foreign corn is exposed in a war and that agriculture and manufactures should be kept in balance. And Alexander Hamilton, in 1791, argued that pecuniary bounties are among the most effective means of encouraging a new manufacture, the case Smith's rule against bounties has to meet. Smith had died the year before, and the argument was carried on without him.

The Digression concerning the Corn Trade takes the rest of the chapter. Smith says that praise of the bounty law and of the system of laws joined to it is “altogether unmerited”, and that the corn trade is four trades, which may be carried on by one man but are in nature separate: the inland dealer, the importer for home consumption, the exporter, and the carrier who imports in order to export again. The first, he says, has an interest that is, even in the years of greatest scarcity, “exactly the same” as that of the people. By raising his price as high as the scarcity requires, he makes them thrifty, and if he raises it too high he is left with corn at the end of the season, and if too low he starves them before the next crop. “Without intending the interest of the people, he is necessarily led, by a regard to his own interest, to treat them, even in years of scarcity, pretty much in the same manner as the prudent master of a vessel is sometimes obliged to treat his crew.” It is the idea of Chapter 2, the second lesson of this course, put without the words “invisible hand”.

Smith's argument against fearing a combination of dealers is about numbers. Corn is the commodity of greatest annual consumption, divided among more owners than any other, scattered everywhere, so the dealers are more numerous than in any other trade, and a dealer who found himself with too much would lower his price and not hold it up for his rivals' benefit. He grants one case: if one great company could hold a whole crop it might destroy part of it to keep up the price, as the Dutch are said to do with the spices of the Moluccas, but he thinks that scarcely possible with corn. His summary of history is strong: “a dearth never has arisen from any combination among the inland dealers in corn, nor from any other cause but a real scarcity”, and a famine from nothing but government’s attempts to remedy a dearth by improper means. Of the fear of engrossing and forestalling, he says it “may be compared to the popular terrors and suspicions of witchcraft”.

Two later arguments press on this. E. P. Thompson argued in 1971 that the crowds who seized grain and fixed prices were acting on a coherent moral economy, with older rules of fair dealing which they expected magistrates to enforce, and that the political economy of Smith's day was a replacement of it, not a plain correction. Amartya Sen argued in 1981 that famines can occur when the food supply is not unusually short, because some groups lose the means to buy food, so that the supply in the market does not settle the matter. Smith's own example is the drought in Bengal “a few years ago”, turned into a famine, he thinks, by restraints on the rice trade, and Sen's chief case is a different Bengal, in 1943. Smith does not claim that markets cure scarcity, only that the dealers are not its cause and that restraints make it worse.

On the other three trades Smith is shorter. The importer for home consumption benefits the people at once and lowers the money price but not the real value of corn. The exporter does not supply the home market directly, though a free export lets the farmer grow a surplus. But “The interest of the inland dealer, however, it has already been shown, can never be opposite to that of the great body of the people. That of the merchant-exporter may, and in fact sometimes is.” The carrier, who imports in order to export, helps the home market too, since he will sell part at home to save the cost of reshipping. He gives two ratios from the Tracts: grain imported is one part in 570 of the grain consumed, and grain exported one part in thirty-one of the annual produce, from which he puts the importance of the inland trade at 570 to one against the import trade and thirty to one against the export trade. Then he disarms them: “I have no great faith in political arithmetic, and I mean not to warrant the exactness of either of these computations.”

Smith closes with candour. “The laws concerning corn may everywhere be compared to the laws concerning religion.” People care so much about their subsistence that government must yield to their prejudices. He credits the statute of the thirteenth year of George III with improving the old system in some respects and worsening it in others, and says of it what was said of the laws of Solon: “though not the best in itself, it is the best which the interest, prejudices, and temper of the times, would admit of”. The history of the corn statutes and the table of duties are in the book. The next lesson takes the third encouragement, treaties of commerce, and the treaty with Portugal that was thought a masterpiece.

Counting the two taxes

Four steps, the same for any bounty on any exported good.

  1. The first tax. Multiply the bounty on each unit by the units exported. This is what the treasury pays, and what the public contributes to it.
  2. The second tax. Multiply the rise in the home price of a unit by the units sold at home. Every home buyer pays it, since the price is one price.
  3. Add and divide. Add the two taxes, and divide by the units exported to see what each exported unit cost the public altogether.
  4. Ask whether the rise is real. A rise in the money price of corn is a rise in the real price only if it buys more labour. Smith says it does not for corn, and the lab takes the rise as given and cannot say.

Smith's corn bounty

Take Smith's supposition: a bounty of 5s. a quarter raises the home price by 4s., and one quarter is exported for 31 eaten at home. Scaled to 1,000 quarters exported and 31,000 eaten at home, in shillings.

first tax: 5 · 1,000 = 5,000 second tax: 4 · 31,000 = 124,000 together: 5,000 + 124,000 = 129,000 a quarter exported: 129,000 / 1,000 = 129 for each 5: 124,000 / 1,000 = 124 = £6 4s

The public pays 129 shillings for each quarter exported, and the second tax is 124/5 times the first. That is Smith's £6 4s against 5s. If the rise were 1s. instead, the total would be 36,000 and the second tax would still exceed the first, 31,000 > 5,000, because so much more corn is eaten than sold abroad.

Common mistakes

1
Taking the 4s. rise as a measurementIt is a supposition, in Smith's words “Let us suppose”, and the one-in-thirty-one ratio is from the Tracts, whose computations he says he does not warrant. The lab shows what the reasoning implies for those figures and not what the bounty did.
2
Treating the money price of corn as its real priceSmith's point is that a bounty can raise the first and not the second. A farmer who sells at 4s. where he sold at 3s. 6d. is not better off if 4s. now buys what 3s. 6d. did. Ricardo's later argument is that restrictions on importing corn do shift income to landlords, so the question of whose real income is affected is not closed by Smith's sentence.
3
Hearing in the Digression a claim that dealers are good menThe argument is about interest and numbers, not character. Smith says that a single company holding the whole crop might destroy part of it, and that the exporter's interest may be opposed to the people's. What he denies is that the inland dealers, being many and scattered, can combine to raise the price beyond what the scarcity requires.

Before you go

Say each idea in your own words, then open it to check.

A bounty pays foreigners to buy

A country cannot force foreigners to buy its goods as it forces its own people, so “The next best expedient, it has been thought, therefore, is to pay them for buying.” Smith says bounties ought to be given only to trades that cannot be carried on without them, and such a trade must sell for less than it cost, so it is a losing trade.

The two taxes

An export bounty imposes two taxes on the people. The first is what they contribute to pay the bounty. The second is the rise in the price of the same goods at home, caused by sending more abroad. For corn the second is the heavier by far, since everyone buys corn.

Nominal price and real price

A bounty raises the money price of corn at home but not its real value, which is the labour it will maintain. Since the money price of corn sets the money price of labour and of home goods, the effect is to lower the real value of silver in that country, and not to enrich the grower.

Summary

Chapter 5 takes the encouragement that pays foreigners to buy, and spends most of its length on the bounty on corn. Compute what a bounty of five shillings the quarter costs the treasury and home buyers on Smith's own supposition, then read the Digression on the Corn Trade for its four trades, its two ratios and its claim about famine.
