Hash Functions and Universal Hashing

Section 11.3 asks what makes a hash function good, gives two heuristics, the remainder on division by m and the fractional part of a product, and then removes the reliance on friendly keys by choosing the function at random from a family in which no pair of keys collides too often. Hash a few keys both ways by hand, then count in the lab, over every function in the family, how often one pair collides.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas. The first two are the heuristics most tables use; the third is the guarantee the section proves.

1

Division method

h(k) = k mod m. One division, so it is fast. Its quality depends on m: a power of 2 makes the hash the key's lowest bits and ignores the rest, and the authors suggest a prime not close to a power of 2.

2

Multiplication method

h(k) = ⌊m·(kA mod 1)⌋ for a constant A between 0 and 1: multiply, keep the fractional part, scale by m and round down. The value of m is not critical, so it is usually a power of 2, which lets a machine compute the hash with one multiplication and a shift.

3

Universal family

A finite set H of hash functions such that for every pair of distinct keys, at most |H|/m of the functions send them to the same slot. A function drawn from H at random makes the pair collide with probability at most 1/m, the chance two independent random slots would agree.

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the count

Read Section 11.3 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the two methods and the universal family in its own words, hashes a few keys both ways by hand, and then counts a pair's collisions over a whole family in the lab.

“Any fixed hash function is vulnerable to such terrible worst-case behavior.” The authors write it in Section 11.3.3, of an adversary who knows the function and picks n keys for one slot, and it is the turn the section takes: two heuristics that work well on ordinary keys, and then a method whose guarantee does not depend on the keys at all. This lesson reads Section 11.3.

A good hash function would satisfy simple uniform hashing, but the section begins by admitting that this can rarely be checked: the distribution of the keys is usually unknown, and the keys may not be independent. What can be done is to make the hash depend on the key in a way unlikely to line up with patterns in the data, such as programs that use identifiers differing in one letter. The functions all take natural numbers, so a string is first read as a number, its characters as the digits of a large radix.

Section 11.3.1's division method takes the remainder k mod m. Its weakness is the modulus. If m is 2ᵖ, the remainder is the lowest p bits of k and the higher bits are ignored, so keys that agree in their low bits all collide; the authors warn against 2ᵖ − 1 for strings too, since permuting the characters leaves the hash unchanged (Exercise 11.3-3). Their advice is a prime not near a power of 2, chosen from the number of slots the expected load calls for.

Section 11.3.2's multiplication method multiplies k by a constant A between 0 and 1, keeps the fractional part, multiplies by m and takes the floor. On a machine with w-bit words, A is taken as s/2ʷ for a w-bit integer s; the product k·s is two words, and the hash is the top p bits of the lower word when m is 2ᵖ. Any A works, some better than others, and the authors report Knuth's suggestion of (√5 − 1)/2 ≈ 0.618, the fractional part of the golden ratio.

Both methods are fixed functions, and Section 11.3.3, starred in the book, starts from the adversary in the quotation. Its answer is the one quicksort used in Chapter 7: randomize. At the start of execution, pick the function by chance from a family designed for the purpose, independently of the keys that will be stored. Then no particular set of keys is bad every time; a bad outcome needs an unlucky draw of the function, and its probability is the same for every set of keys of the same size.

Theorem · Theorem 11.3 and Corollary 11.4

If h is drawn from a universal family and n keys are chained in m slots, a key not in the table hashes to a list of expected length at most α, and a key in the table lies in a list of expected length at most 1 + α. So any sequence of n insertions, searches and deletions containing O(m) insertions takes Θ(n) expected time. The expectation is over the choice of h; nothing is assumed about the keys.

The proof is the indicator method of Chapter 5. For a stored key k, add an indicator for each other key l that lands in k's slot; each has expectation at most 1/m by the definition, and linearity of expectation turns the sum into at most (n − 1)/m other keys, or n/m for a key not stored. The corollary adds this up over the operations, with α kept O(1) by the limit on insertions.

Definition · The family of Section 11.3.3

Choose a prime p larger than every key. For a in 1 to p − 1 and b in 0 to p − 1, let h_ab(k) = ((ak + b) mod p) mod m. The family has p·(p − 1) functions, and Theorem 11.5 proves it universal.

The proof of Theorem 11.5 is a counting argument worth having by heart. Fix keys k ≠ l and write r and s for (ak + b) mod p and (al + b) mod p. Because p is prime and a is not 0, r ≠ s, and each pair (a, b) gives a different pair (r, s): the map is one-to-one from the p·(p − 1) choices of (a, b) onto the p·(p − 1) ordered pairs of distinct residues. So k and l collide for exactly as many functions as there are pairs r ≠ s with r ≡ s (mod m), and for each r at most (p − 1)/m values of s qualify. The count depends on p and m and not on k and l.

Now the lab, the one from the Algorithms Subject's lesson Universal Hashing, where the family is practised further. Choose p, m and two keys x and y; the lab enumerates every (a, b), draws one cell per function, red where the pair collides, and prints the number of functions, the colliding ones, the exact rate, the bound 1/m and whether the rate is within it. A last tile picks two keys that k mod m sends to the same slot, the adversary's move against one fixed function, which collide with probability 1. The worked example p11-m4, the pair 3 and 7, gives 20 of 110 functions, the rate 2/11 against 1/4; p7-m3, the pair 1 and 4, gives 10 of 42, or 5/21 against 1/3; p13-m5, the pair 2 and 9, gives 22 of 156, or 11/78 against 1/5.

Then move the keys and leave p and m alone. At p = 11 and m = 4 every pair you choose collides under 20 functions, which is the proof's count made visible: the residues 0 to 10 fall into classes of sizes 3, 3, 3 and 2 modulo 4, and the ordered pairs within a class number 3·2 + 3·2 + 3·2 + 2·1 = 20. The count is exact for this family. The bound 1/m is what the theorem promises for every universal family, and the expected chain lengths follow from it.

Exercise

Take p = 7 and m = 3, and the keys 2 and 5. Without listing the 42 functions, count by the residue-pair argument how many send the two keys to the same slot, and give the rate as a fraction beside 1/m. Then set the lab to p = 7, m = 3, x = 2 and y = 5 to check, and say why the count matches a preset whose keys are different.

Method

Hashing three ways by hand

Four steps on a few keys, then one in the lab.

1

Divide

Compute k mod m for each key. Then try m a power of 2 and see whether keys that share their low bits collide.

2

Multiply in a small word

Pick w and s ≈ 0.618·2ʷ. Multiply k by s, split the product into a high and a low w-bit word, and take the top p bits of the low word as the slot in 2ᵖ slots.

3

Draw one function from the family

Pick a prime p above every key, then a from 1 to p − 1 and b from 0 to p − 1. Compute ((ak + b) mod p) mod m for each key.

4

Count a pair's collisions exactly

Sort the residues 0 to p − 1 into classes modulo m and count the ordered pairs of distinct residues within a class. That is how many of the p·(p − 1) functions make any two keys collide.

5

Check a pair of your own

Choose p, m and two keys in the lab, predict the count of colliding functions by the class sizes, and read it off the tiles. Then change only the keys.

mod 16: 37, 53, 69, 85 → 5, 5, 5, 5 mod 11: 37, 53, 69, 85 → 4, 9, 3, 8 w = 8, s = 158, p = 3 (m = 8): 77·158 = 12166 = 47·256 + 134 → slot 4 78·158 = 12324 = 48·256 + 36 → slot 1 p = 5, m = 3: classes {0, 3} {1, 4} {2} ordered pairs: 2 + 2 + 0 = 4 of 20 rate 1/5, against the bound 1/3

The four keys differ by 16, so they share their low four bits and k mod 16 puts them in one slot; modulo 11 they spread. In the multiplication, 134 is 10000110 in binary and its top three bits are 100, slot 4; 36 is 00100100, slot 1, so neighbouring keys land far apart. In the family, the pair 1 and 4 collides under the functions with (a, b) equal to (1, 0), (1, 4), (4, 0) and (4, 4), four of twenty, and so would any other pair.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking universal hashing randomizes the keysThe keys are whatever the user or an adversary supplies. What is random is the function, chosen once when the table is created and independently of the keys. Theorem 11.3's expectations are over that choice, and the lab makes this concrete: the pair is fixed and the count runs over every function. The authors compare it to randomized quicksort, whose pivot rather than its input is random.
2
Using a power of 2 as the modulus of the division methodk mod 2ᵖ keeps the lowest p bits and discards the rest, so keys with a common low part collide, as 37, 53, 69 and 85 do modulo 16. The multiplication method is the one that suits a power of 2, because it mixes all the bits of k into the bits it keeps.
3
Expecting a function from a universal family to be good for every set of keysUniversality bounds a probability over the draw. In the worked example the pair 1 and 4 collides under 4 of the 20 functions, so one draw in five is unlucky for that pair. The guarantee is that bad draws are rare for every set of keys, not that any particular function is safe.
