Up and Down

The chapter on space begins with up and down, and with two experiments: Stratton's spectacles, which turned the visual world upside down for a week, and Wertheimer's mirror, in which a tilted room suddenly rights itself. Up and down in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The section asks what it means for something to look the right way up. Three ideas carry its answer.

1

Spatialized and spatializing space

Kant's alternative, as the chapter states it: either I live among things and take space vaguely as their setting, or I reflect and find space as the act of a subject who traces all its relations. The section looks for a third kind of spatiality, neither of things nor of a thinking subject.

2

The spatial level

The orientation in relation to which a spectacle is upright or tilted. It is not given by the image and not chosen by thought; it is set when the body's possible actions and the spectacle come into gear, and it can tilt to a new position as a whole.

3

Anchoring points

Wertheimer's term for the things in a spectacle, walls, doors, a person walking, that take it upon themselves to give the vertical when a new level forms.

The material

The passage first, then the section line by line

The passage is Wertheimer's mirror, as the chapter reports it. The lesson goes back to the chapter's first page, follows Stratton's experiment and the two interpretations the chapter rejects, returns to the mirror and the spatial level, and ends with the inverted face and the first level.

Space, p. 289. “If we so contrive it that a subject sees the room in which he is, only through a mirror which reflects it at an angle at 45° to the vertical, the subject at first sees the room ‘slantwise’. … After a few minutes a sudden change occurs: the walls, the man walking about the room, and the line in which the cardboard falls become vertical.” The sentences are on page 289 of Smith's translation. Smith prints no section headings in this chapter; the section on up and down runs from the chapter's first page, 283, to the end of the paragraph on giddiness and nausea on page 296. Read those pages in your own copy before going on.

The experiment is Max Wertheimer's, from his 1912 study of seen movement, the paper usually taken as the start of Gestalt psychology. In the mirror the man walking about the room seems to lean as he goes, and a piece of cardboard falling down the door-frame seems to fall obliquely; the whole effect is odd. Then, after a few minutes, everything is vertical again. The chapter values the experiment for one feature: the righting is sudden and needs no exploration by movement. The bearings are taken, it says, by a global act of the perceiving subject.

Now back to the chapter's first page. Kant drew a strict line between space as the form of outer experience and the things given in it. Space is not a container, since that relation holds only between things, nor a class to which things belong, since it comes before its parts: on Kant's view, as the chapter states it, space is the means by which the position of things becomes possible. So either I do not reflect, and live among things taking space vaguely as their setting, or I reflect and pass from spatialized to spatializing space, finding a single power to trace out all directions. The chapter's question is whether that alternative is forced, and its method is to look at an experience of space, up and down, in a case where it comes apart and forms again.

Definition · Spatialized and spatializing space

Space as the setting in which things already lie, with its regions of up and down, near and far; and space as the act of a subject who traces and sustains all spatial relations, homogeneous and the same in every direction.

The case is George Stratton's. Stratton, an American psychologist, wore spectacles that inverted the image on the retina, which the eye's lens normally inverts already. In a first short experiment, in 1896, the landscape looked unreal and upside down, and on the second day normal perception began to return while his body felt upside down. In a second, in 1897, lasting a week, things looked inverted at first; from the second day the landscape was no longer inverted but the body felt wrongly placed; from the third day to the seventh the body gradually righted itself, above all when he was active. Actions found their target from the fifth day. Sounds were placed correctly only when their source was also seen. When the spectacles came off, things looked not inverted but queer, and he reached with the wrong hand.

The first interpretation the chapter gives in another's voice: at least implicitly, a footnote says, Stratton's own. The visual world is given turned through 180°, like the pictures in a book someone has turned round, while the world of touch stays the right way; the two conflict until movement guided by sight builds stable associations between the old directions and the new, and the name top is transferred to where the head appears. “But this interpretation is unintelligible.” Head and feet in the image could give the field a direction only if they had one themselves, and “‘Inverted’ or ‘upright’, in themselves, obviously have no meaning.” Intellectualism fares no better. If up and down are relations to axes a mind chooses, each axis is placed only by another and the task of taking bearings is postponed for ever; and since a constituting mind sees from nowhere and every objective relation survives the spectacles, it cannot even grant that the world looked upside down.

Hence the spatial level. Before the experiment perception recognizes a certain level, in relation to which the mirrored room looks tilted; during it the spectacle induces another level, in relation to which the whole field is upright again. Certain objects act as anchoring points and draw the vertical to themselves. The level is not the body's own orientation: a subject who tilts his head holds a stick tilted when asked to hold it upright, but the vertical follows the head only when the field is empty, in the dark. What counts is the body as a system of possible actions. “My body is wherever there is something to be done.” In Wertheimer's mirror the subject at first does not inhabit the room; after a few minutes the reflected room calls up a subject who could live in it, and he feels he has the arms and legs he would need to walk and act there. The level is “a certain gearing of my body to the world”.

The lab computes a model the lesson builds to illustrate this, and no theory says it: perceived tilt is image tilt minus spatial level, period by period. The wertheimer preset has an image tilted 45° and a level of 0° that jumps to 45° at period 3; the series tile prints 45°, 45°, 0°, 0°, 0°. The period is illustrative; the suddenness is the book's. The predicted-tilt and level tiles show the period the slider is set to, 45° and 0° at period 1. The stratton preset has an image turned 180° and a level that moves half the remaining way each period, so the series is 180°, 90°, 45°, 45/2°, 45/4°; the halving is the lesson's illustration, not Stratton's figures. The none preset has nothing tilted, and the series is 0° throughout.

The two rules stand for the chapter's two readings. For Wertheimer it is the jump that matters: the level is re-anchored as a whole, not corrected point by point. For Stratton it is that the change was systematic and yet piecemeal and progressive. Association could not make it systematic, whole regions falling into line together; a change of coordinates by thought could not explain why hearing and the unseen body lagged behind. So I go from one system of positions to the other without the key to either, the chapter says, as someone with no knowledge of music sings in another key a tune he has heard. Notice that under the share rule the model never reaches 0°, and that the chapter's summary of Stratton gives no moment at which the world turned back over either: a landscape no longer inverted from the second day, a body righting itself over the five days after, hearing and the unseen parts of the body never fully caught up. The chapter takes his descriptions and sets aside his interpretation, and how far Stratton's own reports support its reading of them is something later readers still argue about.

The section ends by turning the question round. For a thinking subject a face the right way up and the same face upside down are indistinguishable; for the perceiving subject the inverted face is unrecognizable. Look for long enough at someone lying on a bed from the head of the bed, and the face becomes terrifying, eyelashes and eyebrows taking on a strange materiality. “To invert an object is to deprive it of its significance.” The French word sens, the chapter points out, means both sense and direction. And a level always presupposes an earlier one: “space always precedes itself”. The first level can be anchored nowhere, so my first hold on the world must be the resumption of a pre-personal tradition: another subject beneath me, my body as a system of anonymous functions, for whom a world exists before I am here. When levels give way, the result is not only disorder but giddiness and nausea, the felt awareness of that contingency.

The level gives up and down. Depth is harder, because it seems to belong to the perceiver and not to the things at all. The next lesson, “Depth”, reads the section in which the chapter takes on Berkeley and the geometry of the eyes.

Method

Computing tilt against a level, period by period

Five steps; the model is the lesson's, and the last step says so.

1

Fix the image tilt

Take the tilt of the image in degrees: 45° for Wertheimer's mirror, 180° for Stratton's spectacles.

2

Set the starting level

Take the level the subject brings, 0° at period 1.

3

Apply the rule

Under a switch the level jumps to the image's tilt at the given period; under a share it moves that fraction of the remaining difference each period.

4

Subtract

Perceived tilt in the model is image tilt minus level, period by period.

5

Say what the model is

State that the subtraction is the lesson's illustration, that the suddenness is Wertheimer's report and the halving is not Stratton's, and that the chapter's level is the body's gearing to the spectacle, not a quantity.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking up is where the top of the retinal image isAt its strongest this is the realist's view: the image carries its orientation, and perception reads it off. Stratton's spectacles invert the image, and after days the subject's world is upright with the image still inverted; Wertheimer's mirror tilts the image 45° and after a few minutes the room is vertical with the image unchanged. In the lesson's model the series goes 45°, 45°, 0° while the image stays at 45°. The chapter's conclusion is that the orientation registered on the retina does not govern up and down, and that the level is re-anchored as a whole, not corrected bit by bit.
2
Thinking the spatial level is the body's own orientationThe chapter says the level is not to be confused with the orientation of one's own body. A tilted head drags the vertical with it only in an empty field or in the dark, and in the mirror the room comes upright while the subject's body is as it was. What sets the level is the body as a system of possible actions, a virtual body with a place defined by its task.
3
Taking the model's subtraction for the chapter's theoryNothing in the chapter says perceived tilt is image tilt minus a level. The subtraction is the lesson's way of putting a number on the one feature the reports give, sudden or gradual. The chapter's level is a gearing of the body to the world, and the figure leaves out what makes it one: a room the subject could walk about in and act.
