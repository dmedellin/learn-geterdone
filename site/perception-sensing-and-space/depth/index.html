Depth

Depth in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The section runs for fifteen pages and covers the whole classical theory of depth. Three ideas carry it.

1

Depth as breadth seen from the side

What the chapter says both classical theories assume: that depth is a row of points like breadth, which I happen to see end-on, and which a spectator off to the side would see spread out.

2

Signs of depth

In the classical theory, the facts from which distance is worked out: the angle at which the two eyes converge, and an object's apparent size against its known size.

3

Motive

The chapter's alternative to sign and cause: an antecedent that acts only through its significance, as a death motivates a journey, and whose force is given by the decision it motivates.

The material

The passage first, then the section line by line

The passage is the section's first paragraph, compressed. The lesson follows the section in order: Berkeley and analytical reflection, the signs of depth, the psychologists' causes, the chapter's motives, apparent size, and depth as a temporal dimension.

Space, p. 297. “Traditional ideas of perception are at one in denying that depth is visible. … In both cases depth is tacitly equated with breadth seen from the side, and this is what makes it invisible.” The sentences are on page 297 of Smith's translation, which prints no heading for the section; it begins with the first of them and ends on page 311, before the paragraph that begins with movement. Read pages 297 to 311 in your own copy before going on.

George Berkeley, the Irish philosopher and later bishop, argued in his 1709 New Theory of Vision that distance is not seen. The chapter gives his argument two forms. The retina receives only a flat projection of the spectacle, so depth has no means of being recorded; and if one objects that after the criticism of the constancy hypothesis, the assumption that each point of the stimulus gives one fixed sensation, we cannot judge what we see by the retinal image, Berkeley would probably reply, the chapter supposes, that depth still cannot be seen, because it is not spread out before the eyes but appears to them only foreshortened. Analytical reflection reaches the same verdict for theoretical reasons: even a recorded depth would be a multiplicity that has to be run over and held together, so that distance exists only for a subject who synthesizes it. The two doctrines are opposed, the chapter says, and make the same assumption.

Made explicit, the assumption is this. What I call depth is really a row of points like breadth, and I am badly placed to see it; a spectator looking on from the side would take in at a glance the objects that, for me, hide one another. What makes depth invisible to me is what makes it visible to him as breadth. So the depth declared invisible is already a depth identified with breadth. To treat it so, the subject must leave his place and think himself into a kind of ubiquity: “For God, who is everywhere, breadth is immediately equivalent to depth.” Yet the chapter grants Berkeley one thing outright: depth is not imprinted on the object but plainly belongs to the perspective, which makes it the most existential of the dimensions.

Then the classical theory itself, stated in its own voice. My table is near, the piano farther, the car drives off. These words, it is maintained, mean intervals I cannot see from where I am, revealed by the apparent size of things against their real size and by the eyes, which converge as an object approaches. “The distance is the height of a triangle with its base and base angles given to me”: the base is the distance between the eyes and the base angles are given by their convergence. The footnote cites Malebranche, Descartes's follower, and the picture goes back to Descartes's Optics of 1637, where the eyes are like a blind man's two sticks that run together as the object comes near. The Cartesians called the calculation a natural geometry. Berkeley, in his own book rather than in anything the chapter quotes, denied that anyone performs a geometry and put in its place signs learned by experience: a feeling of the eyes turning that custom has joined to distance.

The chapter's reply is that the signs presuppose what they are meant to give. I can go back from apparent size to distance only if I already know there is a world of undistortable objects and that my body stands before it like a mirror. I can read convergence as a sign of distance only if I picture my gaze as the blind man's two sticks, which puts my eyes, my body and the world in one objective space. Natural geometry and natural judgement, it concludes, are myths in Plato's sense: stories that stand for a significance enveloped in the signs, before either signs or significance is posited.

The lab computes the classical theory, in the small-angle form in which an angle is a length over a distance. With the eyes 6.5 cm apart, the near preset puts two points at 50 cm and 60 cm. The convergence tile gives 6.5 / 50 = 13/100 rad for the first and 6.5 / 60 = 13/120 rad for the second, and the disparity tile, the difference between them, 13/600, about 0.0217 rad. The far preset moves both points ten times farther, to 500 cm and 600 cm, and the disparity falls to 13/6000, a tenth. The interval preset keeps the same 10 cm interval as near but five metres off, 500 cm and 510 cm, and gives 13/51000, one eighty-fifth of the near figure. The equal preset puts both at 50 cm, and the disparity is 0.

These are the figures the theory says the mind uses, and the chapter's first objection, an observation it takes from the Gestalt psychologists, is that nobody has them. I am not expressly aware of my eyes' convergence or of an apparent size when I see something far off. The second is that the disparity the theory starts from is not there to start from: “there is disparity only for a subject who tries to fuse monocular phenomena”, two images that are disparate only for a gaze already tending towards one thing. The figures are exact, and they are the model's; the lesson's comparisons between presets are the model's too.

The Gestalt psychologists, Kurt Koffka and Paul Guillaume among them, had already seen that convergence and apparent size are not explicitly known in perception, and concluded that they are not signs but conditions or causes of depth, a law to be recorded like a law of physics. The chapter says the psychologist here begins a pure description and then interrupts it to put himself back in the world. Its own conclusion: “Convergence and apparent size are neither signs nor causes of depth”. They are present in the experience of depth as a motive is present in a decision. A death motivates my journey because it is a situation that calls for my presence, and in deciding to go I make the motive valid; the relation runs both ways.

Definition · Motive

An antecedent that acts only through its significance, not by any physical power, and whose force is confirmed by what it motivates: a death and the journey to the funeral.

Apparent size gets the same treatment. Asked what diameter I see an ash-tray as having, I cannot answer with both eyes open; I shut one, hold a pencil at arm's length and mark it, and by isolating the ash-tray I make size appear where it had no place. The sides of a road running to the horizon are neither given as converging nor as parallel: they are parallel in depth. And is not a man smaller at two hundred yards than at five? Only if I isolate him and measure. Otherwise “he is anterior to equality and inequality; he is the same man seen from farther away”, a figure that offers my gaze less to hold.

The section ends by making depth temporal. To see an object at a distance is already to hold it, or still to hold it, as I still have in hand the moment just past without any recollection in between; the chapter borrows Husserl's field of presence, which spreads in two dimensions, here and there, past and future. Beneath depth between things lies a primordial depth, the thickness of a medium with no thing in it: one patient, writing, has to push his pen through a certain thickness of whiteness before reaching the paper. And like up and down, far and near come to things from a level of distances and sizes, the scope of our gestures. Depth, in the chapter's account of the reversible cube, is the dimension in which things envelop one another, as breadth and height are those in which they lie side by side.

Space so far has been still. The next lesson, “Movement”, reads the section in which a line of light moves without passing through the space between, and Zeno's old argument comes back.

Method

Computing convergence and disparity

Four steps, and a fifth that is the chapter's.

1

Put everything in one unit

Write the separation of the eyes and the two distances in centimetres.

2

Compute each convergence

Divide the separation by each distance: the angle in radians, in the small-angle form the theory uses. At 6.5 cm and 50 cm that is 13/100.

3

Take the difference

The disparity is the nearer point's convergence minus the farther's.

4

Compare presets

Ten times the distances gives a tenth of the disparity; the same interval farther off gives much less.

5

Say whose calculation it is

State that the figures are the classical theory's, and that the chapter denies both that anyone has them in seeing depth and that they cause it.

Two points near the eyes

Take the near preset: eyes 6.5 cm apart, points at 50 cm and 60 cm. You want each point's convergence and the disparity, as the classical theory computes them.

50 cm: 6.5 / 50 = 13/100 = 0.1300 rad 60 cm: 6.5 / 60 = 13/120 ≈ 0.1083 rad disparity: 13/100 − 13/120 = 13/600 in decimals: ≈ 0.0217 rad at 500 and 600 cm: 13/6000, a tenth

On the classical theory these are the data from which the mind works out that one point is behind the other. The chapter's reply is not that the arithmetic is wrong but that no one who sees the two points has it: the disparity exists only for a gaze already trying to see one thing.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking we see depth by calculating from convergenceThis is the natural geometry of Descartes's Optics and of Malebranche, whom the chapter cites, and, with the geometry replaced by signs learned by association, Berkeley's New Theory of Vision. At its strongest it says the eyes supply exact data, 13/100 and 13/120 rad for points at 50 and 60 cm, a disparity of 13/600, and the mind does the rest. The chapter states it and rejects it: nobody is aware of these figures, the disparity exists only for a gaze trying to fuse two images, and the signs could give space only to someone who already had it. The lab computes the calculation the chapter says nobody performs.
2
Taking convergence for a cause of depthThat is the Gestalt psychologists' correction of the sign theory, and the chapter rejects it too. A cause would make experience a by-product recorded after the fact; the chapter's choice is that either experience is nothing or it must be total. Convergence is present in seeing depth as a motive in a decision, acting through its significance.
3
Thinking the far man is seen smaller and then correctedThe constancy theories say the man two hundred yards off is a smaller image enlarged by the mind, or a compromise between his near and his projected size. The chapter says he is smaller only if isolated and measured; seen in his context he is neither smaller nor equal, but the same man seen from farther away, offering the gaze less to hold.
