Fixations and Saccades

Your eyes do not glide along a line: they stop for about a quarter of a second, jump a few letters, and stop again, and the text is taken in during the stops. A stated model turns the length of a stop, the length and reach of a jump, the share of jumps that go backwards and the length of a word into fixations per word, milliseconds per word and the rate those allow.

The budget in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Every speed claim in this course is checked against one small model. It has five quantities, and each has a measured typical value.

1

Stops and jumps

A fixation is a stop, about 200 to 250 ms long in silent reading. A saccade is the jump between stops: about 7 to 9 letter spaces, in 20 to 35 ms. Vision is suppressed during the jump, so the reading is done in the stops (Rayner 1998).

2

Some jumps go backwards

About 10 to 15% of saccades in skilled readers are regressions, back to text already passed. The model counts each one as a fixation that does not advance, which understates its cost: a real regression also has to travel back.

3

A model returns a rate, not a reader

With the typical values the model allows about 267 words a minute, close to the measured averages for silent reading in English: 238 for non-fiction and 260 for fiction (Brysbaert 2019). It says what follows from the numbers put into it, not what any one reader does.

The material

Stops, jumps and the budget they make

First a timed reading, so that you have a minute of your own reading to set the model against. Then the model, which says how a minute like that is spent.

Start with the warm-up in the lab, Why the Tide Keeps Moving, at your usual pace, and answer its six questions. It is logged as practice and fills none of the model's boxes. It is 453 words long; if you read it at about 250 words a minute, the model below says your eyes stopped about four hundred times on the way through.

It did not feel like four hundred stops. Reading feels like a smooth slide along the line, and that is the picture most readers hold. Watch someone else's eyes while they read, though, and you see jerks and pauses. The jumps were described in Émile Javal's laboratory around 1879 — the observation was his colleague Lamare's, and Ewald Hering made it independently — and Javal's word for them, saccade, is the one that stuck (Wade and Tatler 2009).

Definition · Fixation, saccade, regression

A fixation is a stop of the eyes, during which text is taken in. A saccade is the jump from one fixation to the next; vision is suppressed while it happens. A regression is a saccade that goes back to text already passed.

Rayner's 1998 review of twenty years of eye-tracking gives the typical values for skilled adults reading silently: fixations of about 200 to 250 ms, with 225 a fair middle; saccades of about 7 to 9 letter spaces, taking 20 to 35 ms; and regressions making up about 10 to 15% of saccades. Add a length for a word — six characters with the space, the convention of a standard-length word — and you have the five quantities of the model.

forward fixations per word = 6 / step fixations per word = forward / (1 − regressions) time per word = fixations × (fixation + saccade) rate = 60,000 / time per word

The typical preset puts in 225 ms, 30 ms, 8 characters a step, a regression share of 3/20 and 6 characters a word. Six over eight is 3/4 of a forward fixation per word; dividing by 17/20 gives 15/17 ≈ 0.9 fixations; at 255 ms each that is 225 ms per word, and 60,000 / 225 ≈ 266.7 words a minute.

Now change one thing at a time. The no-regressions preset sets the regression share to 0: 3/4 = 0.75 fixations, 191.25 ms per word, ≈ 313.7 words a minute. That is everything that cutting every regression could buy at these settings, and Looking Back is about why you would not want to. The long-fixations preset keeps the regressions and lengthens each stop to 300 ms: still 15/17 fixations, now ≈ 291.2 ms per word, ≈ 206.1 words a minute. A stop 75 ms longer costs about sixty words a minute; the length of a stop is where most of the budget goes.

What the model leaves out, stated once. It counts a regression as one more fixation that does not advance, but a real regression also travels back and the eyes must then move forward again, so the model understates what looking back costs. Skipping short words and fixating long ones twice are folded into the average step. And six characters a word is a convention, not a measurement of your text.

  • One cost the model leaves out is the end of the line. The eyes make a long jump back to the start of the next line, a return sweep, and it costs about one more fixation per line.
  • On a line of 64 characters at 8 a step, that is about 8 forward fixations and one more for the sweep, so roughly an eighth more time than the model says.
  • Over 30 such lines, about 30 extra fixations: at 255 ms each, about 7.7 seconds a page. Shorter lines mean more sweeps for the same words.

Set the warm-up beside the model. If your warm-up came out at 240 words a minute and the typical preset says 266.7, the gap is not a finding about your eyes: the model's inputs are averages over many readers in a laboratory, and your rate is one passage on one day. What the model gives you is a way to ask where time could go, and how much each part can move the total.

The model assumes that each stop takes in about eight new characters. The next lesson, The Perceptual Span, measures how far one glance reaches.

Method

Doing it again on another day

Five steps, about ten minutes. The first needs a second person or a mirror.

1

Watch eyes read

Ask someone to read a few lines silently while you watch their eyes from the side, or hold a small mirror beside the page and glance at your own. Count the jumps across one line. The oldest demonstration there is still works.

2

Do the warm-up at your usual pace

The warm-up serves the next passage you have not logged. Read once, answer the six questions, and note the rate and the effective rate.

3

Load the typical preset and say each line

Say aloud what each tile is: fixations per word, milliseconds per word, the rate the model allows. Then do the same arithmetic on paper.

4

Move one quantity at a time

Change the fixation, then the step, then the regression share, each by a realistic amount, and write down which moved the rate most.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Open The Daily Drill from Guided Practice and do today's session: one pool passage with the pacer at your hold target, or a test at natural pace when it says one is due.

The typical reader's budget

Fixation 225 ms, saccade 30 ms, 8 characters a step, regressions 3/20 of fixations, 6 characters a word.

forward fixations = 6 / 8 = 3/4 all fixations = (3/4) / (17/20) = 15/17 time per word = (15/17) × 255 = 225 ms rate = 60,000 / 225 ≈ 266.7 words a minute

Dividing by 17/20 is what the regressions cost in the model: three stops in twenty do not advance, so the forward ones must be made up by the rest. The typical preset shows the same four figures.

Common mistakes

1
Picturing the eyes gliding along the lineThey jump and stop. Vision is suppressed during each jump, so nearly all the reading happens in the stops, about four of them a second. The jumps were described in Javal's laboratory around 1879, by Lamare, and by Hering independently; Javal's word “saccade” stuck. Any technique that promises to make the eyes “flow” is describing something the eyes do not do.
2
Reading the model's rate as your rate266.7 words a minute is what five averages imply, not a measurement of anyone. Your warm-up is one measurement of you. When they differ, neither is wrong; they answer different questions.
3
Treating a regression as freeThe model already charges a regression one stop, and a real one costs more, because the eyes must travel back and then forward again. Whether that cost is worth paying is the question of Looking Back.
