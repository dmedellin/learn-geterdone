The Perceptual Span

A row of random letters is flashed for less time than the eyes need to move, and you name the letters at a few marked positions. Scored by distance from the centre, the answers show how far letter identification reaches from one fixation. The span that matters in reading is related but different: it is measured with real text, and it is lopsided.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

One fixation takes in a window of text. How wide it is depends on what is being measured, and the drill and the reading studies measure different things.

1

A flash too short to look at

The letters are shown for 150 ms. Starting a saccade takes about 175 to 200 ms, so the flash is over before the eyes could move to it: what you report was seen from the one fixation on the centre.

2

Asking for a few letters after the flash

Fifteen letters are too many to hold in mind, so the drill marks two or three positions only after the flash has gone. Because you cannot know in advance which will be asked, your answers sample what was available everywhere in the row.

3

The span in reading is lopsided

Measured with real text, the perceptual span reaches about 3 to 4 letters to the left of the fixation and 14 to 15 to the right, and words are identified only within about 7 to 8 to the right. The asymmetry follows attention in the direction of reading (Rayner 1998).

The material

How far one glance reaches

The drill comes first in the lab and takes about five minutes. Read what it does before you start, so that the flash does not surprise you.

The drill in the lab runs twenty flashes. Each begins with a + where your eyes should rest. Then a row of fifteen random letters appears for 150 ms, a mask of # signs covers it, and three positions are marked one at a time; for each, you pick the letter that was there from four, or say you did not see it. Keep your eyes on the + and do not try to look along the row: there is no time.

The flash is shorter than the time it takes to start a saccade, about 175 to 200 ms, so everything you report was seen from the fixation on the centre. The 150 ms is requested; a screen can show it a frame or two longer, and the drill prints what it measured. The drill is logged as other, because it is not a reading.

The answers are scored by position, from −7 on the left to +7 on the right. Accuracy at a position is the letters you got right there over the times it was asked, and your span is the farthest position on each side where it was at least one half. One caution before you read your own: with four choices a pure guess is right one time in four, and twenty flashes of three marks ask each position only about four times on average (the positions are drawn at random, so some are asked more and some hardly at all), so one lucky answer can move a position by a quarter.

The typical preset writes out a reader whose every position was asked ten times: 10/10 at the centre, 9/10 at one letter either side, 8/10 at two, 6/10 at three, 4/10 at four, and 2/10 from five outwards. Its tiles read 1 = 100% at the centre, 1/5 = 20% at the outermost positions (4 right of 20 asked, close to guessing), and a span of left 3, right 3. The strong preset is the same reader with 6/10 at four letters, and its span reads left 4, right 4.

Notice that both spans are symmetric. A row of random letters has no direction to read in and no words to recognise, so nothing pulls attention one way. That is what makes this a different span from the one in reading.

The span in reading

The reading span was measured by McConkie and Rayner in 1975 with a moving window: as a reader's eyes moved, a computer changed the text outside a window around each fixation and left the window intact. When the window was wide enough that reading went on at its normal speed, it held everything the reader was using. The answer, gathered in Rayner's 1998 review, is about 3 to 4 letters to the left of the fixation and 14 to 15 to the right, with words identified only within about 7 to 8 letters to the right; beyond that, the reader picks up mostly the lengths of words.

The lopsidedness is attention, not the eye. For readers of Hebrew, which runs right to left, the span is larger to the left (Pollatsek, Bolozky, Well and Rayner 1981). The span is also smaller on hard text (Rayner 1998) and somewhat larger in faster, more skilled readers (Rayner, Slattery and Bélanger 2010). So it is set by acuity and by attention together, and it does move a little.

What it does not do, on any evidence, is reach a whole line. Some speed-reading courses teach readers to take in a line, or a page, at a glance. If your own drill came out like the typical preset, it puts a number on why that is a large claim: past three or four letters either side, even letters you were trying to catch were mostly guesses. A line of 65 characters is about eight word-identification spans long.

The worked example reads one side of the typical preset: 6/10 at +3 and 4/10 at +4. The identification of random letters ends between those two positions, and the tile says right 3.

A span limits what one fixation can take in. The next lesson, Looking Back, asks what happens when the eyes cannot return to what they have passed.

The drill, in numbers

Your measurement: a + marks where to look, then 15 random letters are flashed for 150 ms (requested; the screen may show them a little longer, and the drill prints what it measured), then a mask. Then 3 of the positions are marked, one at a time, and you pick the letter that was there from four (or say you did not see it). Letters are scored by their position from the centre, −7 to +7. A flash this short is over before the eyes could move: a saccade takes about 175 to 200 ms to start. Random letters measure letter identification by position, not the perceptual span in reading, which is wider to the right. Flashed and streamed displays are not suitable for readers with photosensitive conditions; skip this drill if that applies to you, and the rest of the lesson stands without it.

Method

Doing it again on another day

Five steps, about ten minutes. Sit at the same distance from the same screen each time.

1

Settle the eyes first

Sit at your usual distance in a quiet place. Before each flash, rest your eyes on the + and keep them there.

2

Run the twenty flashes

Answer the marked positions only. When you did not see a letter, say so rather than guess: a guess among four is right one time in four and makes the edges look wider than they are.

3

Read the accuracies by position

Find the centre, the outermost positions and the farthest position each side at half or better. Note how many times each was asked.

4

Set it beside the reading span

Write your span next to 3 to 4 left and 14 to 15 right, and say in a sentence why yours is roughly symmetric and the reading span is not.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Open The Daily Drill from Guided Practice and do today's session: one pool passage with the pacer at your hold target, or a test at natural pace when it says one is due.

Common mistakes

1
Believing a trained eye takes in a line at a glanceIn reading, the perceptual span is about 3 to 4 letters left and 14 to 15 right, and words are identified only within about 7 to 8 letters to the right. It is smaller on hard text and somewhat larger in skilled readers, but there is no evidence that training widens it to a line. Your drill measures a related but different span, letters with no context, and in the typical preset it ends three letters from the centre.
2
Reading the drill's span as the reading spanRandom letters, flashed, with no words and no direction, measure letter identification by position. The reading span is measured with real text and is lopsided. A symmetric left 3, right 3 does not contradict the reading figures; it answers a different question.
3
Trusting one position's scoreTwenty flashes of three marks ask each position about four times on average, and by chance some far fewer; a guess among four is right one time in four. One lucky answer can lift a position over the half line. Read the pattern across positions, not one cell.
