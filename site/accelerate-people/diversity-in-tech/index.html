Diversity in Tech

Chapter 10 closes with diversity: the authors say few teams in their data are diverse, give the shares of respondents by gender and by identification as underrepresented in the years they asked, cite other research on why diversity helps and why people leave, and add that diversity is not enough without inclusion. Read what they report and how far it reaches; then see on an invented sample how the share of a group among respondents can differ from its share of the industry.

Diversity in Tech in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first two are the chapter’s, the third the lab’s.

1

Inclusion

The chapter’s word, after Smith and Lindsay, for an organization in which every member feels welcomed and valued as themselves and has a strong sense of belonging. The authors write that it must be present for diversity to take hold.

2

Underrepresented minority

In the survey, a respondent’s own answer to whether they identify as underrepresented. The authors explain that because the data came from around the world, self-identification was as specific as they could be, since the groups that count as minorities differ by country.

3

Share and ratio

A group’s share is its members who meet a condition (here, answered) over its size. A ratio of two shares says how many times as likely one group is to meet it; a difference in percentage points says how far apart they are.

The material

The chapter's argument, then the count

Read the last part of Chapter 10, from Diversity in Tech to What We Can Do, in your own copy first. The lesson sets out what the authors report, with the year and the sample for each figure, what they borrow from other research, and what can and cannot be concluded.

The authors begin the section with a position and a limit. The position is that diversity matters: they cite research that teams with more diversity in gender or underrepresented minorities are smarter, achieve better team performance and better business outcomes. The limit is on their own data, in which few teams are diverse. They recommend that teams who want high performance recruit and retain more women and underrepresented minorities, and work on other kinds of diversity, for example including people with disabilities. They then add “diversity is not enough. Teams and organizations must also be inclusive.”

Note whose findings these are. The benefits of diversity are reported from other research, by Rock and Grant in 2016, Deloitte in 2013 and Hunt and colleagues in 2015, and later in the section the authors cite a study by Woolley and Malone of the collective intelligence of groups. Accelerate’s own survey does not report a test of them. What it does contribute is a description of who answered, and that is what the next paragraphs set out, in the words and with the years the chapter gives.

On gender, the authors began asking in 2015. Of all respondents, 5 percent identified as female in 2015, 6 percent in 2016 and 6.5 percent in 2017. They say the figures were much lower than they expected, because they cite about 7 percent of women in systems administration in 2011, down from 13 percent in 2008, and 27 percent in computer and information management in a report from 2013. Among the survey respondents, 33 percent reported a team with no women on it, 56 percent a team under 10 percent female and 81 percent a team under 25 percent female; the passage does not name a year for these three figures. For the 2017 study alone the authors report 91 percent male, 6 percent female and 3 percent non-binary or other. They say they started with binary gender in order to compare with existing research, and hope to extend their work to non-binary gender.

On underrepresented minorities, they asked respondents in 2017 whether they identify as underrepresented: 77 percent answered no, 12 percent yes and 11 percent preferred not to respond or answered not applicable. The authors explain that the data came from around the world, so they could not ask about particular groups; the United States defines several minorities that would not make sense elsewhere. They add that they have not yet extended the research to people with disabilities. The sample behind all of these is the survey's: over 23,000 responses in the research as a whole, reached by invitation and referral, which Chapter 15 describes.

What other research says, according to the authors: studies link women in leadership with higher financial performance; there is no significant difference between men and women in ability or aptitude in the STEM fields; and women are leaving tech at a 45 percent higher rate than men, a figure they attribute to a 2017 source. The authors suggest the answer to what keeps women and others out could be nothing more than the widespread belief that some men have an innate brilliance for technical work, and cite reports of harassment, microaggressions and unequal pay. These are the authors’ reading of other people’s studies. They end with resources, among them the Anita Borg Institute, Geek Feminism and Project Include, and with the call for all of us to prioritize diversity and inclusive environments.

Now the count, and a caution about reading a share. The authors write that the numbers they found were lower than they expected. A number like 6.5 percent of respondents is a share of the people who answered, not a share of the industry, and the two can differ for reasons of how the survey was distributed, as the sampling chapter says. In Chapter 15 the authors explain that their invitations came from several mailing lists, referrals and reaching out to underrepresented groups through their own lists, and they name the limits of such a snowball sample themselves.

The lab shows that effect on invented numbers. An invented region has 1,000 engineers: 800 in Group A and 200 in Group B, so Group B is 20 percent of the field. Group B stands for any group that the invitations reach less well. In the preset first-wave 240 of Group A answer and 30 of Group B. The lab reports the shares that answered as 30 percent and 15 percent, a ratio of 2.00 (Group A is twice as likely to answer) and a gap of 15 percentage points. Work out the composition of the sample by hand: 30 of the 270 answers are Group B, 11.1 percent, against 20 percent of the field.

In second-wave an invitation is added through a list that Group B uses, and 60 of its 200 answer. Both groups now answer at 30 percent, the ratio is 1.00 and the gap is 0, and Group B is 60 of the 300 answers, 20 percent, the same as its share of the field. The respondent shares matched the field, in this invented case, only because the answering rates did. Notice also that the lab prints a ratio and an odds ratio separately. The ratio, 2.00 in the first wave, is what the chapter means by ‘times as likely’; the odds ratio, 2.43, is a different number.

Exercise

  • An invented region of 1,000: Group P has 600 people, Group Q 400. In a survey, 180 of P answered and 80 of Q: Group-P, 600, 180; Group-Q, 400, 80.

Find each group's share that answered, the ratio of P to Q and the gap in percentage points. Then find Q's share of the answers and compare it with its share of the 1,000. Type the rows in to check.

These counts say something about how a sample was drawn, and nothing about why people leave or stay, which the authors attribute to the cultures they work in and the studies they cite. The next lesson, Transformational Leadership, begins Chapter 11 and the role of the people who set the tone.

Groups and shares"twice as likely": two shares, their ratio, their difference and the odds ratio

How the lab counts. A group's share is those meeting the outcome over its n, exact. "Times as likely" is the first group's share over the second's, shown to 2 places; the difference is the first share less the second in percentage points, to 1 place. The odds of a group are those meeting it over those not; the odds ratio is the first group's odds over the second's, to 2 places. The two ratios agree only when the outcome is rare, and neither is defined when it divides by 0.

Method

Comparing a sample with its population

Four steps, the last of them on a survey of your own.

1

Write each group's size and responses

For each group, how many were invited (or exist) and how many answered. Keep invited and answered in separate columns.

2

Compute each group's response share

Answered over invited, as a percentage. Then the ratio of one group’s share to the other’s, and the gap in percentage points.

3

Compare the sample with the population

A group’s share of the answers is its answers over all the answers; its share of the field is its size over all sizes. Put the two side by side.

4

Check a survey of your own

Take a survey you have run or answered, and compare who was invited, who answered and who the population is. Name who an invitation might have missed and what would reach them.

Group A answered: 240/800 = 30% Group B answered: 30/200 = 15% Ratio A to B: 30/15 = 2.00; gap: 15 percentage points Answers in all: 240 + 30 = 270 Group B in the sample: 30/270 = 11.1% Group B in the field: 200/1000 = 20%

Group A's odds of answering are 240 to 560, Group B's 30 to 170. Their ratio, 2.43, is not the ratio of shares, 2.00.

Common mistakes

1
Reading 6.5 percent of respondents as the share of women in the industryIt is the share of the people who answered the 2017 survey, reached by invitation and referral. The authors themselves set it beside other figures for the field and found it lower than they expected.
2
Assuming the chapter shows that diverse teams perform better in its own dataThe benefit is reported from other studies, which the authors cite; what the chapter adds is who answered and how few teams are diverse.
3
Thinking diversity alone is the goalThe chapter says that a diverse team is only the start: it must also be inclusive, and without inclusion diversity does not take hold.
