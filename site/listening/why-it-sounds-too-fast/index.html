Why It Sounds Too Fast

People learning English say it is spoken too fast. Usually the trouble is not speed. It is that about fifty very common words change shape when they are said, and those fifty words are nearly half of everything you hear. This lesson counts them on a real page, prints the list, and shows you what each one turns into.

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas, and the second is the one that helps most.

1

A small list of words does most of the damage

The words that change shape are not spread across the whole language. They are about fifty words you already know: a, the, of, to, and, was, have. Everything else is said much as it is written.

2

They are half of what you hear

On the page in the lab, 452 words out of 949 are on that list. So nearly every second word coming at you is one of fifty, in a shape you may never have been taught. That is not speed. That is a short list you have not learned yet.

3

The squashed shape is the normal one

To said as tə is not lazy or careless speech. It is how the word is said in ordinary sentences by everyone. The full form is the special case, used when the word is being stressed on its own.

The material

What the commonest ones turn into

These are the ones used most often on the page in the lab, with the shape they take in an ordinary sentence.

Ask anyone learning English what the hardest part is and a great many will say the same thing: people speak too fast. They can read the page. They know the words. Said out loud, it goes past them.

The speed is mostly not the problem, and this lesson is about what is.

Half the sentence is fifty words

English has a group of very common words that change shape when they are not being stressed — squashed, as this course will call it. To becomes a short flat sound. And loses most of itself. Of, was, have, for, can all do the same.

There are about fifty of them, and that is the whole list. Everything else in English keeps its shape.

Now count how often they turn up. On the page in the lab, which is 949 words of a novel, 452 of those words are on the list. That is 47.6%, and only 51 different words account for all of it.

What that means for listening

Nearly every second word reaching your ear is one of fifty, in a shape you may never have been shown. The words carrying the meaning are still said clearly. What is between them has been flattened.

A learner who knows every word on a page can still fail to hear that page when it is read out, and nothing is wrong with their ears or the words they know. They were taught the written shape of fifty words and not the spoken one.

Why they blur together

The squashed forms nearly all use the same vowel, a short flat sound made in the middle of the mouth. So they are not only shorter, they also sound like each other.

That is why a run of small words can arrive as a single noise. There are four or five words in it, they are all from the same short list, and they are all wearing the same vowel.

Method

Reading a sentence as it will sound

Four steps, and you can do them on paper.

1

Find the words from the list

Go through the sentence and mark every word that appears in the printed list of fifty. In most sentences that is about half.

2

Say the rest of the sentence normally

Those are the words carrying the meaning, and they keep their full shape.

3

Squash the marked ones

Not dropped and not missed out: made shorter, with the vowel reduced to the same flat sound. The lab prints what each one becomes.

4

Say the whole thing again

It will be quicker than your first attempt, and closer to what you hear. Nothing was spoken faster; fewer parts were given full weight.

Every squashed form uses the same vowel, the flat one in the middle of the mouth. That is why they blur together: they are not only short, they all sound alike.

This is also why the list is worth learning as a list. Fifty words, one vowel, and nearly half of every sentence stops being a surprise.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking the words are being droppedThey are not missing. They are short and flat, and a learner listening for the full form hears a gap where a word was. The word was there.
2
Learning only the full formsA course that teaches to as the full form and never mentions the squashed one has taught you the rarer of the two. In ordinary speech the squashed one is normal.
3
Blaming your ears, or the speakerNearly half of what reaches you is fifty words in a shape nobody showed you. That is a gap in what you were taught, and it closes quickly once you can see it.
