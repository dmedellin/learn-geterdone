Schneider: Concrete and Abstract Movement

Gelb and Goldstein's patient performs the movements of his daily life quickly and precisely and fails at movements that answer no actual situation. The case in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The middle of Chapter 3 reads one patient's case at length. Three distinctions carry it.

1

Concrete and abstract movement

Gelb and Goldstein's terms. A concrete movement answers an actual situation, like reaching for the handkerchief to blow your nose; an abstract movement answers none, like bending a finger because the doctor asked.

2

Grasping and pointing

Their German words are Greifen and Zeigen. To grasp a place on the body is to go to it; to point to it is to show it, as one position among others.

3

Projection

The chapter's name for the normal power that makes abstract movement possible: keeping in front of oneself a free space in which what is not there can take on a semblance of existence.

The material

The passage first, then the case line by line

The passage is the chapter's report of what Schneider can do. The lesson reads what he cannot do beside it, the chapter's description of the difference, and the explanation from his eyesight that the chapter argues does not settle it.

Part One, Chapter 3, p. 118. “Even when his eyes are closed, the patient performs with extraordinary speed and precision the movements needed in living his life, provided that he is in the habit of performing them: he takes his handkerchief from his pocket and blows his nose, takes a match out of a box and lights a lamp.” The sentence is on page 118 of Smith's translation. Read from the sentence on page 117 that announces an example of morbid motility to the end of the paragraph on page 138 that closes on the basic disturbance, in your own copy before going on.

The patient is the man the book calls Schneider. He was a soldier wounded in June 1915; the book says later in the chapter that a shell splinter struck him at the back of the head. The psychologist Adhémar Gelb and the neurologist Kurt Goldstein, working in Frankfurt, published on him from 1918. The case was questioned afterwards. In 1949, four years after this book, Bay, Lauenstein and Cibis, and separately Richard Jung, examined him again and doubted the reliability of the reports; Jung found that he could see and recognise most objects. Modern reviews differ over what is left. Goldenberg, in 2003, doubts that it deserves its place as a classic case and judges that Gelb and Goldstein were too eager for an all-embracing theory; Marotta and Behrmann, in 2004, grant the doubts and propose reading it as an integrative visual agnosia. The book's argument concerns the case as Gelb and Goldstein reported it, and so does this lesson.

What he cannot do is set out first. With his eyes shut he cannot make abstract movements, ones that answer no actual situation, such as moving an arm or bending a finger to order. He cannot say where his limbs are, and when he is touched he cannot say where. He manages these things only if he may watch the limb, or go through preparatory movements of the whole body. Yet the passage's movements he makes at once, and at work, making wallets, his output is three quarters of a normal workman's.

Then the dissociation the case is known for, reported, the chapter says, in this patient and also in patients with cerebellar injuries: “the same subject who is unable to point to order to a part of his body, quickly moves his hand to the point where a mosquito is stinging him”. The book's example of a part to be pointed to is the nose, which a patient can point to only if allowed to take hold of it; a note gives that example to one of Goldstein's cerebellar patients. Grasping, the chapter concludes, is different from pointing, and “bodily space may be given to me in an intention to take hold without being given in an intention to know”.

Even a concrete movement done to order is done differently. Asked to give a military salute, Schneider adds the other outward marks of respect; miming combing his hair, he holds an imaginary mirror with the other hand. He can do it only by putting himself mentally in the situation the movement belongs to. A normal person saluting to order knows it is an experiment, plays the soldier for a moment and keeps to the essentials. Schneider, the chapter says, cannot play-act. At work his space is given to him as the world at this moment: the leather to be cut up, the lining to be sewn.

The lab is the lesson's model of that difference. A target is either given in the body's situation, as the itch is given to the scratching hand, or given as a position in objective space, a point on a grid. In the tasks preset the body faces east, and there are three targets: the handkerchief and the mosquito bite, given in the body's situation, and the nose on command, given as a position at (1/10, 0). The room and body tiles print every target's position in both frames, as in the last lesson, the nose ahead 1/10, left 0. The frame tile prints no conversion for the first two, and for the third, converted to ahead 1/10, left 0: the objective position has to be turned into one relative to the body before a hand could go there.

The turned preset turns the body to face north and gives the nose its new room position, (0, 1/10). The conversion has to be done again and gives ahead 1/10, left 0 once more, while the two body-given targets still need none. The command preset shows the command target alone. The model is the lesson's, and it is a simplification: the chapter does not say that Schneider fails because he cannot do the arithmetic, but that pointing asks for a body placed among places, which is what he lacks.

The chapter's word for it is background. When I beckon a friend, I beckon over there, where he is, and if he hesitates my gesture changes without any intervening thought. Make the same movement for nobody, as a flexion of the forearm, and the body that was the vehicle of the movement becomes its end. In the chapter's words, “the background to concrete movement is the world as given, whereas the background to abstract movement is built up”, and “Concrete movement is therefore centripetal whereas abstract movement is centrifugal.” What Schneider lacks, it says, is neither motility nor thought, but something between them, a motor project or motor intentionality; the normal power that makes abstract movement possible it calls projection.

Definition · Concrete and abstract movement (Gelb and Goldstein's terms)

A concrete movement adheres to a situation already given and draws its background from it; an abstract movement throws out its own background, a virtual space in which a movement can be made for no actual purpose.

Then the objection, at its strongest. Science looks beneath phenomena for causes, and here the cause seems known. Schneider's injury was occipital, at the back of the brain where sight is processed; he recognises nothing by sight alone, and his abstract movements succeed when he watches the limb. John Stuart Mill's methods of induction, the Victorian logician's rules for finding a cause by what varies with what, would conclude that pointing and abstract movement depend on visual representation, and concrete movement on touch. Gelb and Goldstein's first works read the case that way.

The chapter's reply comes in stages. Disorders of pointing and abstract movement occur in cerebellar patients too, in whom there is no reason to presume a primary disorder of sight, so there is no reason to pick out sight as the cause. There are signs that touch is affected too: a patient Goldstein sets beside Schneider can knock at a door only if it is within reach, even with his eyes on it, though the chapter grants that the visual reading can always be kept with an auxiliary hypothesis. And in psychology, it argues, no experiment can decide between the visual and the tactile reading, because both are names for one central disturbance. “There are, moreover, no explanations without comprehension.” Sight, touch and motility are three expressions of something more basic, not three causes.

That leaves the question of what the basic disturbance is, and the chapter's next move is to try the intellectualist answer. The next lesson, “The Intentional Arc”, reads it.

Method

Classifying a task by how its target is given

Four steps, on the lesson's model.

1

Name the task

Say what the movement is for: blowing your nose, scratching a bite, showing the doctor where your nose is.

2

Say how the target is given

In the body's situation, when the task itself places it, as the itch places the spot to be scratched; as a position in objective space, when it is named or shown as one point among others.

3

Convert where needed

An objectively given target must be put into the body's frame, ahead and left or right, before a hand could go to it. A body-given one needs nothing.

4

Name the task that separates the two

Find the task that is grasping in one form and pointing in the other, the bite scratched and the place shown on command, and say which, on Gelb and Goldstein's report, the patient can do.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking Schneider's trouble is a visual deficit, so a problem of seeing and not of movingHis injury was occipital, he recognises nothing by sight alone, and his abstract movements succeed when he watches the limb, so Mill's methods point to sight. The chapter takes this reading up and argues it does not cover the case: the same disorders occur in cerebellar patients with no primary disorder of sight, there are signs that touch is affected too, and the deficit is in the power of projecting a motor situation, of taking the body as a place among places on command. In the lab the command target is the one that needs converting.
2
Thinking Schneider has lost the knowledge of where his body isHe finds the bite at once and takes hold of his nose without searching. What fails is one kind of knowledge of place, the kind that gives the place as a position to be shown. The chapter's sentence is that bodily space may be given in an intention to take hold without being given in an intention to know.
3
Taking the normal person's movement to order for Schneider's, only quickerThe chapter warns against reading the normal off the pathological by a change of sign. Schneider's preparatory movements are substitutes for a function he has lost, not a slowed-down version of what everyone does; a normal person saluting to order plays the soldier, and Schneider cannot play-act.
