Bigger or More Big

A comparison of an adjective is made with -er or with more, and books say that short words take -er and long words take more. Counting the parts of the word makes that exact. On the 367 comparisons in the novel, the rule is right on 337. Reading the 30 it gets wrong shows what an old text does that a modern one does not.

The rule, and its score on the novel

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas. The third is where the 30 wrong forms are.

1

Count the parts, not the letters

A part is one beat of a word, built round a vowel sound: big has one, happy two, beautiful three. The lab takes the count from CMUdict, a dictionary of sounds. Word Stress goes further into parts; here you only need to count them.

2

Three parts or more: more, every time

In the novel, adjectives of three parts give 18 forms and none takes -er or -est. Adjectives of four parts or more give 10 forms with the same result. One part goes the other way: 268 forms with -er or -est and only 2 with more or most.

3

Two parts is where the choosing is

The novel gives 54 forms of two parts an ending and 15 a more. The rule takes adjectives of two parts ending in -y and sends the rest to more, and nearly all of its mistakes are here.

The material

The 30 forms the rule gets wrong

30 of the novel's 367 forms break rule A. They come in three kinds.

The rule most books give is about size: short words take -er and long words take more. It is a good start, and it leaves one question open. How short is short? Pleasant and handsome are both eight letters and two parts, and the novel puts -er on both.

Count parts instead of letters. Big has one part, happy two, beautiful three. The rule then has three lines. One part takes -er and -est. Two parts ending in -y take them too, with the y changed to i. Everything else takes more and most.

How the rule scores on a novel

The lab holds 367 comparisons from Pride and Prejudice. Rule A is right on 337, which is 91.8%. The table of parts shows why it works so well. Of the forms of one part, 268 take an ending and 2 take more. Of the forms of three parts and longer, none take an ending: 18 and 10 take more. The only place the two choices both occur in numbers is two parts, 54 with an ending and 15 with more.

Where the rule breaks

Adjectives of two parts are the whole difficulty. Rule A sends only those ending in -y to an ending, and the novel gives an ending to far more: 54 of its 69 forms of two parts. 22 of the 30 wrong forms are this. They belong to ten adjectives: handsome, pleasant, common, gentle, minute, narrow, noble, quiet, severe and stupid. Some of the ten are still good English with an ending: quieter, narrower, gentler. A modern writer would give handsome and pleasant more and most. The lab cannot sort the two groups, because the sort is a judgement about how English sounds now; the novel is not wrong, it is 1813.

Rule B tries to catch some of them by also giving an ending to two parts that end in -ow, -le or -er. It moves four forms, gentlest, narrowest, nobler and noblest, and the score goes from 337 to 341 of 367, or 92.9%. That is a rule built to fit the novel. The other 18 forms end in -e, -t, -n and -d, and no short ending covers them.

The other eight

Three are oftener, which is an adverb: the rule is for adjectives, and these lines are outside it. Five go the other way. More angry, more likely and most likely are words of two parts ending in -y that the novel gives more, and more strange and most sure are words of one part that it gives more. Today more likely is the usual form, so that pair is a hole in the rule's -y line and not a fault in the novel.

A second text and a modern one

The play has 49 forms. Rule A is right on 44, which is 89.8%, and rule B on 45, which is 91.8%. The misses are remotest twice, noblest, oftener and pleasanter, the same kinds as the novel. The two modern documents have 32 forms, and both rules are right on all 32. None of the 32 uses more or most. They are the Census Bureau story “U.S. Population Aging as Nation Turns 250” (2026) and the US Supreme Court opinion Stanley v. City of Sanford, 606 U.S. 46 (2025), with words such as older and earlier, so 32 right says that nothing in them breaks the rule. It does not say that a modern writer prefers more handsome.

Take the rule, use it on a word you have not met, and check an old form against its date before you copy it.

Method

Choosing the form for an adjective in front of you

Four steps, then one check.

1

Say the adjective and count its parts

Big has one. Happy has two. Beautiful has three.

2

One part: add the ending

Big becomes bigger and biggest. The last letter doubles here as it does in When the Last Letter Doubles.

3

Two parts ending in -y: change the y to i and add the ending

Happy becomes happier and happiest.

4

Every other adjective: put more or most in front

More beautiful, most important. The adjective does not change.

5

Check the age of what you are copying

The novel is from 1813. It writes handsomer and pleasanter. Today's writing, as the lab's modern lines show, keeps the rule above.

Four adjectives, one rule, one old habit

Count the parts, apply the rule, then look at the last line.

big 1 part bigger happy 2 parts happier beautiful 3 parts more beautiful handsome 2 parts more handsome handsomer (1813)

The last two lines are the largest group of misses. Of the 30 wrong forms, 22 are an adjective of two parts that the novel gives an ending: handsomest 4, pleasanter 4, handsomer 3, pleasantest 2, and one each of commonest, gentlest, minutest, narrowest, nobler, noblest, quieter, severest and stupider. Rule B moves four of them to the right side, those of gentle, narrow and noble, and its score is 341 of 367, or 92.9%. The rule that is right today for handsome and pleasant is more.

Of the other eight, three are oftener, an adverb, and the rule was not built for it. The last five go the other way: more angry, more likely and most likely are words of two parts ending in -y that the novel gives more, and more strange and most sure are words of one part that it gives more.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking more is always safeMore is safe for a word of three parts or more: the novel uses it on all 28 such forms. For a word of one part it is the error, as in more big: the novel puts -er or -est on 268 of its 270 forms of one part. Its two exceptions, more strange and most sure, are a writer's choice and not a rule.
2
Putting -er on a long wordBeautifuler is not a form anyone wrote. Not one of the 28 forms of three parts or more has an ending, in a novel that writes handsomer without a second thought.
3
Copying every form from an old book22 of the 30 forms the rule gets wrong are an ending on a word of two parts. Some, such as quieter, are still good English; others, such as handsomer and pleasanter, a modern writer would give more. A form that is in a printed book is not for that reason a form to copy. Look at the date.

Note: The lab counts spellings and takes the parts from CMUdict, so it says nothing about meaning or taste. It cannot show that a modern writer would choose more handsome: the two modern documents have 32 forms, all 32 with -er or -est (a Census Bureau story and a Supreme Court opinion, named in the lesson, with words such as older and earlier), so they show only that nothing in them breaks the rule. The novel is the place where the rule fails and the place the reader learns it from.
