Code-Tuning Strategies

Tuning in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The chapter is about where to spend effort on speed; the lesson keeps its definition, its principle, and the arithmetic that decides whether a change matters.

1

Code tuning

Changing correct code, at the scale of a few lines or one routine, to make it run more efficiently. The chapter separates it from changes to requirements, design or hardware, which it puts first.

2

The Pareto principle

Here, the observation that a small part of a program accounts for most of its running time. The book reports Boehm's 20 percent of routines taking 80 percent of the time and Knuth's under 4 percent of a program taking over half.

3

Speedup

The time before a change divided by the time after it. A routine made k times faster changes the total only by the share of the time that routine took.

The material

The chapter's strategy, then the arithmetic of a profile

The lesson follows the six sections and then works out what speeding up one routine does to the whole program.

Code Complete, Chapter 25, p. 608. “Most programs spend most of their time in a small fraction of their code. You won’t know which code that is until you measure it.” Read Chapter 25, pages 587 to 608, in your own copy first.

Your program is slow, and you have a suspect: the formatting routine, with its string building, which you have always thought wasteful. Chapter 25 asks you first whether the code is the place to look at all. Section 25.1 (p. 588) separates performance from code speed. McConnell's example is his own: a camera whose software made him upload pictures one at a time, replaced by a card reader that let him copy them all at once. He did not care whether the reader's code was faster, because his throughput was. Before code, the section lists six other places to look: the program's requirements, where a performance target is often stated that nobody needs; its design; the design of classes and routines; its dealings with the operating system; the compiler; and the hardware. Code tuning is last.

Section 25.2 (p. 591) defines code tuning as changing correct code, a few lines or a routine at a time, to run more efficiently, and admits its appeal. Then the Pareto principle. The book reports Barry Boehm's figure, that 20 percent of a program's routines consume 80 percent of its time (1987), and Donald Knuth's, that less than 4 percent of a program usually accounts for more than half of its run time (1971). Knuth found it with a profiler that counted the lines executed, and the book draws the consequence: measure to find the hot spots, and put your effort there. These are their programs' figures, from 1971 and 1987; your profile is what applies to yours.

The section then takes apart four beliefs it calls old wives' tales: that fewer lines of source run faster; that some operations are probably faster than others, when the answer changes with every compiler and machine; that you should optimise as you go; and that speed matters as much as correctness. Against optimising as you go it cites Knuth's warning about premature optimisation, and points out that nobody can tell which small part of a program will be the bottleneck until the program works. M. A. Jackson's rules of optimisation, which the chapter quotes in its margin, make the point drily: the first is don't, and the second, for experts only, is not yet. Good compilers, it adds, optimise straightforward code better than tricky code.

Section 25.3 (p. 597) names the usual sources of slowness, among them input and output, paging, system calls, interpreted languages and errors, and notes that the relative cost of common operations changed between the book's editions. Section 25.4 (p. 603) insists on measurement, with a story against himself. McConnell once rewrote a matrix-summing routine with pointers, sure he had saved thousands of multiplications, and measured it to enjoy the result: no improvement at all, at any size, because the compiler was already doing the same thing. What tuning without measurement reliably produces, he concludes, is harder code. Section 25.5 (p. 605) adds that gains come from many changes in combination, and Section 25.6 (p. 606) gives the approach: save a working version, measure, decide whether the problem is design or code, tune the bottleneck, measure each change, and revert any that does not help.

Definition · Speedup from tuning one routine

If a routine took t of a program's total time T and is made k times faster, the new total is T minus t plus t over k, and the speedup is T over the new total. The saving can never exceed t, however large k is.

The lab takes a profile, routine by routine, with a factor for each: 1 for unchanged, 10 for ten times faster. The profile is the lesson's illustration, not the book's: parse 600 ms, compute 250, format 100, write 50. The tune-format preset makes format ten times faster. Time before is 1,000 ms; Time after is 910, since 100 ms became 10; Speedup is 100/91, about 1.10; Saved is 9%. Your suspect was ten times faster and the program 9 percent faster.

The tune-parse preset only halves parse, the routine that takes most of the time: Time after 700, Speedup 10/7, about 1.43, Saved 30%. The tune-the-small-ones preset makes format and write each a thousand times faster: Time after 17003/20 = 850.15, Speedup 20000/17003, about 1.18, Saved about 15.0%, half of what halving parse gives. The last tile, Routines reaching 4/5 of the time, reads 2 of 4 for all three: taken largest first, parse and compute already make 850 of the 1,000 ms before any change. That four-fifths is the lab's target, not a law; on your own profile the count could be one routine or six.

Method

Deciding what to tune

Four steps from a profile, and one with a profile of your own.

1

Measure first

Take a profile of the program on a realistic run, and list its routines by the time each takes, largest first.

2

Find the few that matter

Count how many routines, from the top of the list, make up most of the time. Those are the only candidates.

3

Work out the most a change can save

For a routine taking t of the total T, made k times faster, the new total is T minus t plus t over k. Even an infinite k saves only t.

4

Measure the change, and keep it only if it helps

Save a working version, make one change, measure again, and revert the change if the time did not fall.

5

Profile a program of your own

Take a profile of a program of yours, type its top four routines into the lab with their times, and note how many reach four-fifths of the time and what the best single change could save.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking a tenfold speedup of a routine makes the program much fasterA routine that runs ten times faster feels like a large win, and on its own benchmark it is one. The program gains only in proportion to the share of its time that routine took, which is why the chapter says to measure before tuning. On the lesson's illustration tune-format makes a routine ten times faster and the program 9 percent faster, while tune-parse makes the hot routine only twice as fast and saves 30 percent.
2
Believing fewer lines run fasterThe chapter's first old wives' tale. The number of lines of source says nothing reliable about the size or speed of the machine code, and its own measurements show a loop written out line by line running faster than the loop. Only a measurement says which is faster.
3
Tuning as you writeOptimising every routine as it is written spends the effort on code that is not the bottleneck, because nobody can tell which small part of a program will be until it runs. The chapter's order is to make it correct and clear, then measure, then tune the few places the measurement points to.
