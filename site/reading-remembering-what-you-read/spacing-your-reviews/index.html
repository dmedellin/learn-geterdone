Spacing Your Reviews

A review schedule is a first gap, a rule for the next gap, a number of reviews and their length. Compute the days the reviews fall on and the minutes they cost, for gaps that double, gaps that stay equal and reviews crammed into one day. Spaced review keeps more at a delay than massed review; whether the gaps should grow is less settled.

A schedule in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

What you read fades, and a review slows the fading. The question here is when to review, not how often.

1

Spaced and massed

Massed review is all of it in one sitting; spaced review divides the same minutes among sessions with gaps between them. The total can be the same; what changes is when.

2

A schedule is arithmetic

Take a first gap and multiply each gap by the same number to get the next. The review days are the running totals of the gaps: gaps of 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 days put reviews on days 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31.

3

Expanding or equal

Gaps that grow are one reasonable choice, and gaps that stay the same are another. The evidence that spacing helps is strong; the evidence that the gaps should grow is mixed.

The material

When to come back to a text

A schedule is a handful of additions. The research says which kind of schedule to choose, and how firmly.

If the delayed test from Retrieval Beats Rereading is due, this page shows its banner in place of the warm-up: take the test first, from the link, and come back. Otherwise the warm-up reading above the panel is today's timed reading, a hard passage with six questions, logged as practice; another visit serves a second one.

The first measurements of forgetting were Hermann Ebbinghaus's, published in 1885. Testing his own memory for lists of nonsense syllables, he measured how quickly it faded, and he reported that learning spread over several days held better than the same learning massed together.

Cepeda and colleagues (2006) gathered the later studies of verbal recall into one review. Spaced practice beat massed practice, and the best gap grew with how long the material had to be remembered: the longer you need a text, the longer the gaps between reviews can be.

The expanding preset is the schedule the lab opens with: a first gap of 1 day, each gap twice the last, five reviews of 10 minutes. The gaps are 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 days, so the reviews fall on days 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31, and they cost 5 × 10 = 50 minutes.

gaps: 1 2 4 8 16 days: 1 3 7 15 31 5 × 10 = 50 minutes

The equal preset spends the same 50 minutes with a first gap of 6 days and a multiplier of 1, so every gap is 6 days: reviews on days 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30. It ends near the same day as the expanding schedule, with its gaps all the same size.

The massed preset spends the 50 minutes the other way: a first gap of 1 day and a multiplier of 0, so every later gap is 0 and all five reviews fall on day 1, one after another. (The lab takes no first gap of 0, so day 1 stands for the day of the cramming; what matters is that the five reviews share one day.)

Massed review is the one that feels best. By the fifth pass in a row the text is fluent, a self-test during the session scores high, and each review is easier than the last. Spaced reviews feel worse, because each one starts with something forgotten. The spaced schedule is the one retained better at a delay (Cepeda et al. 2006).

Should the gaps grow? Expanding gaps are one reasonable schedule, but they are not what the review found. On expanding against equal gaps the evidence is mixed: Karpicke and Roediger (2007) found that expanding retrieval helped retention in the short term, and that equally spaced retrieval did as well or better over the longer term.

A review of a reading is best done as Retrieval Beats Rereading suggests: a free recall with the text closed, then a look back at what you missed, rather than a reread. Ten minutes covers an article.

Method

Planning reviews for a text you need

Five steps. The second is a choice, not a formula.

1

Decide how long you need it

A text you need next week and a text you need in a year want different gaps; the longer you need it, the longer the gaps can be.

2

Choose a first gap and a rule

A day, then doubling, is one reasonable start; equal gaps of a few days are another. Put the review days in your calendar.

3

Review by recalling

At each review, write what you remember with the text closed, then check it against the text and note what you missed.

4

Count the minutes

Reviews times minutes each. If the total is more than the text is worth to you, drop a review rather than closing the gaps.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Common mistakes

1
Massed review works better, because it feels betterReview crammed into one sitting feels fluent and produces high scores during the session; spaced review is retained better at a delay (Cepeda et al. 2006). The massed and the expanding presets both cost 50 minutes; only the second spreads them to day 31.
2
Taking the expanding schedule to be the proven oneDoubling gaps are one reasonable schedule, not a finding. The evidence for spacing is strong; on expanding against equal gaps it is mixed, and Karpicke and Roediger (2007) found equal gaps as good or better over the longer term.
3
Reviewing by rereadingA review that rereads the text is a second reading, with the fluency problem of Retrieval Beats Rereading. Recall first with the text closed, then check: the review is then a retrieval, spaced.
