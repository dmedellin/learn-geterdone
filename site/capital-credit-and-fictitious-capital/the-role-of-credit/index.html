The Role of Credit in Capitalist Production

Chapter 27 lists what credit does: it equalises profits, cheapens circulation, forms stock companies and puts other people's capital at the disposal of a few. Compute a stock company's profit paid out as dividend and its manager's salary, set beside an owner who runs his own capital and a dealer who works mostly with borrowed money.

The stock company in five lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Engels says this chapter came almost whole from Marx's manuscript. It is short, a numbered list, and it contains some of the most quoted sentences in Volume III.

1

The stock company

A business whose capital is put up by shareholders who do not run it. It can be far larger than any one fortune, as railways and canals had to be.

2

Owner and manager

The two sides of the old capitalist, split between two people: the owner of capital, who receives a dividend, and the functioning manager, who is paid a salary.

3

Command of others' capital

Credit lets a dealer work with capital that is mostly borrowed, so that the risk he runs is, in Marx's phrase, social property and not his own.

The material

The split in the capitalist, then Marx's verdict on it

The passage comes from the chapter's third point, on stock companies. The verdict comes a page later, after a bracketed insertion by Engels.

Volume III, Chapter 27. “Transformation of the actually functioning capitalist into a mere manager, an administrator of other people's capital, and of the owners of capital into mere owners, mere money-capitalists.”

If you have a pension or a fund that holds shares, you are one of these “mere owners”. You will never meet the people who run the firms whose dividends you receive, and they are paid salaries, like you. In Marx's day the stock company was new outside railways, canals, banks and insurance, and he saw in it the figure of Volume I's capitalist splitting in two.

Read the rest of the point. The manager's salary “is, or is supposed to be, a mere wage of a certain kind of skilled labor, the price of which is regulated in the labormarket”. The shareholders' dividend may contain both interest and profit of enterprise, the whole profit, but it is received “only in the form of interest, that is, in the form of a mere compensation of the ownership of capital”. In Merchant's Capital and Interest the profit of a borrowed capital split into interest for the owner and profit of enterprise for the man who ran it. Here the man who runs it is an employee, and nothing is left that could be called profit of enterprise.

The lab makes that a sum. In the stock-company preset £1,000 of shares earn £220 at a rate of profit of 22%; the manager is paid a salary of £20, and the lab counts the remaining £200 as a dividend at 20% on the shares, the “interest” on them. Profit of enterprise is £0. In the owner-managed preset an owner works his own £1,000 at 20%: reckoned the way Chapter 23 reckons it, £50 is interest on his capital at 5% and £150 is profit of enterprise, but both are his. In the leveraged preset a dealer works £1,000 of which £800 is borrowed at 5%: the lenders get £40, he keeps £160, and on his own £200 that is 80%. The figures are illustrative.

That last preset is the chapter's fourth point. Credit gives the individual “absolute command of the capital of others”, and with it of the labour of others, “within certain limits”. Two of the old defences of profit go with it. The capitalist who saved his capital? “Equally stale becomes the phrase concerning the origin of capital from saving, for what he demands is precisely that others shall save for him.” And the speculating merchant's risk: “What the speculating wholesale merchant risks is social property, not his own.”

Volume III, Chapter 27. “This is the abolition of the capitalist mode of production within capitalist production itself, a self-destructive contradiction, which represents on its face a mere phase of transition to a new form of production.”

This is the sentence the chapter is remembered for, and it is worth reading slowly. The “abolition” is of capital as private property run by its owner: the means of production are already held in common, by thousands of shareholders, and run by hired staff. But the same paragraph says what it brings with it: “a new aristocracy of finance, a new sort of parasites in the shape of promoters, speculators and merely nominal directors”. Marx calls the stock company “private production without the control of private property”, and sets beside it the workers' co-operative factories, where the opposition of capital and labour is overcome “only in the form of making the associated laborers their own capitalists”.

Engels added a bracketed paragraph thirty years later, on what had happened since: cartels fixing output, and then trusts. His example is the United Alkali Trust, which brought more than thirty works under one management, with shares of about £5 million given to the former owners and £1 million of working capital raised from the public. Marx also notes, in passing, that a company that pays only interest can go on operating, so such firms “do not necessarily come under the regulation of the average rate of profit”, and he says outright that this “is one of the causes, which stem the fall of the rate of profit”.

The standard objection came in 1932, when Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means showed how widely ownership and control had separated in American corporations and argued the opposite moral: that managers, unchecked by scattered owners, could run firms in their own interest. Later economists made this the principal-agent problem, and executive pay tied to profits is the attempt to solve it. On that account the manager is not simply a wage earner. Marx's chapter takes the salary at its face, a wage set in the labour market, and the lab does the same.

Marx ends with credit's double character. It drives production to its limits and speeds the crises, and it is also “a transition to a new mode of production”. That is why, he says, its great promoters “from Law to Isaac Pereire” have the “pleasant character of swindlers and prophets”.

So far credit has been about who owns and who runs. “Medium of Circulation and Capital” asks how much money a business economy needs, and whether it matters that some of it is capital.

Who receives the profit

The capital employed, the rate of profit, the capital that is lent or held as shares, the rate paid on it and any manager's salary go in. The lab pays the salary and the interest out of the profit and prints what is left as profit of enterprise. In the stock-company preset the “interest” is the dividend on the shares.

Method

Dividing a company's profit

Four steps for a capital that is owned by one party and run by another.

1

Compute the profit

Multiply the capital employed by the rate of profit.

2

Pay the manager

Take out the manager's salary, counted as the wage of a kind of skilled labour.

3

Pay the owners

Multiply the capital the owners or lenders put up by the rate they are paid. In a stock company that rate is the dividend on the shares.

4

See what is left

Profit of enterprise is whatever remains. When the dividend takes all of it, the whole profit has taken the form of interest.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking that in a stock company the profit of enterprise stays with the people who run itIn the stock-company preset the profit is £220, the manager is paid a £20 salary, and the remaining £200 goes to the shareholders as a 20% dividend: profit of enterprise is £0. Marx's point is that the whole profit takes “the form of interest”, and the function of the old capitalist is paid as “a mere wage of a certain kind of skilled labor”. Whether managers in fact take more, as Berle and Means argued, is a question about real firms the lab does not settle.
2
Reading the “abolition” as the end of capitalismMarx calls it a “transition” that stays “ensnared in the boundaries of capitalism”. Profit is still paid out, now to shareholders, and the same page describes a “new aristocracy of finance”.
3
Treating borrowed capital as the borrower's risk aloneIn the leveraged preset £800 of the £1,000 is other people's. If the business fails, it is chiefly their capital that is lost; the dealer has £200 at stake and keeps £160 of the profit when it succeeds.
