Honour, Dignity and Worthiness

Honour is the sign of the value we set on a man, dignity is the value the commonwealth sets, and neither is the same as his worth or his worthiness.

The passage first, then the chapter line by line

Chapter 10. “The publique worth of a man, which is the Value set on him by the Common-wealth, is that which men commonly call DIGNITY. And this Value of him by the Common-wealth, is understood, by offices of Command, Judicature, publike Employment; or by Names and Titles, introduced for distinction of such Value.”

In the last lesson the buyers were private: a city, a faction. Here the buyer is the commonwealth itself, which can set a value on a man that no one else's need would give him, and can set it by a stroke of the pen. Think of a title or a rank: it costs the man nothing to be addressed by it and it changes what everyone around him does. That is why Hobbes calls dignity a man's public worth: a worth, but one set by a single buyer whose valuation binds everyone.

Before it, though, he has defined honour, and the order matters. “The manifestation of the Value we set on one another, is that which is commonly called Honouring, and Dishonouring.” Honour is the sign. And it is measured relatively: “high, and low, in this case, is to be understood by comparison to the rate that each man setteth on himselfe.” Praise a man at exactly the rate he sets on himself and you have neither honoured nor insulted him.

Then comes a long list of ways of honouring, and its logic is the same every time. “To pray to another, for ayde of any kind, is to HONOUR; because a signe we have an opinion he has power to help”. “To obey, is to Honour; because no man obeyes them, whom they think have no power to help, or hurt them.” Great gifts honour, because they buy protection; little gifts dishonour, because they are alms. To give way, to agree, to imitate, to trust, to hearken to counsel are all honours, and the opposites are all dishonours. Each is read as a signal of an opinion about power, which is why the chapter defines honourable things the way it does: “Honourable is whatsoever possession, action, or quality, is an argument and signe of Power.”

It follows, and Hobbes says so plainly, that honour has nothing to do with justice. “Nor does it alter the case of Honour, whether an action (so it be great and difficult, and consequently a signe of much power,) be just or unjust: for Honour consisteth onely in the opinion of Power.” His examples are the adulteries of Jupiter, piracy, a lawful trade among the Greeks and other nations before great commonwealths, and the duel in his own day. It is among the harder passages of the chapter, and it is worth noticing that he describes what men honour, not what they should.

Then the commonwealth's own honours, and his best scene. “The King of Persia, Honoured Mordecay, when he appointed he should be conducted through the streets in the Kings Garment, upon one of the Kings Horses, with a Crown on his head, and a Prince before him, proclayming, Thus shall it be done to him that the King will honour”. (It is from the Book of Esther.) Another King of Persia, to a man who asked to wear one of the royal robes, gave leave “but with this addition, that he should weare it as the Kings foole; and then it was Dishonour.” The same robe, the same horse. What made the difference was what the sovereign meant by it, and Hobbes draws the moral: “So that of Civill Honour, the Fountain is in the person of the Common-wealth, and dependeth on the Will of the Soveraigne”.

That is dignity, and the lab shows the difference between it and worth. The same four men are here, with the same buyers. Now the commonwealth sets each man a dignity: 40 for the general, who has been made a duke; 34 for the judge; 10 for the orator, who holds no office; 15 for the merchant. These are invented figures, as the prices were. The titles preset puts them beside the three prices of each man. The general's dignity is above every buyer's price for him; the judge's is above two and equal to the third, the peace buyer's 34; the orator's and the merchant's are below. Everyone's dignity but the judge's differs from all three prices.

The raised-orator preset has the commonwealth raise the orator to 30. That equals what the faction would give for him, and is above the war buyer's 19 and the peace buyer's 28. The market preset sets every man's dignity at what the peace buyer would pay, 20, 34, 28 and 24, and nothing then differs from that buyer's price. Hobbes is not saying the commonwealth should do either. He says dignity is an act of the sovereign, so it can fall anywhere.

One word is left, and Hobbes keeps it apart from the others, in the short passage that closes the chapter. “WORTHINESSE, is a thing different from the worth, or value of a man; and also from his merit, or desert”. It is fitness: he is worthiest to be a commander who is best fitted with the qualities the command requires. A man may be worthy of riches or office and yet have no claim to it, and then he cannot be said to merit it, “For Merit, praesupposeth a right, and that the thing deserved is due by promise”, a matter Hobbes defers to the chapters on contracts. So there are four things: what a buyer would pay, what the sovereign has declared, what the man is fit for, and what he is owed. A single table cannot show the last two, and the lab does not try.

The objection from the last lesson comes back here. Kant objects to worth as price: to price a person is to treat as replaceable what has no equivalent, and what a person has instead is a dignity that no price or title can add to or take away. Hobbes uses the word for nearly the reverse, a public valuation that the sovereign can raise or lower at will. The reply open to him, and the one his chapter bears out, is that he describes how men value one another, and says that honour consists only in the opinion of power; he does not say how they ought to. Whether a person can be priced at all is the question between them, and the lab cannot answer it.

Chapter 10 ends there. The next lesson, The Difference of Manners, moves to Chapter 11, where Hobbes asks what all this reckoning of power does to the desires of the men who do it.

Comparing dignity with price

Four steps, starting from prices already computed.

  1. Price each man for each buyer. Do the sum of the last lesson: need times amount for each power, added.
  2. Write the dignity the commonwealth sets. One number for each man. It comes from the sovereign's office or title, not from any buyer's need.
  3. Compare, buyer by buyer. For each man and each buyer, say whether the dignity is above, equal to or below the price.
  4. Find the men the commonwealth values differently from everyone. These are the men whose dignity equals none of the buyers' prices. Their public worth is an act of the sovereign, not a reflection of any market.

Common mistakes

1
Treating honour as a verdict on a man's goodnessHobbes says honour “consisteth onely in the opinion of Power”, and whether an action is just or unjust does not alter it. The piracy and the adulteries he lists were honoured. He is describing what men honour and why; the chapter does not say that they are right to.
2
Merging dignity with worthWorth is what buyers would give, which differs from buyer to buyer. Dignity is one figure set by the commonwealth and it can sit anywhere against the prices. In the titles preset the orator's dignity of 10 is below all three buyers' prices, while the general's 40 is above all three.
3
Reading worthiness as a high priceWorthiness is fitness for a particular charge. A man may be worthy of an office and priced low by every buyer, or priced high and unfit for it. Hobbes takes care to say that it differs from worth and from merit.

Before you go

Say each idea in your own words, then open it to check.

Honour

“The manifestation of the Value we set on one another” is honouring and dishonouring. To value a man highly is to honour him, and to value him low is to dishonour him, measured against the rate he sets on himself. Obeying him, praying to him and giving him great gifts are all honours, because each is a sign that he is thought to have power.

Dignity

The public worth of a man is the value the commonwealth sets on him, and men call it dignity. It is shown by offices of command, judicature and public employment, and by names and titles made for distinguishing such value. Its fountain is the sovereign's will.

Worthiness

Worthiness is a different thing from worth and from merit: a particular ability or fitness for what a man is said to be worthy of. A man may be worthy of an office and yet have no right to it, and then he does not merit it.

Summary

The rest of Chapter 10 sorts out four words that ordinary speech runs together: honour, dignity, worth and worthiness. Honouring is the outward sign of a valuation, dignity is the valuation the commonwealth makes by office and title, and worthiness is fitness for a charge. Put a dignity beside the men's prices from the last lesson and see who the commonwealth honours above or below what any buyer would give.
