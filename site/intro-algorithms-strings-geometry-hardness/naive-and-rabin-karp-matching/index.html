Naive and Rabin-Karp String Matching

Sections 32.1 and 32.2: the string-matching problem as finding every valid shift, the naive matcher and its (n − m + 1)·m worst case, and Rabin and Karp's idea of reading each window as a number modulo a prime, rolling it forward in constant time, and checking the characters only when the numbers agree. Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the first two sections of the chapter, and the rest of it keeps all three.

1

A valid shift

The pattern P of length m occurs with shift s in the text T of length n when the m characters of T after position s are exactly P. The shifts run from 0 to n − m, so there are n − m + 1 of them, and the problem is to report every valid one.

2

A rolling hash

Read a window of m characters as an m-digit number in radix d, reduced modulo a prime q. Dropping the leading digit, shifting and adding the next character gives the next window's value in a constant number of arithmetic steps, whatever m is.

3

A spurious hit

A shift whose window has the same residue modulo q as the pattern and different characters. Residues that differ prove a shift invalid; residues that agree prove nothing, so every agreement is checked character by character.

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the count

Read the opening of Chapter 32 and sections 32.1 and 32.2 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the argument in its own words and then runs the rolling hash on a text written for it.

“If q is large enough, then we hope that spurious hits occur infrequently enough that the cost of the extra checking is low.” The word to notice is hope. Section 32.2 of Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein gives an algorithm whose worst case is no better than the naive one and whose ordinary behaviour is far better, and it is careful to say which of those claims is proved. This lesson reads the opening of Chapter 32 and sections 32.1 and 32.2, and then counts the hope on a text of its own.

The chapter states the problem precisely. A text T[1..n] and a pattern P[1..m] are arrays of characters from a finite alphabet Σ. The pattern occurs with shift s when T[s+1..s+m] equals P[1..m], for s between 0 and n − m, and the task is to find every such valid shift. The chapter's opening table sets the four algorithms side by side by preprocessing time and matching time, and the authors fix the notation the later sections need: the prefix and suffix relations on strings, P_k for the first k characters of the pattern, and a cost model in which comparing two strings costs time proportional to one more than the length of their common prefix.

Section 32.1 is short because the naive matcher is. It tries every one of the n − m + 1 shifts and, at each, compares characters until one differs or all m agree. The worst case is (n − m + 1)·m comparisons, and the authors show it is reached: a text of n copies of one letter and a pattern of m copies of it make every shift valid and every comparison necessary. With m near n/2 that is Θ(n²). The section's diagnosis is the one the rest of the chapter answers: the naive matcher throws away what one shift taught it about the text before it tries the next.

Section 32.2 turns the comparison of strings into the comparison of numbers. With decimal digits for characters, a window of m characters is an m-digit number, and the pattern's number p can be computed by Horner's rule in Θ(m) steps. The next window's number follows from the current one by removing the high-order digit, multiplying by the radix and adding the new low-order digit. That is equation (32.1), and it makes all n − m + 1 window values cost Θ(n − m + 1) together. If the numbers could be compared in constant time, matching would be linear.

They cannot, because an m-digit number does not fit in a word. So the authors reduce everything modulo a prime q chosen so that d·q fits in one word, with equation (32.2) as the rolling step and h, the weight of the high-order digit, precomputed as d^(m−1) mod q. Now the residues fit, and the price is one-sided: different residues prove the window differs from the pattern, but equal residues do not prove it equal. Procedure RABIN-KARP-MATCHER therefore checks the characters of every window whose residue matches, and its loop invariant is simply that the residue in hand is the current window's.

The analysis comes in two strengths, and the section keeps them apart. The worst case is still Θ((n − m + 1)·m), because a text in which every shift is valid forces a full check at every shift. The expected case rests on a heuristic assumption, which the authors label as one: that reduction modulo q behaves like a random map, so that a given window collides with probability about 1/q. Then the spurious hits number about n/q, and with few valid shifts and q larger than m the expected matching time is O(n + m). You will meet the same rolling hash, and the sweep over moduli used below, in the Algorithms lesson A Rolling Hash, and a Collision You Can Read.

Now the count. The lab hashes every window of a text in a chosen base and modulus and prints the windows, the hashes equal to the pattern's, the real matches beside the matches found by comparing every shift directly, the collisions, the characters compared while verifying (the lab charges m for each check, as the book's cost model does for a full match), the characters spent on collisions, and how many of the 46 primes under 200 produce at least one collision on the same text, with the largest of them.

Its presets are the lab's own. english searches a line of prose for a three-letter pattern at modulus 41: 41 windows, 7 equal hashes, 4 real matches, 3 collisions and 9 characters wasted; 18 of the 46 primes collide, the largest 137. clean is the same line at modulus 1000003, with 0 collisions. dna finds 3 collisions at modulus 13 that waste 21 characters; binary finds 4 at modulus 11, and only 6 of the 46 primes collide on it, 17 the largest; abra finds 6 real matches and 4 collisions at modulus 13.

Then type the lesson's own instance: the text a banana and a bandana in a cabana, the pattern ana, base 256. The text has 34 characters, so there are 32 windows and the naive bound is 32 · 3 = 96 comparisons. The valid shifts are 3, 5, 19 and 31, and two of them overlap inside banana. At modulus 11 the lab finds 5 equal hashes: the 4 matches and 1 collision, the window at shift 10, an n, a d and a space. Verifying costs 15 characters, 3 of them wasted. At modulus 17 the collisions vanish; on this text 17 of the 46 primes under 200 collide, the largest 181.

Now set the modulus to 13 and the count jumps to 8 spurious hits, 36 characters compared and 24 wasted. Nothing about 13 is small next to m = 3; the cause is in the text. That is exactly the gap the book's hedge leaves: the n/q estimate assumes the residues of this text's windows look random, and on one text, at one modulus, they need not. The count is evidence about this text; the expected bound is a statement under an assumption the count cannot test.

Method

Running Rabin-Karp by hand

Four steps settle one window, and the fifth is on a text of your own.

1

Count the shifts first

There are n − m + 1 of them, from 0 to n − m. The naive bound is that number times m; write it down before anything else.

2

Hash the pattern by Horner's rule

Reduce the radix modulo q once, then take each character in turn: multiply the running residue by the radix, add the character, reduce. Keep the result: every window is compared with it.

3

Roll one window forward

Subtract the leading character times h = d^(m−1) mod q, multiply by d, add the next character, reduce. Then hash the new window from scratch and check that the two agree.

4

Check every equal residue

Compare the m characters of each window whose residue equals the pattern's. Count the matches and the spurious hits separately.

5

Do it on your own text

Paste a paragraph of your own into the lab with a short pattern, and find a prime under 200 at which it collides and a larger one at which it does not. Write down both, and one sentence on why the larger prime is not guaranteed clean.

One spurious hit at modulus 11, checked by hand

Base 256, modulus 11, pattern ana, in the lesson's text. Character codes: a is 97, n is 110, d is 100, the space is 32.

256 mod 11 = 3, h = 256² mod 11 = 9 a ≡ 9, n ≡ 0, d ≡ 1, space ≡ 10 pattern ana: (9·3 + 0)·3 + 9 = 90 ≡ 2 window and, shift 9: (9·3 + 0)·3 + 1 ≡ 5 roll to shift 10: (5 − 9·9)·3 + 10 = (5 − 4)·3 + 10 = 13 ≡ 2 window nd space, from scratch: 1·3 + 10 ≡ 2 residues agree, characters differ

The rolled value and the value computed from scratch agree, which is the loop invariant of the matcher holding at shift 10. The residue 2 equals the pattern's, so the matcher compares the three characters and finds n, d and a space where it wanted a, n and a: a spurious hit, the only one at this modulus. The four valid shifts cost 12 character comparisons to confirm and the one collision 3 more, the 15 the lab prints.

Common mistakes

1
Taking a matching residue for a matchThe residue is a fingerprint of the window, and fingerprints collide. Section 32.2 is explicit that equal residues only make a shift worth checking. On the lesson's text at modulus 11, the window at shift 10 has residue 2, the pattern's, and holds n, d and a space. A matcher that reported it would be wrong.
2
Reading the expected time as a guaranteeThe O(n + m) expected matching time depends on a heuristic assumption about the residues and on there being few valid shifts. The worst case is still that of the naive matcher. A text of one repeated letter, searched for a run of the same letter, makes every shift valid and every check full.
3
Expecting a larger modulus always to helpThe n/q estimate is about the average over moduli. On one text the collisions depend on the text: the lesson's text collides at 13 and not at 17, at 23 and not at 19, and at 181, while 47 is clean. A prime that works on one input is not shown to work on the next.
