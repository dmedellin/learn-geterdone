Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ideas carry these sections. The first answers every question in 33.1, and the third is a technique the rest of the chapter reuses.

1

The cross product

For vectors p1 = (x1, y1) and p2 = (x2, y2), p1 × p2 = x1·y2 − x2·y1, the signed area of the parallelogram they span. Its sign says whether p1 is clockwise or counterclockwise from p2 about the origin; zero means the two are colinear.

2

Straddling

A segment straddles a line when its two endpoints lie strictly on opposite sides of it. Two segments meet exactly when each straddles the line through the other, or when an endpoint of one lies on the other, the boundary case.

3

A sweep

An imaginary vertical line moves across the plane from left to right, stopping only at event points such as segment endpoints. The algorithm keeps, in a balanced search tree, the order of the segments the line currently cuts, and tests only segments that become neighbours in that order.

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the count

Read the opening of Chapter 33 and sections 33.1 and 33.2 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out the argument in its own words and then computes the four orientations for segments written for it.

“We need neither division nor trigonometric functions, both of which can be computationally expensive and prone to problems with round-off error.” That is the promise section 33.1 makes, and it keeps it: every question the section asks is answered with additions, subtractions, multiplications and comparisons. This lesson reads the opening of Chapter 33 and sections 33.1 and 33.2, and then does the arithmetic on segments of its own.

The chapter opens by saying what computational geometry is, with its inputs given as sets of points with real coordinates, and works in the plane throughout. Section 33.1 asks three questions: given two directed segments from a common point, is one clockwise from the other; given two consecutive segments, do they turn left or right where they join; and do two segments intersect. Each takes O(1) time. The authors contrast their method with the obvious one, solving for the intersection of the two lines, which divides and is fragile when the segments are nearly parallel.

All three answers come from the cross product, which the section defines as the determinant x1·y2 − x2·y1 and reads as the signed area of a parallelogram. Positive means p1 is clockwise from p2 about the origin; negative, counterclockwise; zero, colinear. To ask about two segments from a common point p0, translate so that p0 is the origin and take the cross product of the differences. To ask which way the path p0, p1, p2 turns at p1, take (p2 − p0) × (p1 − p0): negative is a left turn, positive a right turn, zero means the three points are colinear.

The intersection test combines four of these. SEGMENTS-INTERSECT computes, with a helper called DIRECTION, the orientation of each endpoint of one segment relative to the other segment. If the first pair of values have opposite signs and so do the second pair, each segment straddles the line through the other and they cross. If some value is zero, that endpoint is colinear with the other segment, and ON-SEGMENT finishes the case by checking whether the point lies within the segment's bounding box. Otherwise they do not meet.

Section 33.2 asks a larger question: do any two of n segments intersect? Testing every pair costs Θ(n²). The sweep costs O(n lg n). It assumes no segment is vertical and no three meet at one point, sorts the 2n endpoints from left to right, and keeps the segments the sweep line currently cuts in a total preorder by height, stored in a red-black tree. At a left endpoint it inserts the segment and tests it against the segments just above and below; at a right endpoint it tests those two neighbours against each other and deletes the segment. The correctness argument is that the leftmost intersection, if there is one, is between two segments that become neighbours at some event before the sweep passes it.

That procedure, ANY-SEGMENTS-INTERSECT, answers yes or no. It does not find every intersection, and the section says so; a report of all k crossings needs more work. The Algorithms lesson Do Two Segments Meet covers the pairwise test with the same lab used here, and The Sweep Line and the Work It Does Not Save runs a sweep.

Now the lab. Type four points, p1 and p2 for one segment and q1 and q2 for the other, and it prints the four orientations, whether the segments cross properly (in their interiors), whether they touch, whether their bounding boxes overlap, the verdict from the four signs, and a second verdict from solving the two parametric equations exactly, with whether the two routes agree. One thing to know before you read its table: the lab's orientation orient(a, b, c) is (b − a) × (c − a), positive for a left turn, which is the negative of the book's DIRECTION(a, b, c). The verdicts do not care; the signs are reversed.

The lab's presets cover the cases the boundary rule exists for. proper is an ordinary crossing: proper yes, touching no, they meet. touch has one segment end on the other: proper no, touching yes. overlap is two colinear segments sharing a stretch, shared two segments with only an endpoint in common, and point a segment that is a single point lying on the other; all three touch and meet. apart is colinear with a gap and parallel never meets; in both the boxes do not overlap and the verdict is that they miss. The two routes agree on every preset.

Then type the lesson's segments: p1 = (1, 1), p2 = (7, 5), q1 = (2, 6), q2 = (6, 0). The lab prints orientations −26, 26, 26 and −26: each pair has opposite signs, so the crossing is proper. Now move q1 to (4, 3) and q2 to (4, 8). The third orientation becomes 0, because (4, 3) lies on the line through p1 and p2, halfway along. The sign test alone decides nothing; the boundary case finds the point inside the segment, and the lab reports touching yes, proper no, they meet.

Method

Deciding whether two segments meet

Four steps follow SEGMENTS-INTERSECT; the fifth is a pair of your own.

1

Compute the two orientations against the second segment

With the book's DIRECTION, d1 = (p1 − p3) × (p4 − p3) and d2 = (p2 − p3) × (p4 − p3). Opposite signs mean p1p2 straddles the line through p3p4.

2

Compute the two against the first segment

d3 = (p3 − p1) × (p2 − p1) and d4 = (p4 − p1) × (p2 − p1). Opposite signs mean p3p4 straddles the line through p1p2.

3

Decide the general case

If both pairs have opposite signs, the segments cross. If neither pair has a zero and either pair has equal signs, they do not.

4

Finish any zero with the bounding box

For each zero, the endpoint is colinear with the other segment: it lies on that segment exactly when its coordinates fall between the segment's endpoints in both x and y.

5

Build a case of your own

Choose two segments that share only an endpoint, compute the four values, and say which of them are zero. Type the points into the lab and check that it reports touching yes and proper no.

Common mistakes

1
Testing only for straddlingIf the four values are all nonzero, the straddle test decides. When one is zero it decides nothing: on the lesson's second pair, (4, 3) lies on the line through (1, 1) and (7, 5) and the segments touch, while the lab's apart preset has every value zero and the segments miss. The boundary case is half of the procedure, not a footnote.
2
Mixing the two sign conventionsThe book's DIRECTION(a, b, c) is (c − a) × (b − a) and the lab's orient(a, b, c) is (b − a) × (c − a); one is the negative of the other. A rule such as positive is a left turn is true in one convention and false in the other. Fix one convention before reading any sign, and note that the intersection verdict is unchanged because it compares signs in pairs.
3
Solving for the intersection pointWriting each segment as y = m·x + b and solving needs division and fails for vertical segments. Section 33.1 avoids it on purpose: the four cross products of integer coordinates are exact integers. The lab's second route does solve the equations, exactly and without dividing, and agrees.
