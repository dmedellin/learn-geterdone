All-Pairs Shortest Paths

Chapter 25 answers the shortest-path question for every pair at once. Section 25.1 lets paths grow one edge at a time and finds matrix multiplication inside; Section 25.2's Floyd-Warshall algorithm lets in one interior vertex at a time in Θ(V³); Section 25.3's Johnson reweights so that Dijkstra can run from every vertex. Chapter 25 in seven lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three ways to cut the same problem into subproblems, each giving one of the chapter's algorithms.

1

Paths of at most m edges

Section 25.1's subproblem: the least weight of a path from i to j using at most m edges. Going from m − 1 to m means trying every last edge, which is a matrix product with minimum in place of sum and addition in place of product. With no negative cycle, m = n − 1 gives the distances.

2

Paths through the first k vertices

Floyd-Warshall's subproblem: the least weight of a path from i to j whose interior vertices all lie among vertices 1 to k. Allowing vertex k either does not help or splits the path at k into two paths through the first k − 1 vertices.

3

Reweighting

Johnson's device: give each vertex a number h(v) and replace each arc's weight w by w + h(u) − h(v). Every path from u to v changes by the same h(u) − h(v), so shortest paths stay shortest (Lemma 25.1); with h taken as distances from an added vertex, every new weight is nonnegative.

The material

The chapter's argument first, then the matrix

Read Sections 25.1 to 25.3 in your own copy first. The lesson sets out each argument in its own words, then runs Floyd-Warshall on a graph written for the lesson and reweights it by hand.

“This problem might arise in making a table of distances between all pairs of cities for a road atlas.” Chapter 25 of CLRS wants the whole table at once. The obvious answer runs a single-source algorithm from every vertex: Dijkstra |V| times if no weight is negative, O(V³) with an array, or Bellman-Ford |V| times otherwise, O(V²E), which is O(V⁴) on a dense graph. The chapter does better, and most of it works on an adjacency matrix rather than lists.

Section 25.1 builds a dynamic program from the subpaths lemma. Let the entry in row i and column j of a matrix L(m) be the least weight of a path from i to j with at most m edges. L(1) is the weight matrix itself. A path of at most m edges is a path of at most m − 1 edges followed by one more edge, so L(m) comes from L(m − 1) by trying every possible last edge. Written out, that step is a matrix product in which minimum replaces sum and addition replaces multiplication. With no negative cycle, shortest paths are simple, so L(n − 1) is the answer.

Computing L(2), L(3), up to L(n − 1) one product at a time costs Θ(n⁴). But the product is associative, and any L(m) with m at least n − 1 is the same answer, so the section squares instead: L(2), L(4), L(8), until the exponent passes n − 1. That is ⌈lg(n − 1)⌉ products, Θ(n³ lg n) in all. The authors' point is the shape, shortest paths as repeated matrix multiplication, more than the bound.

Section 25.2 changes the subproblem and wins the logarithm back. Let D(k) hold, for each pair, the least weight of a path whose interior vertices all come from vertices 1 to k. A best such path either avoids vertex k, so its weight is the entry of D(k − 1), or passes through k once and splits there into two paths that use only vertices 1 to k − 1. Each entry of D(k) is the smaller of those two. FLOYD-WARSHALL computes D(1) up to D(n) with three nested loops, k outermost, Θ(n³) in all, and a predecessor matrix alongside rebuilds the paths. The same scheme with logical or and and computes a graph's transitive closure.

Section 25.3's Johnson's algorithm suits sparse graphs. Add a new vertex with a 0-weight arc to every vertex, run Bellman-Ford from it, and stop if it finds a negative cycle; otherwise call the distances h. Replace each arc's weight w from u to v by w + h(u) − h(v). The triangle inequality makes every new weight nonnegative, and Lemma 25.1 shows every path between the same two vertices shifts by the same amount, so shortest paths are unchanged. Then Dijkstra from every vertex, with a Fibonacci heap, gives O(V² lg V + VE) in all.

Through vertex 3, row 4 fills in, 4 to 1 at −1 and 4 to 2 at 2, and 2 to 1 becomes 2. Through vertex 4, three cells improve: 1 to 3 to 6, 2 to 1 to 1 and 2 to 3 to 3. The final rows are 0, 3, 6, 5; 1, 0, 3, 2; −2, 1, 0, 3; and −1, 2, 1, 0. The entry for 4 to 2 first appears at k = 3, because its path needs both 3 and 1 inside.

Reweight the same graph by hand as Johnson would. From an added vertex with 0-weight arcs to all four, Bellman-Ford gives h = (−2, 0, 0, 0): only vertex 1 gains, through 3. The new weights are 1 for 1 → 2, 4 for 2 → 3, 0 for 3 → 1, 5 for 1 → 4, 1 for 4 → 3 and 2 for 2 → 4, all nonnegative. The shortest reweighted route from 1 to 3 is 1 → 2 → 4 → 3 at 4, and undoing the shift, 4 − h(1) + h(3) = 6, matches the matrix.

The subproblem is the whole content: after step k, the cell D[i][j] is the best route from i to j whose interior vertices are all among the first k. That is why the k loop is the outer one — write the loops in another order and you compute something else that often looks right. Step k below and watch which cells move.

Method

Running Floyd-Warshall by hand

Five steps, the last on a graph of your own.

1

Write the weight matrix

Row i, column j holds the weight of the arc from i to j, 0 on the diagonal and infinity where there is no arc. This is D(0).

2

Allow vertex k

For each pair, compare the current entry with the entry from i to k plus the entry from k to j, and keep the smaller. Note which cells move.

3

Repeat for k from 1 to n

Keep k as the outer loop: every entry must be finished for vertices 1 to k − 1 before vertex k is allowed.

4

Read the diagonal

A negative entry on the diagonal means a negative cycle through that vertex, and the other entries are not distances.

5

Do it on a graph of your own

Write the costs between four or five places of your own as arcs, one of them negative such as a refund, and type them into the lab. Step k from 1 to n and note which entries move at each step.

Common mistakes

1
Reading Floyd-Warshall's k as the number of edges on a pathSection 25.1's index m counts edges; Section 25.2's k bounds the labels of interior vertices, however many edges the path has. On the lesson's graph the entry from 4 to 2 first appears at k = 3 with a path of three edges, and the entry from 3 to 2 at k = 1 with two.
2
Treating the loop order as a detailWith the pair loops outside and k inside, each entry would be computed once with whatever its neighbours held at the time, which is not the recurrence. The lab's hint makes the same point: the outer k loop is the subproblem order, and putting it anywhere else computes something that often looks right.
3
Thinking reweighting can add one constant to every arcAdding the same constant to every arc does make weights nonnegative, but it penalises a path once per edge, so paths with more edges lose and the shortest paths change. Johnson's shift w + h(u) − h(v) moves every path between the same two ends by the same amount, which is why Lemma 25.1 can say nothing changes.
