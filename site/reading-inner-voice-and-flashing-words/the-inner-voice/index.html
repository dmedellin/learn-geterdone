The Inner Voice

Most readers hear the words as they read silently, and the speed-reading tradition calls that voice a brake held at speaking pace. Read one easy story aloud and another silently, compute the two rates and their ratio, and see what the ratio says about the advice to get rid of the voice.

Two rates and a ratio

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The claim to test is simple: if the inner voice held you to the pace of speech, you could not read silently faster than you read aloud.

1

Two readings, one ratio

You read one passage aloud and time it, then a second silently and time it. Dividing the silent rate by the aloud rate gives a single number: how many times faster your eyes and your inner voice go than your mouth.

2

The published averages

Brysbaert (2019) pooled the studies of adult reading rates in English: about 238 words a minute for silent reading of non-fiction, 260 for fiction, and about 183 for reading aloud. They are averages over many readers, not targets.

3

Inner speech is not speaking

The voice you hear when you read silently is not your mouth moving quietly. Inner speech is not full articulation, so it is not tied to the pace at which lips, tongue and breath can make the sounds (Rayner and colleagues, 2016).

The material

What the ratio says about the voice

One reading aloud, one silently, and a division. The argument is in what the division can come out as.

The lab below has two readings of two easy stories. Read the first, The Bell at Six, aloud at a natural speaking pace — a whisper is enough — and press Done when you finish speaking; it has no questions. Read the second, Bread in the Dark, silently at your usual pace, then answer its six questions. The lab divides the second rate by the first.

The advice you are testing is old and widely repeated in speed-reading courses and books: the voice in your head is a habit left over from learning to read aloud, it holds silent reading to the pace of speech, and a fast reader must learn to stop hearing the words. Readers who practise this often say the voice grows fainter as they speed up, and the experience is real enough to take seriously.

The claim makes a prediction you can check in five minutes. If the voice held you to speaking pace, your silent rate could not be much above your aloud rate. The published averages already say otherwise: Brysbaert's review puts silent reading of non-fiction at about 238 words a minute and reading aloud at about 183. On average, adults read silently about 1.3 times as fast as they read aloud.

Definition · The ratio

The silent-to-aloud ratio is the silent rate divided by the aloud rate, each in words a minute. Above 1, you read silently faster than you speak; the lab prints it as a fraction and a percentage, so 1.3 reads as about 130%.

The preset typical is those averages as one reader: 183 words aloud in 60 seconds, which is exactly what the “I can’t read aloud here” button writes, and 476 words silently in 120 seconds with five of six questions right. The tiles read 183 wpm and 238 wpm, and the ratio 238/183, about 130.1%.

The preset fast-silent is a reader who read the whole of The Bell at Six, 477 words, aloud in 159 seconds, which is 180 wpm, and the 453 words of Bread in the Dark silently in 84.9375 seconds, which is 320 wpm. The ratio is 16/9, about 177.8%: silent reading nearly 1.8 times the speed of speech, by a reader who may well still hear every word.

Now read your own pair. A ratio above 1 means you already read silently faster than you can speak, and so whatever your inner voice is doing, it is not holding you to the pace of your mouth. A ratio near 1 or below is unusual for easy stories; check that you read the silent passage at your usual pace and not carefully. Either way, if your result goes the other way from Brysbaert's averages, you have not refuted the review, and the review has not refuted you: it describes many readers, and this is one pair of your readings.

Only the silent reading has questions, and the lab compares rates, not effective rates. The aloud reading is there to time your speaking pace, and a reader performing a text aloud is attending to the performance as much as to the meaning. The silent side still prints its comprehension and effective rate, so you can see that the rate was a reading and not a skim; and because it is logged as a test, the verdict tile sets its rate against your baseline range, as it does for any test reading. Both stories are from the easy band, so expect that verdict to flatter you; your log records the band, and the medians that later lessons set against your baselines leave this test out.

If you cannot read aloud where you are, press “I can’t read aloud here”. The lab writes Brysbaert's oral average, 183 words in 60 seconds, into the aloud side, labels it as a published figure, and logs nothing for that side. Your ratio is then your silent rate against other people's speaking rate, which is a weaker comparison; do the aloud reading on another day if you can.

So the voice is not a brake set at speaking speed. Whether it does useful work, and what happens to your reading when it is kept busy, is a different question, and the next lesson, Silencing the Inner Voice, tests it with a matched pair of passages.

Method

Measuring your speaking and silent rates

To repeat the comparison on another day, use two fresh passages of the same difficulty, and these steps in this order.

1

Choose two fresh, easy passages

Two texts you have not read, of about the same length and difficulty. Easy texts keep the aloud reading from turning into a struggle with pronunciation.

2

Read the first aloud at a natural pace

As you would read to someone across a table, not a performance and not a race. A whisper is enough. Time it from the first word to the last.

3

Read the second silently at your usual pace

Time it, then answer questions on it with the text out of sight, so that the silent rate is a reading and not a skim.

4

Divide the silent rate by the aloud rate

Each rate is words × 60 / seconds. A ratio above 1 means your silent reading already outruns your speech.

5

Then today's Daily Drill

Open The Daily Drill and run today's session: the next pool passage with the pacer at your hold target, or a test at natural pace when the panel says one is due. A comparison like this one is a separate reading, never a replacement for that session.

The published averages as a ratio

Brysbaert's averages for adult readers of English: about 238 words a minute silently, for non-fiction, and about 183 aloud.

silent rate = 238 words a minute aloud rate = 183 words a minute ratio = 238 / 183 ≈ 1.3

The average reader reads silently about 1.3 times as fast as they read aloud. The advice to eliminate the voice assumes the voice sets the speed; the ratio shows that silent reading is already past the speed of speech.

Common mistakes

1
The inner voice limits you to speaking speed, so it must goSilent reading is already faster than reading aloud on average: about 238 words a minute against 183 in Brysbaert's review. Inner speech is not full articulation, so it is not held to the speed of the mouth. A voice that accompanies reading at 1.3 times the pace of reading aloud is not a brake set at speaking pace.
2
Reading the published averages as targets238 and 183 are averages over many readers and many texts. A reader whose ratio is 1.5 or 1.1 is not ahead or behind; the ratio only has to be above 1 for the argument to go through for that reader.
3
Treating one pair as a fact about yourselfThe two readings were on different stories, in one order, on one day. Easy stories, a nervous aloud reading or a careful silent one all move the ratio. It is one measurement of each rate, and a second pair on another day will differ.
