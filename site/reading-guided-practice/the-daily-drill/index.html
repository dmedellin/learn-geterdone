The Daily Drill

The Daily Drill is the routine the rest of the Subject keeps going: one unread passage from the practice pool, read with the pacer at your hold target and scored, ten to fifteen minutes a day. Every fifth session is a test at natural pace with no pacer. The target rises by a twentieth after three holds in a row and falls by a twentieth after two misses, and the lab reads the next target and the next test from your log.

The routine in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

A routine has to be short enough to keep and exact enough to read back. Three ideas make this one both.

1

A session is one passage

The pool holds 18 passages, rising from easy to hard, served in order and never repeated; a test takes the next standard one. One passage behind the pacer and its six questions is a session, and ten to fifteen minutes a day is the routine.

2

The target moves by a twentieth

Three holds in a row at a target, four or more right each time, raise it by a twentieth: 288 becomes 302.4. Two misses in a row lower it by a twentieth: 288 becomes 273.6. Anything else keeps it where it is.

3

Tests are the readings that count

Every fifth session is a test: a standard pool passage at natural pace, with no pacer. Paced readings are practice. Only tests and baselines go into the verdicts, because only they measure your own pace.

The material

The routine

The numbers are the Subject's, chosen to be easy to keep; the reasons for them follow the list.

Today's session is in the lab below. Type your hold target from Choosing a Target Pace into the target box, tick the pacer box, and press Start: the lab serves the next pool passage you have not read. After Done it asks whether your attention left the text, then the six questions.

  1. Ten to fifteen minutes a day: one pool passage, read with the pacer at your hold target, then its questions. At most two timed readings in a sitting.
  2. Every fifth session, a test: tick the test box, and Start serves the next standard passage of the pool, to read at your natural pace with no pacer. The lab says when a test is due.
  3. Three holds in a row at a target, four or more right each time, raise the target by about 5%, a twentieth. Two misses in a row lower it by the same.
  4. If your attention left the text, make the next session shorter, not the pacer slower. When the pool reaches the hard passages, find your hold again; it will be lower.

Each rule is there for a reason you have already met. A session is short because a timed reading done while tired or interrupted measures the tiredness. The test has no pacer because a paced rate is the target's, not yours; only an unpaced reading can show whether your own pace has moved, and the rule from One Reading Is One Measurement reads the median of the last three tests. The target moves by a twentieth so that one good day or one bad one cannot move it, and three holds against two misses makes it a little harder to go up than to come down.

The lab reads three figures from your log. The hold target is the fastest target at which your last three paced practice readings at that target all scored four or more; until you have three at one target it says “not yet”. The next target applies the rule to your last readings at your latest target. The next test counts the sessions you have read on this page since your last test reading of a standard passage; four of them make the fifth a test. Readings in other lessons, the three rungs of your ladder among them, are not sessions, and a scan, a skim or a test on an easy or hard passage does not restart the count; a test reading of a standard passage in a later lesson's warm-up does.

Those tests always fall on standard passages. The pool starts with eight easy passages, and your baselines were standard ones, so a test on an easy passage would come out faster for that reason alone. When the test box is ticked, Start skips ahead to the next unread standard passage, from “The Engineer of the Pond” on. Your log also records each passage's band, and the verdicts and the curve set only tests of standard passages against your baselines.

The preset three-holds is a reader with three paced readings at 288 that scored 5, 4 and 5 of six. All three held, so the hold target is 288 and the next target is 288 × 21/20 = 302.4. They have had three practice sessions since their last test, so the test is one session away.

The preset two-misses is a reader whose last two paced readings at 288 scored 3 of six each. There is no hold yet at 288, and two misses in a row bring the target down to 288 × 19/20 = 273.6. Two practice sessions since the last test leave two more before the next.

The preset test-due is a reader with four paced readings at 288 scoring 4, 3, 5 and 4. The last three are not all holds and the last two are not both misses, so the target stays at 288 and there is no hold yet. Four practice sessions since the last test make today's session a test: no pacer, natural pace.

What the research supports, and what it does not

Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) gave the name deliberate practice to work of this kind: effortful, aimed at the edge of what you can do, with immediate feedback. Their study was of musicians, and how much of expert performance such practice explains is disputed. The routine borrows the shape, not a promise. On reading itself, Rayner and colleagues' 2016 review concluded that readers are unlikely to double or triple their rate while understanding as well as at their normal pace. What the routine guarantees is a log in which your tests can show what happened, whichever way it goes.

If the pool runs out, the lab says so; keep going with your own text: open “Or paste a text of your own” above the Start button and paste it there. Your own text has no questions, so it is logged as practice and its comprehension is not scored, and it cannot be a test: it keeps the habit going, while the tests, which need a standard passage with questions, come from the pool while its standard passages last and afterwards from the readings the later lessons log as tests.

From here on, every lesson in the Subject ends with this session. The next lesson, Sprints Above Comfort, tries one of the tradition's ways of pushing the pace, and judges it by the same rule.

Method

Each day's session

Ten to fifteen minutes, in this order. The lab keeps the count; you keep the habit.

1

Open this page and read the two tiles

The next pacer target and the next test come from your log. If a test is due, tick the test box and leave the pacer off.

2

Type the target and start

Press “Use my next target” (or type it into the target box), tick the pacer box, and press Start. Keep your eyes on the lit line until it ends.

3

Tick the attention box honestly, then answer

If your attention left the text, say so. It goes into your log, and it means tomorrow's session should be shorter, not the pacer slower.

4

Stop after two timed readings

One passage is a session. If you have time for a second, read it; after two, stop. A third measures your tiredness.

5

Then today's Daily Drill, every day

Come back to this lesson, The Daily Drill, tomorrow, and on every day you can. Every later lesson ends by sending you here.

Common mistakes

1
Reading as much as possible, as fast as possibleMore is not better when the reading is past the point where you understand it or after your attention has gone. Short, regular sessions at a pace you still understand, with regular tests, are the routine; a session's length is set by your attention, not by ambition.
2
Counting paced readings as progressA paced rate is the target's. It goes up when you raise the target, whether or not your own pace has moved. Only the tests, read without the pacer, can show that, and only the median of the last three can show it reliably.
3
Raising the target after one good dayOne reading with six right is one reading. The target rises after three holds in a row, so that the pace moves up only when it has been held more than once.

Note: The routine is the Subject's, not a finding: the twentieth, the three and the two, and the test every fifth session were chosen to be easy to keep and hard to fool.
