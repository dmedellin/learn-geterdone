Capabilities to Drive Improvement, and the Stats

Appendix A lists the 24 capabilities and says where each is read; Appendix B lists the statistical findings by category. Read them as references, with attention to the verbs, and then profile an invented organization's six teams by category to find where a capability is least often in place.

The appendices in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

Three terms carry the lesson. The first is Appendix A's, the second Appendix B's, the third the lab's.

1

Capability

A practice or condition that the survey analysis tied to better software delivery, at a level of statistical significance. Appendix A counts 24, in five categories: continuous delivery, architecture, product and process, lean management and monitoring, and cultural.

2

Correlation and prediction

Appendix B's two terms for how the findings are stated. A correlation says two variables move together; a prediction, which the authors call inferential, says one construct affects another on a theory tested against the data. Neither is a proof of cause.

3

Category profile

For a team or an organization, how many of each category's capabilities are in place. It carries information that the single total hides.

The material

A list, a reference, and the verbs in it

Skim Appendix A and Appendix B in your own copy first; they are references, not arguments. The lesson reads them for how the claims are worded, and then uses Appendix A's list as the lab's frame.

“Correlation looks at how closely two variables move together (or don’t) but it doesn’t tell us if one variable’s movement predicts or causes the movement in another variable.” Appendix B begins by saying this to the reader, as a reminder, and then lists what the research found, organized by category. This lesson takes the two appendices that end the book. Appendix A is the list of capabilities, each followed by the chapter that treats it. Appendix B is the statistical summary. Appendix C, on the methods, is read in Surveys, Data and Statistical Methods.

Appendix A says the research found 24 key capabilities that drive better software delivery performance, at a level of statistical significance, and sorts them into five categories, in no particular order within each. Continuous delivery has eight, all read in Chapter 4: version control, deployment automation, continuous integration, trunk-based development, test automation, test data management, shifting security left and continuous delivery itself. Architecture has two, from Chapter 5: a loosely coupled architecture and empowered teams. Product and process has four, from Chapter 8. Lean management and monitoring has five, four from Chapter 7 and one from Chapter 13. Cultural has five, from Chapters 3, 5, 10 and 11.

The Quick Reference at the front of the book is the short form of the same list, and says that Appendix A is the detailed guide; the appendix is best used the way its authors call it, as a handy list. A few of the entries carry qualifications that are easy to lose, such as that peer review does better than external change approval boards, and that a team should keep its test data to the least it needs. The lesson does not reproduce the paragraphs. The lab's names for the capabilities are short labels of its own for the book's 24, each written as one hyphenated word because the kit takes names without spaces.

Appendix B states the same findings the earlier chapters reported, by category: organizational performance, software delivery performance, quality, burnout and deployment pain, technical capabilities, architecture, lean management, lean product management, culture, identity, employee Net Promoter Score and job satisfaction, leadership, diversity, and a miscellany. It opens by defining its verbs. A correlation is two things moving together. A prediction, which the authors say is inferential prediction, is a stronger statement, that one construct affects another, used when an experiment cannot be run and tested with regression. Read each line by its verb: 'is correlated with' is one claim and 'predicts' another, and Chapter 2 tells the reader that 'drives', 'affects' and 'impacts' are other words for the second, which is still not a randomized test.

Three kinds of line are worth reading with care. Some state a result across years: the survey's high performers were twice as likely as low performers to exceed organizational performance goals, which the previous lesson recomputed. Some state a result for one year and sample, and say so. A follow-up to the 2014 data used respondents who named their employer, just over a thousand of them in 355 companies. For those at publicly traded companies the high performers' market capitalization had grown 50 percent more over three years; the appendix adds that later years could not repeat the analysis, the data being too few. And some describe who answered: women were 5 percent of respondents in 2015, then 6 percent, then 6.5 percent in 2017, which is the kind of fact to weigh when Fowler's foreword wonders how the sample reflects the IT world.

The appendix is also candid in a way that a list of results rarely is. It reports that teams with the strongest transformational leaders were no more likely, and perhaps less, to be high performers, and, for software delivery performance, that the construct is made from three of the four measures, with change fail rate highly correlated with it and left out. Those are reasons not to read the capabilities as a recipe, or the groups as a ladder.

Now the lab, which uses Appendix A as its frame. Six teams of one invented organization each list the capabilities they have in place. On the preset six-teams the teams have 18, 14, 8, 13, 7 and 11 of the 24, 71 of the 144 possible. By category the organization has 26 of 48 continuous delivery capabilities in place, 5 of 12 in architecture, 10 of 24 in product and process, 11 of 30 in lean management and monitoring and 19 of 30 in cultural ones. Three categories are below half, lean management and monitoring lowest at 36.7 percent. One capability, team experimentation, is in place in none of the six teams.

The lab's other view is a team. On the preset same-total two teams have 13 capabilities each: Orchid, from the previous lesson, and Elm. Orchid is spread: 4 of 8 continuous delivery, 1 of 2 architecture, 2 of 4 product and process, 3 of 5 lean management and 3 of 5 cultural. Elm is lopsided, with 6 of 8 and 4 of 5 in continuous delivery and culture but 1 of 4 and 1 of 5 in product and process and lean management. The same total gives two different pictures, which is why Appendix A sorts the capabilities and does not rank them.

Exercise

  • Willow, an invented team, has these ten in place: version-control, deployment-automation, continuous-integration, test-automation, loosely-coupled-architecture, empowered-teams, customer-feedback, small-batches, generative-culture, meaningful-work.

Count Willow's capabilities in each of the five categories, against the category's size. Find the category with none in place and the one with every capability in place. Then type the team into the lab as a record starting willow to check.

That is the last lesson of the read-along. The appendices point back to the chapters, and the chapters to the surveys; from here the book's practice is your own team's, as its authors say, to be developed and measured on its own log.

How the lab counts. A team lists the capabilities it has in place; the rest are not. Categories and capabilities go in the order given. With a maturity model, a team reaches the highest level whose required capabilities, and every lower level's, are all in place (none when the first level's are not); the capabilities it has that a higher level requires are listed as what the level hides. A capability belongs to at most one level; the level tile names it and its place (level 2 of 3). With a second date, each team named there is compared with itself: added, then dropped. The fewest is the capability in place in the fewest teams; ties go in the order given, the first 3 named and the rest counted (the table lists all). Names are one word each: write a multiword capability with hyphens (trunk-based-development).

Method

Profiling an organization

Five steps, the last of them on teams of your own.

1

Fix the frame

Take Appendix A's 24 capabilities in their five categories, of sizes 8, 2, 4, 5 and 5.

2

List each team's capabilities

For each team write the capabilities it has in place, by its own account, as a short list.

3

Count by category

For each category add the teams' capabilities in place, and divide by the category's size times the number of teams.

4

Find the thin spots

Note the categories with the smallest shares and the capabilities the fewest teams have. They are places to ask why.

5

Do it on teams of your own

List three teams you know and type them in. Write one sentence on a capability none has, and what routine might bring it.

Six teams, by category

Work two of the categories of the preset six-teams by hand: architecture has 2 capabilities and lean management and monitoring has 5, across 6 teams.

architecture in place, by team: 2, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1 = 5 architecture possible: 2 · 6 = 12; share 5 / 12 = 41.7 percent lean management in place, by team: 3, 2, 2, 1, 1, 2 = 11 lean management possible: 5 · 6 = 30; share 11 / 30 = 36.7 percent

Dividing by the number possible keeps a category of two capabilities from looking small beside one of five. The lowest share says where to look first, not where the organization has gone wrong.

Common mistakes

1
Reading every line of Appendix B as a causeThe appendix defines its own verbs. A correlation is two variables moving together, and may be due to a third variable or to chance; a prediction is an inference from a theory tested on survey data; neither is a randomized test of cause. Read the verb on each line.
2
Reading a capability total as a maturity scoreTwo teams can have 13 of 24 and very different profiles. Appendix A groups capabilities by kind and presents them in no particular order; it does not rank them or give a level. A total can hide the category where a team is thin.
3
Carrying a figure out of its year and sampleThe 50 percent higher market capitalization growth comes from a follow-up to the 2014 data, among just over a thousand respondents in 355 companies, publicly traded ones among them, and the appendix says it could not be repeated. A figure belongs to the sample and the year that produced it.
