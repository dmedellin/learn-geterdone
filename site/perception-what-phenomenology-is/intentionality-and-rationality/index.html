Intentionality and Rationality

The Preface ends with intentionality, which it widens from the acts of judgement to an operative intentionality already at work in desire and in the landscape, and with a rationality that is the agreement of perspectives, guaranteed by no standpoint above them. The Preface's last themes in six lines

Core ideas

What this lesson establishes

The last pages of the Preface move fast, from intentionality through history to rationality. Three ideas hold them together.

1

Intentionality

Consciousness is always consciousness of something. The Preface says this is too often taken for phenomenology's great discovery, and that it can only be understood through the reduction.

2

Operative intentionality

Husserl's fungierende Intentionalität: the directedness that makes the world and our life one before any judgement, at work in desires, evaluations and the landscape we see. Its counterpart is the intentionality of acts, which judges and takes positions.

3

Rationality

On the Preface's account, the fact that perspectives blend, perceptions confirm one another and a meaning emerges. It is not set in a realm apart, and nothing outside the perspectives guarantees it.

The material

The passage first, then the Preface's last pages line by line

The passage is the Preface's account of rationality, near its end. The lesson reads it, goes back to the pages on intentionality that prepare it, and follows the Preface to its last sentence.

Preface, p. xxii. “To say that there exists rationality is to say that perspectives blend, perceptions confirm each other, a meaning emerges. … The phenomenological world is not pure being, but the sense which is revealed where the paths of my various experiences intersect, and also where my own and other people's intersect and engage each other like gears.” The two sentences are from page xxii of Smith's translation. Read from the section on intentionality, which begins on page xix, to the end of the Preface on page xxiv, in your own copy before going on.

Picture a cereal box on the table between you and someone sitting opposite. You see the front, with its picture; they see the back, with the list of ingredients; both of you see the top. Neither of you has seen the box whole, and neither of you doubts that it is one box. If they read out the ingredients, you do not check the claim against a view of the box from nowhere. There is no such view to check it against. What you have is your side, theirs, and the top you share, fitting together. That, in a kitchen, is what the passage calls rationality.

The Preface reaches it by way of intentionality, and it begins by deflating the idea. Intentionality is too often cited as phenomenology's main discovery, it says, when it can only be understood through the reduction. “All consciousness is consciousness of something”: there is nothing new in that. The idea came to Husserl from his teacher Franz Brentano, who in 1874 had made directedness at an object the mark of the mental, and the Preface points further back, to Kant. In the Refutation of Idealism Kant showed that inner perception is impossible without outer perception, so that the world is already anticipated in the consciousness of my own unity.

What is new, the Preface says, is that the world's unity is lived as ready-made, already there, before knowledge posits it, sets it down as an object, in a specific act. It finds the first sign of this in Kant himself, in the Critique of Judgement: in finding something beautiful I feel a harmony between sensation and concept, and between myself and others, that is itself without a concept. And from there Husserl draws the distinction the whole book will use. There is the intentionality of acts, of our judgements and the positions we deliberately take, the only kind the Critique of Pure Reason discusses. And there is “operative intentionality”, which makes the world and our life one before any judgement, and shows itself in “our desires, our evaluations and in the landscape we see”, more clearly than in knowledge, “furnishing the text which our knowledge tries to translate into precise language”.

Definition · Operative intentionality

The directedness at a world that is already at work before any act of judgement, giving the world and our life their unity; knowledge translates what it furnishes, and does not produce it.

The pages that follow widen the point to history, and here the Preface makes a joke, borrowed and then improved. It is true, as Marx says, that history does not walk on its head, and also true that “it does not think with its feet”; it is neither its head nor its feet we should worry about, the Preface says, but its body. Economic and psychological explanations of a doctrine are all true, since a thinker thinks only from what he is, and every view of an event or a doctrine is true provided it is not isolated: followed deep enough, each reaches one core of existential meaning. That is the passage's idea before it is stated: many views, one sense, found where they cross.

Now the passage. Rationality is “precisely proportioned”, the sentence before it says, to the experiences in which it is disclosed: there is exactly as much of it as there is agreement among them. “Perspectives blend, perceptions confirm each other, a meaning emerges”: three verbs, none of them a verdict from outside. Then the refusal, in the sentence the passage leaves out. Rationality is not to be set in a realm apart, in absolute Spirit, as Hegel would have it, or in a world in the realist sense, a world in itself behind every view. The sense is “where the paths of my various experiences intersect”, and where mine and other people's do, meshing “like gears”.

That refusal is the Preface's target throughout, and it deserves its strongest statement. If every view is only a view, the idealist argues, nothing among the views can tell a true one from a false one; something that is not a view, a consciousness that holds them all together, must be the condition of their agreeing. For Kant every representation must be able to be accompanied by one I think, and earlier pages of the Preface describe the idealism in which we are all one light and Peter's and Paul's perspectives blend because they were one perspective all along. The Preface's answer is that the philosopher, the meditating ego, the “impartial spectator”, does not rediscover a rationality already given. It establishes it, “by an act of initiative which has no guarantee in being”, and whose only justification, the Preface adds, is the power it gives us to take our own history upon ourselves.

The lab puts the passage's views on a cube. The three preset has three viewers. One looks from front, right and above, along the direction 1 2 2, whose length is 3; the lab's projection draws the front at 2/3 of its true area, the right side at 1/3 and the top at 2/3. One looks from back, left and above, along -2 1 -2, and the projection draws the back at 2/3, the left at 2/3 and the top at 1/3. One looks straight at the front and it is drawn whole. Between them they see 5 of the 6 faces, and the cover tile adds that the two oblique viewers already see all five between them: the third, looking straight at the front, adds no face, only a second sight of one. The top is seen by two viewers, projected differently, and is one top. Nobody sees the bottom, and nobody needs to, to see a cube.

The two preset has one viewer in front and one behind: 1 face each, 2 of 6 between them, and nothing seen in common. The conflict preset keeps the three viewers and has the first report seeing front, right and below. The lab's consistency tile prints no: front-right-above (below): a viewer looking down on the cube cannot see its underside. Notice how the lab knows. It checks each report against a cube it holds complete, from outside every view, which is exactly the standpoint the Preface denies us when it says, in the section on the reduction, that there is no thought which embraces all our thought. In perception the report would be caught the other way, by failing to mesh with the other views.

The Preface ends by refusing to call any of this unfinished in the bad sense. Rationality is not a problem with an unknown behind it; the world and reason may be called mysterious, but that mystery defines them and no solution dispels it. “True philosophy consists in relearning to look at the world”, and the unfinished character of phenomenology is “not to be taken as a sign of failure”. Its last comparison is with novelists, a poet and a painter: phenomenology, it says, “is as painstaking as the works of Balzac, Proust, Valéry or Cézanne”, by the same attentiveness and wonder, and the same will to seize the meaning of the world as it comes into being.

The Preface has said what the method is. The book now turns to what stands in its way: Classical Prejudices reads the Introduction, where Merleau-Ponty takes apart the two theories of perception he calls empiricism and intellectualism, beginning with the idea of a pure sensation.

Method

Computing what several views see

Five steps, for any set of whole-number view directions.

1

Find each direction's length

Square the three components, add them and take the root. The kit accepts only directions whose length is a whole number, such as 1 2 2, of length 3.

2

List the faces each view sees

A view sees the right face if its first component is positive and the left if negative, the top or bottom by the second, and the front or back by the third.

3

Foreshorten

In the projection a face seen at an angle is drawn at its component over the direction's length, as a fraction of its true area: from 1 2 2 the right face is drawn at 1/3.

4

Take the union

Collect every face some view sees, and name the faces no view sees.

5

Check each report

Compare what each viewer says they see with the faces their direction allows; a face the direction cannot see makes the report inconsistent.

Common mistakes

1
Thinking rationality needs a viewpoint above all viewpoints to check themAt its strongest this is the idealist's argument: views alone cannot sort true from false, so a consciousness outside them must be the condition of their agreeing. The Preface denies there is such a standpoint to occupy; the meditating ego and the impartial spectator establish rationality rather than find it. The lab shows the difference against itself. In the three preset the views confirm one another, the top projected at 2/3 and at 1/3 and still one top. The conflict preset's verdict, no: front-right-above (below), is reached by checking against a complete cube the lab holds from nowhere, which is the one thing a perceiver never has.
2
Taking operative intentionality for a quiet kind of judgementOperative intentionality is not a judgement made quickly or without noticing. It comes before judgement and furnishes the text that judgement translates: it is in desire, evaluation and the landscape seen, and the Preface says it shows there more clearly than in objective knowledge.
3
Reading the unfinished as a failureThe Preface expects phenomenology to stay unfinished, because it is itself in history and must question itself as it questions everything else. It says that this is not a sign of failure but was inevitable, because its task was to reveal the mystery of the world and of reason, and it compares the work to the novelist's or the painter's: to seize the world's meaning as it comes into being.
